
# Chapter 1 · Oral evidence (Arts. 70, 71)

## 1.1 The rule

1. **Art. 70** — **all facts, except the contents of documents**, may be proved by oral evidence. (Contents of documents are proved by the documents themselves — Arts. 72–76.)
2. **Art. 71 — oral evidence must be direct**:
   1. a fact which could be **seen** — by a witness who says **he saw** it;
   2. a fact which could be **heard** — by a witness who says **he heard** it;
   3. a fact perceived by **any other sense** — by a witness who says he **perceived** it by that sense;
   4. an **opinion** or its grounds — by the person who **holds** that opinion on those grounds.

## 1.2 The hearsay rule (LL.B. 2021)

**Hearsay** is a statement made **out of court** by a person not called as a witness, repeated in court by another to prove the **truth** of what it asserts ("Z told me that A stabbed B"). It is **excluded** by Art. 71 because the original speaker was **not on oath**, **cannot be cross-examined**, and his demeanour cannot be observed; and repetition invites **distortion**.

**What is not hearsay:** evidence that a statement **was made** (not of its truth) — e.g., a threat, a defamatory statement, words of a contract — is **original** evidence.

**Exceptions** (where hearsay is admitted):

1. **Res gestae** statements (Art. 19) and statements accompanying **conduct** (Art. 21);
2. **Admissions and confessions** (Arts. 30–43);
3. **Statements of persons who cannot be called** — dying declarations and the rest of **Art. 46**; **electronic records** (Art. 46A);
4. **Former evidence** (Art. 47);
5. **Public records**, books of account, maps, law books (Arts. 48–52);
6. **Expert opinions** in **treatises** offered for sale, if the author is dead or unavailable (Art. 71, first proviso);
7. ***Shahada ala al-shahadah*** (Art. 71, third proviso).

## 1.3 Provisos to Article 71

1. **Treatises** — opinions of experts in **treatises commonly offered for sale**, and their grounds, may be proved by producing the treatise if the author is **dead, cannot be found, is incapable, or cannot be called** without unreasonable delay or expense.
2. **Material things** — if oral evidence refers to the existence or condition of a **material thing** (other than a document), the court **may require its production** for inspection (e.g., the weapon, the damaged goods).
3. ***Shahada ala al-shahadah*** (testimony upon testimony) — if a witness is **dead, cannot be found, is incapable**, or his attendance cannot be procured without unreasonable delay or expense, a party has the **right** to produce evidence by which **a witness can appoint two witnesses to depose on his behalf** — **except in Hudood** cases. This is an **Islamic** form of permitted secondary oral evidence.

## 1.4 Weighing oral evidence — key concepts (SHC 2022)

1. **Interested witness** — one who has a motive to see the accused convicted (enmity, desire for revenge); not necessarily a **related** witness. A related witness is a **natural** witness when the occurrence happened at home; relationship alone does not make him unreliable, but his evidence is **scrutinised**.
2. **Chance witness** — one who claims to have been present at the scene **by chance** (not resident, no reason to be there); his evidence is regarded with **caution** unless he gives a **plausible reason** for his presence that is borne out by other evidence.
3. **Falsus in uno, falsus in omnibus** ("false in one, false in all") — formerly not applied in the subcontinent (the court would "sift the grain from the chaff"); the Supreme Court has now held that a witness found to have **deliberately lied** on a **material** point must be **discarded as a whole**, and false witnesses may be proceeded against (*Notice to Police Constable Khizar Hayat*, PLD 2019 SC 527; verify).
4. **Sole testimony** — evidence is **weighed, not counted**; one reliable witness may suffice (Art. 17(2)(b)) except where the law requires a number.
5. **Improvement** — a witness who **improves** his statement at the trial over his earlier statement (FIR, s. 161) to strengthen the case loses credibility on that point.

# Chapter 2 · Documentary evidence (Arts. 72 to 89)

## 2.1 Primary evidence — Article 73 (SHC 2022)

"**Primary evidence** means the **document itself** produced for the inspection of the court."

1. **Expl. 1** — a document executed in **several parts** — each part is primary; in **counterpart** — each counterpart is primary against the party executing it.
2. **Expl. 2** — documents made by **one uniform process** (printing, lithography, photography) — each is primary evidence of the contents of the **rest**, but not of a common **original** (*illus.*: placards printed at one time).
3. **Expl. 3 (2002)** — a **printout** or other output of an **automated information system** is **not denied** the status of primary evidence solely because it was in electronic form, if the system was **in working order** at all material times (**presumed**, absent contrary evidence).
4. **Expl. 4 (2002)** — a printout or reproduction of an **electronic document** first generated or stored electronically is **primary** evidence where a **security procedure** was applied to it at the time.

## 2.2 Secondary evidence — Article 74

"**Secondary evidence**" means and includes —

1. **certified copies** under the Order;
2. copies made from the original by **mechanical processes** which ensure accuracy (photocopies, photographs), and copies **compared** with such copies;
3. copies **made from or compared with** the original;
4. **counterparts** of documents as against the parties who did **not** execute them;
5. **oral accounts** of the contents of a document by a person who has **himself seen it**.

*Illustrations:* a photograph of an original is secondary evidence if it is proved that the thing photographed was the original; a copy of a copy, **not compared** with the original, is **not** secondary evidence; an **oral account of a copy** is not secondary evidence.

## 2.3 When secondary evidence may be given — Articles 75 to 77

**Rule (Art. 75):** documents **must be proved by primary evidence** except as provided. **Exceptions (Art. 76):**

{w:8,62,30}
|(a)|The original is in the **possession or power of the adverse party**, or of a person **out of reach** of the court's process, or of a person **legally bound** to produce it, and he does **not produce it after notice** (Art. 77)|Any secondary evidence|
|(b)|The existence, condition or contents have been **admitted in writing** by the adverse party|The **written admission**|
|(c)|The original has been **destroyed or lost**, or cannot be produced for any reason **not arising from the party's own default**|Any secondary evidence|
|(d)|Copies have been made by **microfilming or other modern devices** because of the volume or bulk of the original|Any secondary evidence|
|(e)|The original is **not easily movable** (an inscription on a wall)|Any secondary evidence|
|(f)|The original is a **public document** (Art. 85)|A **certified copy** only|
|(g)|A **certified copy** is permitted by law to be given in evidence|A **certified copy** only|
|(h)|The originals are **numerous accounts** which cannot conveniently be examined, and the fact to be proved is the **general result**|Evidence of the result by a **skilled person** who examined them|
|(i)|An original forming part of a **judicial record** is not available and only a certified copy exists|A **certified copy of that certified copy**|

**Notice to produce (Art. 77)** — required before secondary evidence under (a), except where the document is itself a **notice**, the adverse party **must know** he will be required to produce it, he obtained it by **fraud or force**, he **has it in court**, he has **admitted its loss**, or the holder is **out of reach** of the court.

**Practice:** a party must first lay the **foundation** — prove loss, destruction, or non-production after notice — and **apply** for permission to lead secondary evidence; a **photocopy** is not admissible merely because it is produced; it must be **proved** to be a copy of the original. Under **O. VII r. 14 / O. XIII CPC**, documents must also be produced at the proper stage.

## 2.4 Proof of signature, handwriting and execution — Articles 78 to 84

1. **Art. 78** — if a document is alleged to be **signed or written** by a person, the signature or handwriting **must be proved** to be his (Topic 1, Chapter 8.3 — the six modes).
2. **Art. 78A (2002)** — if an **electronic document** is alleged to be signed or generated by a person through an information system and this is **denied**, the application of a **security procedure** to the signature or document must be proved.
3. **Art. 79 — attested documents:** a document **required by law to be attested** shall **not be used as evidence** until **two attesting witnesses at least** have been **called** to prove its execution, if **two are alive**, subject to the court's process and capable of giving evidence. **Proviso:** it is **not necessary** to call an attesting witness to prove a **registered** document (**not being a will**), unless its execution is **specifically denied**.
4. **Art. 80** — if no attesting witness can be found, it must be proved that they are **dead or cannot be found** and that the document was **executed** by the person.
5. **Art. 81** — the **admission** of a party of his own execution is sufficient proof **against him**.
6. **Art. 82** — if the attesting witness **denies or does not recollect** execution, it may be proved by **other evidence**.
7. **Art. 83** — a document **not required by law to be attested** may be proved as if **unattested**.
8. **Art. 84** — **comparison** of a disputed signature, writing or seal with one **admitted or proved**; the court may direct a person present to **write words or figures** for comparison (also finger impressions).

:::tip Article 17 and Article 79 together
Documents of **financial or future obligations** reduced to writing (agreements to sell, pronotes, receipts) **must be attested by two men or one man and two women** (Art. 17(2)(a)), so they are "required by law to be attested", and **Art. 79** then requires **two attesting witnesses** to be called. A plaintiff suing on such an agreement must produce **both** marginal witnesses (or prove under Art. 80 why he cannot); the scribe or a single witness is not enough. Wills (Succession law) and gifts by registered deed raise their own rules.
:::

## 2.5 Public and private documents — Articles 85, 86

**Public documents (Art. 85):** (1) documents forming the **acts or records of acts** of the **sovereign authority**, of **official bodies and tribunals**, and of **public officers** (legislative, judicial, executive) of Pakistan or a foreign country; (2) **public records kept in Pakistan of private documents** (e.g., the register copy at the Sub-Registrar's office); (3) documents forming part of the **records of judicial proceedings**; (4) documents **required to be maintained** by a public servant under any law; (5) **registered documents** whose **execution is not disputed**; (6) **certificates** deposited in a repository under the **Electronic Transactions Ordinance 2002**. **All others are private** (Art. 86).

## 2.6 Certified copies — Articles 87 to 89

1. **Art. 87** — every public officer having custody of a public document which a person has a **right to inspect** shall give him on demand a **copy** on payment of fees, with a **certificate** at the foot that it is a **true copy**, dated, subscribed with name and official title, and **sealed** where authorised.
2. **Art. 88** — such certified copies may be produced in **proof of the contents** of the public document.
3. **Art. 89** — other public documents: Government acts and notifications (by departmental records certified by the head, or Government printing); legislative proceedings (journals, published Acts); foreign executive and legislative acts; municipal proceedings; foreign public documents (original or certified copy with a **notary's or Pakistan Consul's** certificate).

## 3.1 The power-of-attorney presumption — Article 95 (LHC 2023)

> "95. The Court **shall presume** that every document purporting to be a **power-of-attorney**, and to have been **executed before**, and **authenticated by**, a **notary public**, or any **Court, Judge, Magistrate, Pakistan Consul or Vice-Consul**, or **representative of the Federal Government**, was so executed and authenticated."

### Requirements for the presumption

1. The document must **purport to be a power of attorney** — an instrument by which the principal **authorises** the attorney to act for him.
2. It must **purport to have been executed before** one of the named functionaries — the principal must have **appeared** and signed **in his presence** (not merely signed elsewhere and sent).
3. It must **purport to be authenticated** by that functionary — his **certificate or endorsement** that the principal, **identified** to him, executed it, with his **signature, seal** and **date**.
4. The functionary must be one of those **named** — a **notary public** (under the Notaries Ordinance 1961), a **court, Judge or Magistrate**, a **Pakistan Consul or Vice-Consul** (for documents executed **abroad**), or a **representative of the Federal Government**.
5. It must be **duly stamped** (Stamp Act 1899, Schedule I, Art. 48) — an unstamped instrument is inadmissible until the duty and penalty are paid (Stamp Act ss. 35–36).
6. **For immovable property**, since **2022** a power of attorney concerning immovable property of Rs. 100 or more must be **registered** (Registration Act s. 17(1)(ba)); and for **presentation** of documents for registration, only powers authenticated under **s. 33** of the Registration Act are recognised (Civil Law-II, Topic 4).

### Scope and limits

1. The presumption covers **execution and authentication only** — not the **genuineness of the principal's consent** against a plea of fraud, nor the **scope of authority**, nor that the attorney **acted within** it. These must be proved by evidence.
2. It is a **rebuttable** presumption ("shall presume … unless disproved" — Art. 2(8)); the principal may prove forgery or that he never appeared.
3. Powers of attorney are **construed strictly**: an attorney may do only what is **expressly** authorised; a general power to "manage" does not authorise a **sale** or a **gift**, and the attorney cannot transfer the principal's property **to himself or his relatives** without the principal's **specific consent** — such transactions are viewed with suspicion and the attorney must prove good faith (Art. 127 — active confidence).
4. A power of attorney **not** executed before such a functionary must be **proved** like any other document — execution and signature (Art. 78), and attestation if required.

# Chapter 4 · Exclusion of oral by documentary evidence (Arts. 102 to 110)

## 4.1 Article 102 — the best-evidence rule for written transactions

When the terms of a **contract, grant or other disposition of property**, or any matter **required by law to be in writing**, have been reduced to the form of a document, **no evidence** shall be given in proof of its terms **except the document itself**, or **secondary evidence** where admissible. *Exceptions:* the appointment of a public officer may be proved orally when it is shown that a person acted as such; **wills** admitted to probate may be proved by the probate.

## 4.2 Article 103 — no oral evidence to vary the terms

When the terms of a document have been proved under Art. 102, **no evidence of any oral agreement or statement** shall be admitted, **as between the parties** or their representatives, to **contradict, vary, add to or subtract from** its terms. **Six provisos** — the following may be proved:

1. **Invalidating facts** — **fraud, intimidation, illegality, want of due execution, want of capacity, want or failure of consideration, mistake** in fact or law;
2. a **separate oral agreement** on a matter on which the document is **silent** and not inconsistent with it (regard being had to the **formality** of the document);
3. a separate oral agreement constituting a **condition precedent** to any obligation under it;
4. a **distinct subsequent oral agreement** to **rescind or modify** it — **except** where the contract is by law required to be **in writing** or has been **registered**;
5. a **usage or custom** annexing incidents to contracts of that kind, if not repugnant to the express terms;
6. any fact showing how the **language** of the document is **related to existing facts**.

*Illustration (b):* A agrees in writing to pay B Rs. 1,000 on 1 March 1984; an oral agreement made at the same time that the money should not be paid till 31 March **cannot** be proved.

**Who may give such evidence — Art. 109:** **strangers** (persons who are not parties or their representatives) may give evidence of any fact tending to vary the terms — the rule binds only the **parties**.

## 4.3 Applying the document to facts — Articles 104 to 108

1. **Art. 104** — language **plain in itself** which **applies accurately** to existing facts — **no evidence** may be given to show it was **not meant** to apply to them (*illus.*: "my estate at Rangpur containing 100 bighas" — no evidence that another estate was meant).
2. **Art. 105** — language plain in itself but **unmeaning in reference to existing facts** — evidence may show it was used in a **peculiar sense** (*illus.*: "my house in Karachi" where A had none in Karachi but one at Keamari, possessed by B since the deed).
3. **Art. 106** — language that could apply to **any one, but only one, of several** persons or things — evidence of facts showing **which** was intended (*illus.*: "my white horse" where A has two; "Hyderabad" — Deccan or Sind).
4. **Art. 107** — language applying **partly to one set** of facts and **partly to another**, but wholly to neither — evidence to show which was meant ("my land at X in the occupation of Y").
5. **Art. 108** — evidence to show the meaning of **illegible** or not commonly intelligible characters, **foreign, obsolete, technical, local** expressions, **abbreviations** and words used in a peculiar sense.
6. **Art. 110** — the **Succession Act** rules on the construction of **wills** are saved.

**Note:** the Order has **no counterpart of s. 93 of the Evidence Act** (patent ambiguity — "language ambiguous or defective on its face"); its Arts. 104–108 correspond to ss. 94–98. Explanation 3 to Art. 102 also allows **oral evidence** of facts merely **mentioned** in a document that are not its terms (*illus.*: a receipt does not bar oral proof of payment).

# Chapter 5 · Facts which need not be proved (Arts. 111 to 113)

## 5.1 Judicial notice — Articles 111, 112 (LL.B. 2021; LHC 2023)

**Meaning and rationale.** Some facts are so **notorious** or so **official** that proving them would waste time; the court **notices** them itself. "**No fact** of which the court will take **judicial notice** need be proved" (Art. 111).

**Mandatory judicial notice — Art. 112(1)** — the court **shall** take judicial notice of:

1. **All-Pakistan laws**;
2. **Articles of War** for the armed forces;
3. the **course of proceedings of Parliament** and of the provincial legislatures;
4. the **seals** of all courts in Pakistan, of Admiralty and Maritime courts, of **notaries public**, and all seals authorised by law;
5. the **accession to office, names, titles, functions and signatures** of persons filling public offices, if notified in the **Gazette**;
6. the **existence, title and national flag** of every State recognised by the Federal Government;
7. the **divisions of time**, the geographical divisions of the world, and **public festivals, fasts and holidays** notified in the Gazette;
8. the **territories** of Pakistan;
9. the commencement, continuance and termination of **hostilities** between Pakistan and any other State;
10. the names of the **members and officers** of the court and of **advocates** and others authorised to appear;
11. the **rule of the road** on land or at sea.

**Aids — Art. 112(2), (3):** on these matters and on **public history, literature, science or art**, the court may consult **books or documents of reference**, and may refuse to take notice until the party produces them.

**Beyond the list** — courts also notice **notorious facts** of common knowledge (e.g., that the sun rises in the east; general economic conditions; that Islamabad is the federal capital), but **local or special laws** and **customs** must ordinarily be **proved**.

## 5.2 Facts admitted — Article 113

No fact need be proved which the parties or their agents **agree to admit at the hearing**, or **before the hearing** agree to admit **by writing**, or which by any **rule of pleading** in force (as amended in **2025**) they are **deemed to have admitted** in their pleadings (e.g., **O. VIII r. 5 CPC** — facts not specifically denied). **Proviso:** the court may, in its discretion, **require the facts admitted to be proved** otherwise. **Judicial admissions** of this kind are **conclusive** for the case (unlike Art. 45 admissions).

# Chapter 6 · Estoppel (Arts. 114 to 116)

## 6.1 General estoppel — Article 114

> "When one person has, by his **declaration, act or omission**, **intentionally caused or permitted** another person to **believe a thing to be true** and to **act upon such belief**, neither he nor his representative shall be allowed, in any suit or proceeding between himself and such person or his representative, to **deny the truth of that thing**."

**Ingredients:** (1) a **representation** of an existing fact (by words, conduct or silence where there is a duty to speak); (2) made **intentionally** or permitted; (3) the other party **believed** it; (4) **acted** on it, to his detriment; (5) the proceeding is **between the same persons** or their representatives. *Illus.:* A falsely leads B to believe land is his and B buys it; A later acquires title — A cannot set aside the sale.

**Estoppel is a rule of evidence**, not a cause of action; there is **no estoppel against a statute** or against the **law**; and no estoppel where **both parties knew** the truth.

## 6.2 Kinds of estoppel

1. **By record** — a judgment binding parties (res judicata; Arts. 54–55).
2. **By deed** — a party to a deed cannot deny its recitals as against the other party.
3. **In pais / by conduct** — Art. 114.
4. **Promissory estoppel** — a promise intended to be acted upon and acted upon binds the promisor, even the State, unless overriding public interest (equitable doctrine).
5. **Estoppel of tenant (Art. 115)** — no **tenant**, during the continuance of the tenancy, may deny that his **landlord had title** at the **beginning** of the tenancy; no **licensee** may deny that the person who gave the licence had **title to possession** when it was given.
6. **Estoppel of acceptor, bailee, licensee (Art. 116)** — the **acceptor of a bill of exchange** cannot deny the drawer's authority to draw it or endorse it; a **bailee or licensee** cannot deny that his bailor or licensor had authority at the beginning of the bailment or licence.

# Chapter 7 · Burden of proof and presumptions (Arts. 117 to 129)

![Burden of proof — the scheme](burden_map.png)

## 7.1 The general rules

1. **Art. 117** — whoever desires a court to give judgment as to any legal right or liability dependent on facts **which he asserts** must **prove** that those facts exist; then the **burden of proof lies on that person**.
2. **Art. 118** — the burden of proof in a suit or proceeding lies on the person **who would fail if no evidence at all were given** on either side (*illus. (a)*: A sues B, in possession, for land left by B's father to A — burden on A; *(b)*: execution of a bond admitted, B pleads **fraud** — burden on B).
3. **Art. 119** — the burden as to a **particular fact** lies on the person who wishes the court to **believe in its existence** (*illus.*: the accused's **alibi** — on him).
4. **Art. 120** — the burden of proving a fact necessary to make **evidence admissible** is on the person who wishes to give it (proving **death** before a dying declaration; proving **loss** before secondary evidence).

**Burden of proof and onus** — the **legal burden** (Art. 117) is fixed and never shifts; the **evidential burden** (the onus of producing evidence) **shifts** during trial as each side's evidence tilts the balance. In a civil case **issues** are framed with the onus on the party asserting (e.g., "Whether the plaintiff is entitled to specific performance? OPP" — "onus on the plaintiff").

## 7.2 Special rules

## 7.3 Legitimacy — Article 128

1. **Conclusive proof** — a child born **during a valid marriage** between his mother and any man, **not earlier than six lunar months** from the date of marriage, **or within two years after its dissolution** (the mother remaining **unmarried**), is the **legitimate** child of that man — **unless** (a) the husband **refused or refuses to own** the child (lian), or (b) the child was born **after six lunar months** from the date on which the woman had **accepted that her iddat had ended**.
2. **Non-Muslims** — the Article does not apply if inconsistent with their faith.
3. **Compare the Evidence Act s. 112** (any time during marriage or within 280 days after dissolution, unless non-access) — the QSO follows **Muslim law**.

## 7.4 Presumptions of fact — Article 129

"The court **may presume** the existence of any fact which it thinks **likely to have happened**, regard being had to the **common course of natural events, human conduct and public and private business**." **Illustrations:** (a) a person in **possession of stolen goods soon after the theft** is the thief or a knowing receiver, unless he accounts for it; (b) an **accomplice is unworthy of credit** unless corroborated; (c) a **bill of exchange** was accepted for good consideration; (d) a thing shown to exist continues to exist for its usual period; (e) **judicial and official acts** have been **regularly performed**; (f) the **common course of business** was followed; (g) **evidence which could be and is not produced** would, if produced, be **unfavourable** to the person withholding it; (h) a man who refuses to answer a question he is not compelled to answer — the answer would be unfavourable; (i) a document creating an obligation in the **obligor's** hands — the obligation has been **discharged**. The court must also consider the **qualifying facts** set out (e.g., a shopkeeper with a marked coin in his till; a river course changed by floods).

**Presumption (g) — withholding evidence** — is frequently applied: where a party does not produce the **best evidence** in its power (a material witness, an original document, the CCTV footage), the court may draw an **adverse inference**.

# Chapter 8 · Examination of witnesses (Arts. 130 to 161)

![Examination of a witness](examination_flow.png)

## 8.1 Order and admissibility — Articles 130, 131

1. **Art. 130** — the **order** of production and examination of witnesses is regulated by the law of procedure (CPC O. XVIII; CrPC), and in its absence by the **court's discretion**.
2. **Art. 131** — the **judge decides admissibility**: he may ask how the fact would be **relevant** and admits it only if relevant; a fact admissible only on proof of another must wait until that is proved (unless the party undertakes to prove it later); he decides the **order** of proof of interdependent facts.

## 8.2 The three stages — Articles 132, 133 (LL.B. 2021)

{w:22,78}
|**Examination-in-chief**|By the party **calling** the witness — to elicit the facts supporting his case; **no leading questions** on disputed matters (Art. 137)|
|**Cross-examination**|By the **adverse party** — to test **truth, accuracy and credit**, to elicit facts favourable to the cross-examiner; **need not be confined** to the matters in chief (Art. 133(2)); **leading questions allowed** (Art. 138); a party who does not cross-examine on a material point is taken to **accept** it|
|**Re-examination**|By the party calling — **directed to explaining** matters raised in cross-examination; **new matter** only with the court's permission, and then the adverse party may **further cross-examine** on it (Art. 133(3))|

**Others:** a person summoned only to **produce a document** does not become a witness and cannot be cross-examined unless called as a witness (Art. 134); **witnesses to character** may be cross-examined and re-examined (Art. 135).

## 8.3 Leading questions — Articles 136 to 138

1. **Definition (Art. 136):** a question **suggesting the answer** which the person putting it wishes or expects to receive ("Did you see A stab B with a knife?").
2. **Not in chief or re-examination**, if objected to, **except with the court's permission** (Art. 137(1)); the court **shall permit** them on matters **introductory, undisputed**, or already sufficiently proved (Art. 137(2)).
3. **May be asked in cross-examination** (Art. 138).

## 8.4 Questions in cross-examination — Articles 139 to 149

1. **Art. 139** — a witness may be asked whether a contract or grant was in **writing**; the adverse party may object to oral evidence of its contents until the document is produced.
2. **Art. 140 — previous statements in writing:** a witness may be cross-examined about his **previous written statements** without showing them to him; but **to contradict** him, his attention must first be **called to those parts** to be used (the method for using **s. 161 CrPC** statements and the FIR).
3. **Art. 141** — questions lawful in cross-examination to (1) **test veracity**, (2) discover **who he is and his position in life**, (3) **shake his credit** by injuring his character — even if the answer may criminate him.
4. **Art. 142** — if relevant to the suit, Art. 15 applies (must answer, with protection).
5. **Art. 143** — if relevant **only to credit**, the **court decides** whether to compel an answer, considering whether the imputation is serious, its remoteness, and disproportion; the court may draw an inference from refusal.
6. **Art. 144** — no such question without **reasonable grounds**; **Art. 145** — the court may report an advocate who asks such a question without grounds.
7. **Art. 146** — the court may **forbid indecent or scandalous** questions unless they relate to facts in issue.
8. **Art. 147** — procedure in **defamation** cases (questions about the defamatory matter).
9. **Art. 148** — the court **shall forbid** questions intended to **insult or annoy** or needlessly offensive.
10. **Art. 149** — answers to questions testing **veracity** on collateral matters are **final** — no contradicting evidence, except (i) a previous conviction, and (ii) facts showing **bias** or impartiality.

## 8.5 The hostile witness — Article 150 (LHC 2020)

1. **Meaning:** the QSO does not use the word "hostile". A witness is commonly called **hostile** (adverse or unfavourable) when, being called by a party, he **resiles** from his earlier statement or shows **animus** against that party and tries to damage its case.
2. **Provision — Art. 150:** "The Court may, **in its discretion**, permit the person who calls a witness to put any questions to him which **might be put in cross-examination** by the adverse party." The court should exercise the discretion when it appears from the witness's **demeanour, attitude, or contradiction of earlier statements** that he is not speaking the truth for the party calling him.
3. **What happens when declared hostile:**
   1. the party calling may **cross-examine** him, with **leading questions**, and confront him with his **previous statements** (Art. 140);
   2. the party may **impeach his credit** with the court's consent (Art. 151);
   3. his **evidence is not wiped off the record** — the court may **rely on such part** of his testimony as is **credible and corroborated**, whether it favours the prosecution or the defence;
   4. the adverse party may still **cross-examine** him afterwards;
   5. a hostile prosecution witness may be proceeded against for **false evidence** if he lies.

## 8.6 Impeaching the credit of a witness — Article 151

The credit of a witness may be impeached **by the adverse party**, or, **with the consent of the court**, **by the party who calls him**:

1. by the evidence of persons who testify that, from their **knowledge** of the witness, they believe him to be **unworthy of credit** (they may not give reasons in chief, but may be asked in cross-examination — Explanation);
2. by proof that the witness has been **bribed**, or has accepted the offer of a bribe, or any other **corrupt inducement**;
3. by proof of **former statements inconsistent** with any part of his evidence which is liable to be contradicted;
4. **(former clause (4) — that a prosecutrix in a rape case was of generally immoral character — omitted in 2016).**

**Modes of impeaching one's own witness** (LHC 2020): (i) **cross-examination** with the court's permission (Art. 150); (ii) the three modes of **Art. 151** with the court's consent; (iii) **contradiction** by previous written statements (Art. 140); and (iv) calling **other witnesses** to prove the facts differently.

## 8.7 Corroboration — Articles 152 to 154

1. **Art. 152** — a witness who is to be corroborated may be questioned about **other circumstances** he observed at or near the time and place.
2. **Art. 153** — **former statements** of a witness relating to the same fact, made **at or about the time** the fact took place, or **before any authority legally competent to investigate**, may be proved to **corroborate** his later testimony (the FIR to corroborate its maker; a prompt complaint).
3. **Art. 154** — when a statement under Art. 46 or 47 is proved, anything may be proved to contradict, corroborate, impeach or confirm the credit of its maker.

## 8.8 Refreshing memory — Articles 155 to 157

1. **Art. 155** — a witness may **refresh his memory** by referring to any **writing made by himself** at the time of the transaction, or **so soon afterwards** that it was fresh in his memory; or to a writing **made by another** and **read by him** at that time, knowing it correct; with permission, to a **copy** if the original's non-production is explained; an **expert** may refer to **professional treatises**.
2. **Art. 156** — he may testify to facts in such a document though he has no specific recollection, if he is sure they were correctly recorded (e.g., a doctor's notes).
3. **Art. 157** — the **adverse party** may see the writing, **cross-examine** on it, and put it in evidence.

## 8.9 Production of documents — Articles 158 to 160

1. **Art. 158** — a witness summoned to produce a document **shall bring it** to court notwithstanding any objection; the **court decides** the validity of the objection (and may inspect it, except a document of State).
2. **Art. 159** — a party who calls for and inspects a document produced on notice is **bound to give it as evidence** if the producing party requires.
3. **Art. 160** — a party who **refuses to produce** a document on notice **cannot afterwards use it** as evidence without the other party's consent or the court's order.

## 8.10 The judge's power — Article 161

The judge may, to discover or obtain proper proof of relevant facts, **ask any question he pleases**, in any form, at any time, of any witness or party, about any fact **relevant or irrelevant**, and **order the production** of any document or thing; no party may object, or cross-examine on the answer without leave. **But:** the **judgment must be based on facts declared relevant and duly proved**; the judge may not compel answers privileged under Arts. 4–14, ask improper questions (Arts. 143–144), or **dispense with primary evidence** of a document except as allowed. (Compare CrPC s. 540 and CPC O. XVI r. 14.)

# Chapter 9 · Improper admission, oath and modern devices (Arts. 162 to 164)

## 9.1 Improper admission or rejection — Article 162

The improper admission or rejection of evidence is **not by itself** ground for a **new trial or reversal**, if, **independently** of the evidence objected to and admitted, there was **sufficient evidence** to justify the decision, or if the rejected evidence, had it been received, **ought not to have varied** the decision. Objections to the **mode of proof** must be taken **when the document is tendered**; if not taken then, they cannot be raised later (but objections to **admissibility** in law — e.g., an unregistered or unstamped document for a barred purpose — may be raised at any stage).

## 9.2 Decision on oath — Article 163

1. When the **plaintiff takes oath** in support of his claim, the court **shall**, on the plaintiff's application, call upon the **defendant to deny the claim on oath** (an Islamic mode of deciding civil claims — *al-bayyinatu ala al-mudda'i wal-yaminu ala man ankar*).
2. The court may pass orders as to **costs** and other matters.
3. Not applicable to **Hudood** or other **criminal** cases.
4. The parties may also agree to be bound by a **special oath** (Oaths Act 1873, ss. 8–11) — the oath is then **conclusive** as against the person who offered it.

## 9.3 Evidence through modern devices — Article 164 (LHC 2020)

> "164. Depending on the nature of case and circumstances, the Court may, if deem appropriate, allow to be produced any **evidence or witnesses recorded by the Court through the modern devices or techniques** including **video call, viber, skype, imo, whatsapp, facebook messenger, line caller and video conference**, etc." (as substituted in **2023**)

### How courts treat audio and video evidence

The Supreme Court has laid down that an **audio or video recording** is admissible only if its **genuineness and integrity** are proved: (1) the **person who recorded** it must produce it and be cross-examined; (2) the **original device or first copy** must be produced, with a reliable **chain of custody**; (3) a **forensic examination** should show it is **not edited, tampered or doctored**; (4) the **voices and persons** must be identified; (5) the **date, time and place** must be established; (6) it is ordinarily **corroborative**, not stand-alone, evidence (*Ishtiaq Ahmed Mirza v. Federation of Pakistan*, PLD 2019 SC 675; verify). **Advantages** of technology in proceedings: recording evidence of witnesses **abroad**, sick or vulnerable witnesses (rape victims, children), **saving time and expense**, security in terrorism cases, transparency; **risks**: tampering, identity of the witness, tutoring off-camera, connectivity — the court must ensure the witness is **alone**, properly **identified**, and the **oath** administered.

# Chapter 10 · Discussing admissibility — the judge's method

:::tip Five-step method for "discuss the admissibility of evidence"
1. **Identify** the item — oral or documentary; original or copy; electronic or paper; statement by whom, to whom, when.
2. **Relevancy** — which Article makes it relevant (Arts. 19–69)?
3. **Exclusionary rules** — hearsay (Art. 71); confession to police (Arts. 38–39); privilege (Arts. 4–14); oral evidence of a written contract (Arts. 102–103); character (Arts. 66–68); without-prejudice (Art. 36).
4. **Mode of proof** — primary or secondary (Arts. 73–77); signature and attestation (Arts. 78–84; Art. 17); certified copy (Arts. 85–89); presumption available (Arts. 90–101); **stamp** and **registration** (Stamp Act s. 35; Registration Act s. 49); electronic authenticity (Arts. 73, 78A, 164).
5. **Weight** — credibility, corroboration, interest, consistency with medical and circumstantial evidence; presumptions (Art. 129); state the conclusion — admitted / excluded / admitted for a limited purpose.
:::

:::eg Worked examples
1. **A photocopy of an agreement to sell** is tendered by the plaintiff. — Secondary evidence; inadmissible unless loss of the original or non-production after notice is proved (Arts. 76(a), (c), 77); and the agreement, being a document of a future obligation, must be attested by two men (Art. 17) and proved by two attesting witnesses (Art. 79).
2. **A recovery memo of a pistol pointed out by the accused in police custody**. — The accused's statement "I hid it under the bed" is admissible to the extent it **distinctly relates** to the discovery (Art. 40); the rest is barred by Arts. 38–39; the recovery witnesses must be credible and independent where possible.
3. **A WhatsApp video of the occurrence** recorded by a bystander. — Relevant (Arts. 19, 46A); admissible if the maker produces it, the original device or first copy is available, a forensic report rules out tampering, and the persons are identified (Art. 164; *Ishtiaq Ahmed Mirza*).
4. **The statement of the deceased recorded by the investigating officer under s. 161 CrPC.** — Admissible as a **dying declaration** under Art. 46(1) (CrPC s. 162(2)); weight depends on fitness, voluntariness and corroboration.
5. **An oral promise by the landlord, made when signing a written lease, not to increase rent for five years.** — Excluded by Art. 103 if it varies the written terms; admissible under proviso (2) only if the lease is silent and it is consistent; the 2022 registration rules may also bar reliance if the lease is unregistered (Registration Act s. 49).
:::
