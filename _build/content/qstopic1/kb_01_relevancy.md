
# Chapter 1 · Nature and scheme of the law of evidence

## 1.1 What the law of evidence does

Substantive law creates rights and liabilities; the **law of evidence** tells the court **which facts may be proved, how they are proved, who must prove them, and how much proof is enough**. It is **procedural** (adjective) law — but a rule of evidence can decide a case. It applies to **all judicial proceedings** in or before any court, including a **court martial** and any **tribunal** or authority exercising judicial or quasi-judicial powers, but **not to arbitrators** (Art. 1(2)), and it **overrides other laws** (Art. 165). It does not apply to **affidavits** presented to a court or officer (they are governed by the CPC and CrPC).

## 1.3 Three questions a judge asks about every piece of evidence

1. **Relevancy** — is the fact relevant under Arts. 18–69? (Only relevant facts and facts in issue may be proved — Art. 18.)
2. **Admissibility** — even if relevant, is it excluded by a rule (a confession to police — Art. 38; oral evidence of a written contract — Art. 102; a privileged communication — Arts. 4–14; hearsay — Art. 71)? Has it been **proved in the manner** the law requires (primary evidence — Art. 75; attesting witnesses — Art. 79)?
3. **Weight** — how far is it **believed**, judged by the standard of a **prudent man** (Art. 2(4)) — the court's assessment of credibility, consistency and corroboration.

:::maxim Relevancy is the genus, admissibility the species
Every admissible fact is relevant, but not every relevant fact is admissible. Relevancy is **logical** (defined by the Order); admissibility is **legal** (the rules that let the relevant fact in or keep it out). "Weight" is for the judge's judgment.
:::

# Chapter 2 · Definitions — Article 2 (LHC 2023)

## 2.1 Court, document, evidence, fact

1. **Court** — all **Judges and Magistrates** and all persons, **except arbitrators**, legally authorised to take evidence.
2. **Document** — any matter expressed or described **upon any substance** by letters, figures or marks, intended to be used, or which may be used, for **recording** that matter (a writing, a printed or photographed word, a **map or plan**, an **inscription on a metal plate or stone**, a **caricature**). Electronic documents are given meaning by the **Electronic Transactions Ordinance 2002** (Art. 2(1)(e)).
3. **Evidence** — includes (i) all **statements** which the court permits or requires to be made before it **by witnesses** in relation to matters of fact under inquiry — **oral evidence**; and (ii) all **documents produced for the inspection of the court** — **documentary evidence**.
4. **Fact** — (i) anything, state of things, or relation of things **capable of being perceived by the senses** (physical facts); and (ii) any **mental condition** of which any person is conscious (psychological facts) — e.g., that a man heard or saw something, said certain words, holds an opinion, has an intention, acts in good faith, has a certain reputation.

*Note on "evidence":* the definition is **inclusive but narrow** — it speaks of what is produced **in court**. Other materials on which a court acts — the **accused's statement under s. 342 CrPC**, **local inspection**, **material objects** (weapons, blood-stained clothes), **admissions in pleadings**, **judicial notice** and **presumptions** — are not "evidence" in this sense but are matters the court may consider.

## 2.2 Relevant fact and fact in issue

1. **Relevant** (Art. 2(2)) — one fact is relevant to another when connected with it in any of the ways set out in the relevancy Articles (18–69).
2. **Facts in issue** (Art. 2(3)) — any fact from which, by itself or with other facts, the **existence, non-existence, nature or extent of any right, liability or disability** asserted or denied in the suit or proceeding **necessarily follows**. In a civil suit, the fact asserted or denied in answer to an **issue** framed under the CPC is a fact in issue. *Illus.:* in a murder trial — that A caused B's death; that A intended it; that A received grave and sudden provocation; that A was of unsound mind.

## 2.3 Proved, disproved, not proved

{w:20,80}
|**Proved** (Art. 2(4))|When, after considering the matters before it, the court **either believes it to exist**, or considers its existence **so probable that a prudent man ought**, in the circumstances of the particular case, **to act upon the supposition that it exists**|
|**Disproved** (Art. 2(5))|When the court **believes that it does not exist**, or considers its non-existence so probable that a prudent man ought to act on the supposition that it does not exist|
|**Not proved** (Art. 2(6))|When it is **neither proved nor disproved** — the evidence leaves the court in doubt|

**The prudent man test** is one standard for all cases, but **its application** differs: in **civil** cases a **preponderance of probability** satisfies the prudent man; in **criminal** cases the prudent man will not act unless guilt is proved **beyond reasonable doubt**, because the consequences are grave.

:::eg Illustrations (for the exam)
1. **Proved:** two independent eye-witnesses, corroborated by the post-mortem and the recovery of the weapon, say A shot B — the court believes A shot B.
2. **Disproved:** A's plea that he was in Lahore is supported by hospital records showing his admission there that day — the court believes he was not at the scene in Islamabad: the prosecution allegation is disproved.
3. **Not proved:** the plaintiff alleges an oral loan of Rs. 500,000; he produces one interested witness; the defendant denies; nothing tips the balance — the loan is **not proved**, and the party bearing the burden (the plaintiff) **fails**.
:::

## 2.4 May presume, shall presume, conclusive proof

{w:20,40,40}
|Term|Meaning|Examples|
|**May presume** (Art. 2(7))|The court **may** regard the fact as proved unless and until disproved, **or may call for proof** — a **discretionary** presumption of fact|Art. 129 (possession of stolen goods; accomplice unworthy of credit; withheld evidence); Art. 100 (documents 30 years old)|
|**Shall presume** (Art. 2(8))|The court **shall** regard the fact as proved **unless and until disproved** — a **compulsory but rebuttable** presumption of law|Art. 90 (certified copies); Art. 95 (**powers of attorney**); Art. 92 (documents kept under law)|
|**Conclusive proof** (Art. 2(9))|On proof of one fact the court **shall regard the other as proved** and **shall not allow evidence to disprove it** — an **irrebuttable** presumption|Art. 128 (birth during a valid marriage — legitimacy); Art. 55 (judgments in rem)|

## 2.5 Judicial notice (LHC 2023)

**Judicial notice** means the court **recognises a fact as true without proof** because it is notorious or official (Arts. 111–112). "No fact of which the court will take judicial notice need be proved" (Art. 111). The court **shall** take judicial notice of All-Pakistan laws, Articles of War, the course of proceedings of legislatures, the **seals** of courts and notaries, the accession to office, names and **signatures of public officers** (if gazetted), the existence and flag of States recognised by the Federal Government, **divisions of time** and geography, **public festivals, fasts and holidays** notified, the territories of Pakistan, the commencement and termination of **hostilities**, the names of **members and officers** of the court and of **advocates**, and the **rule of the road** (Art. 112(1)). On these and on matters of **public history, literature, science or art**, the court may consult **books of reference** (Art. 112(2)), and may refuse to take notice until the party produces such a book (Art. 112(3)). *Examples:* that 14 August is Independence Day; that a particular day in Ramadan was a public holiday; the date of a Gazette notification of a Federal law. (Full treatment — Topic 2.)

# Chapter 3 · Witnesses — competence (Arts. 3, 16, 17)

## 3.1 Who may testify — Article 3

1. **General rule:** "**All persons shall be competent to testify** unless the Court considers that they are prevented from **understanding the questions** put to them, or from giving **rational answers** to those questions, by **tender years, extreme old age, disease** (of body or mind), **or any other cause** of the same kind."
2. **First proviso:** a person **convicted by a court for perjury or giving false evidence** is **not competent** to testify;
3. **Second proviso:** unless the court is satisfied that he has **repented** and **mended his ways**;
4. **Third proviso:** the court shall determine competence in accordance with the qualifications prescribed by the **Injunctions of Islam** (Quran and Sunnah) for a witness — and **where such a witness is not forthcoming**, the court **may take the evidence of a witness who may be available** (so the Islamic standard is the ideal, but the absence of such witnesses does not stop the trial).
5. **Explanation:** a **lunatic** is not incompetent unless his lunacy prevents him from understanding questions and giving rational answers.

**Competence and credibility** — competence is the **legal capacity** to give evidence (decided by the judge); **credibility** is whether the evidence is **believed** (the weight).

### Child witnesses

1. **No minimum age** — the test is **understanding** and **rational answers**, not years.
2. The judge first holds a **preliminary examination (voir dire)** — simple questions about the child's name, school, family, the difference between truth and lies — and **records** his satisfaction about competence.
3. A child who does not understand the **nature of an oath** may give evidence **without oath** (Oaths Act 1873, s. 13 principle); his evidence is still evidence.
4. **Caution:** children are easily **tutored**; the rule of prudence is to look for **corroboration**, though a conviction on the uncorroborated testimony of a truthful child is lawful.

### Police witnesses

Police officers are **competent** witnesses like any others; there is **no rule** that their evidence must be rejected or must always be corroborated. But since they are interested in the success of their case, courts **scrutinise** it with care, especially in **recovery** cases, and expect **independent witnesses** from the locality where available (CrPC s. 103) — except under special laws (CNSA s. 25), where police evidence alone may suffice if confidence-inspiring.

### Other persons

1. **Parties** to a civil suit and their spouses — competent.
2. **Accused** — competent as a witness for the defence **on oath** at his own request (CrPC s. 340(2)); all accused, including an accomplice, are liable to **cross-examination** (Art. 44).
3. **Dumb witnesses** — may give evidence by writing or signs; such evidence is **oral** evidence.
4. **Judges and Magistrates** — competent, but privileged as to their judicial conduct (Art. 4).

## 3.2 The Islamic standard — Article 17

1. **Art. 17(1):** the **competence** of a person to testify, and the **number of witnesses** required in any case, shall be determined in accordance with the **Injunctions of Islam**.
2. **Art. 17(2):** unless otherwise provided in the Hudood laws or a special law —
   1. **(a)** in matters pertaining to **financial or future obligations**, **if reduced to writing**, the instrument shall be **attested by two men, or one man and two women**, "so that one may remind the other", and evidence shall be led accordingly (Surah al-Baqarah 2:282);
   2. **(b)** in **all other matters**, the court may accept or act on the testimony of **one man or one woman** or such other evidence as the circumstances warrant.

**Effect of Art. 17(2)(a):** a written **agreement to sell**, **pronote**, **receipt**, or **loan agreement** must be attested by two men (or one man and two women), and **both** attesting witnesses must be **produced** to prove it (read with **Art. 79**). Failure to produce them is generally **fatal** to proof of the document (*Hafiz Tassaduq Hussain v. Lal Khatoon*, PLD 2011 SC 296; verify the citation).

## 3.3 The accomplice — Articles 16, 129(b)

1. **Art. 16:** "An accomplice shall be a **competent witness** against an accused person, **except** in the case of an offence punishable with **hadd**; and a conviction is **not illegal** merely because it proceeds upon the **uncorroborated** testimony of an accomplice."
2. **Art. 129(b):** the court **may presume** that an accomplice is **unworthy of credit, unless he is corroborated in material particulars**.
3. **Reconciliation:** Art. 16 is a rule of **law** (conviction on uncorroborated accomplice evidence is legal); Art. 129(b) is a rule of **prudence** (the court should ordinarily look for corroboration). Together: the court **may** convict without corroboration, but **as a matter of practice** it requires **independent corroboration** connecting the accused with the crime in **material particulars** — especially for an **approver** pardoned under CrPC s. 337.
4. **Why distrust?** An accomplice is a self-confessed criminal, hopes for leniency, and may shift blame.

## 4.1 Self-incrimination — Article 15 (LHC 2020)

1. **Rule:** a witness is **not excused** from answering a question relevant to the matter in issue (civil or criminal) on the ground that the answer **will criminate** him, or expose him to a **penalty or forfeiture**.
2. **Protection (proviso):** no such answer which a witness is **compelled** to give shall subject him to **arrest or prosecution**, or be **proved against him** in any criminal proceeding, **except a prosecution for giving false evidence** by that answer.
3. **How the protection is claimed:** the witness should **object** to the question, so that the answer is shown to have been **compelled**; a voluntary answer volunteered without objection may not attract the protection.
4. **Accused persons** — distinct: an **accused** cannot be compelled to be a **witness against himself** (Constitution, **Art. 13(b)**); he gives evidence only at his own request (CrPC s. 340(2)), and his examination under s. 342 is without oath.
5. **Questions affecting credit only** (Arts. 141–143) — where the question is relevant **only to credit**, the court decides whether the witness shall be compelled to answer (Topic 2).

# Chapter 5 · Relevancy of facts (Arts. 18 to 29) — LHC 2017

## 5.1 The basic rule — Article 18

Evidence may be given of the **existence or non-existence of every fact in issue** and of such other facts as are **declared relevant** by the Order, **and of no others**. The Explanation preserves the **CPC** rules — e.g., a document not produced at the first hearing cannot be proved later except as the CPC allows (*illus. (b)*; O. VII r. 18, O. XIII r. 2).

## 5.2 The principal classes of relevant facts

![The principal classes of relevant facts](relevancy_classes.png)

{w:14,86}
|Art. 19|**Same transaction (res gestae)** — facts which, though not in issue, are so connected with a fact in issue as to **form part of the same transaction**, whether at the same time and place or not (*illus.*: what A, B or bystanders said or did at the beating, or so shortly before or after it as to form part of the transaction)|
|Art. 20|**Occasion, cause or effect** — facts which are the occasion, cause or effect of facts in issue, or the **state of things** under which they happened, or which afforded an **opportunity** (marks of a struggle at the scene; B showed his money at the fair before the robbery)|
|Art. 21|**Motive, preparation and conduct** — any fact showing **motive or preparation**; the **previous or subsequent conduct** of a party or of the victim, if influenced by a fact in issue (absconding; procuring poison; destroying evidence; the victim's **prompt complaint** of rape or robbery — *illus. (j), (k)*); statements accompanying and explaining conduct (Expl. 1); statements made **in a person's presence** affecting his conduct (Expl. 2 — the accused **ran away** on hearing "the police are coming")|
|Art. 22|**Explanatory and introductory facts** — facts necessary to explain or introduce a relevant fact, to support or rebut an inference, to **establish identity**, to fix **time or place**, or to show the **relation of parties** (basis of the **identification parade**)|
|Art. 23|**Conspiracy** — where there is reasonable ground to believe that persons conspired, anything said, done or written by any one of them **in reference to the common intention**, after it was first entertained, is relevant **against each** — both to prove the conspiracy and each person's part in it|
|Art. 24|**Facts inconsistent with or making a fact highly probable or improbable** — the basis of the plea of **alibi** (A was at Lahore when the crime was committed at Peshawar) and of eliminating other suspects|
|Art. 25|**Damages** — facts enabling the court to determine the amount|
|Art. 26|**Right or custom** — transactions by which it was created, claimed, recognised or denied, and **particular instances** of its exercise|
|Art. 27|**State of mind or body** — intention, knowledge, good faith, negligence, rashness, ill-will; bodily feelings — relevant when in issue; must relate to the **particular matter** (Expl. 1); a **previous conviction** may then be relevant (Expl. 2) (possession of many stolen articles shows knowledge; a dog's previous bites show the owner's knowledge of its ferocity)|
|Art. 28|**Accident or design** — a **series of similar occurrences** shows the act was intentional (successive insured houses burnt; repeated false entries)|
|Art. 29|**Course of business** — the existence of a course of business according to which an act would naturally have been done (a letter posted in the usual way and not returned)|

## 5.3 Plea of alibi — Article 24 (LL.B. 2021)

**Alibi** ("elsewhere") — the accused says he was **at another place** when the offence was committed, so he **could not** have committed it. The fact of his presence elsewhere is "not otherwise relevant" but becomes relevant under **Art. 24** as **inconsistent** with the fact in issue (his presence at the crime). **Burden:** the prosecution must prove presence; the accused who pleads alibi must **establish it** (Art. 122 principle — facts within his knowledge), but even an alibi not fully proved may create **reasonable doubt**. It must be raised at the **earliest** opportunity (s. 342 statement), and supported by credible, preferably documentary, evidence; a **false alibi** is a circumstance against the accused but not proof of guilt.

## 5.4 Identification evidence (LHC 2017)

Identity is relevant under **Art. 22**. The **substantive** evidence is identification **in court**; a prior **test identification parade** before a Magistrate **corroborates** it. Identification evidence is **dangerous** because honest witnesses can be **mistaken** — fleeting glimpse, darkness, stress, the weapon focus, cross-race or stranger identification, and the risk that the witness saw the accused at the police station or in the newspapers. **Safeguards:** a parade held **promptly**; the accused kept **muffled**; **dummies** of similar description; the Magistrate records the **role** attributed and any **objection**; the court weighs the **opportunity** the witness had (Turnbull-type caution — duration, distance, light, prior acquaintance). Identification **without assigning a specific role** carries little weight.

# Chapter 6 · Admissions and confessions (Arts. 30 to 45) — LHC 2023

## 6.1 Admission — Articles 30 to 36

1. **Definition (Art. 30):** "An admission is a **statement, oral or documentary**, which **suggests any inference** as to any fact in issue or relevant fact, and which is made by any of the persons, and under the circumstances, hereinafter mentioned." Statements **generated by an automated information system** may be attributed to the person controlling it (Explanation, 2002).
2. **Who may make it (Arts. 31–33):** (i) a **party** or his **authorised agent**; (ii) a party suing or sued in a **representative** character — only while he held that character; (iii) persons with a **proprietary or pecuniary interest** in the subject-matter, while the interest continues; (iv) **predecessors in interest** — persons from whom the parties derive their interest; (v) persons whose **position or liability** must be proved against a party (Art. 32 — *illus.*: C's statement that he owed rent to B is an admission against A, B's rent-collector); (vi) persons to whom a party has **expressly referred** for information (Art. 33 — "Go and ask C").
3. **Proof (Art. 34):** admissions may be proved **against** the maker or his representative, but **not by or on his behalf** — **except** (1) when it would be relevant as a statement of a dead person under Art. 46; (2) when it states a **state of mind or body** at or about the time, accompanied by **conduct** making its falsehood improbable; (3) when it is relevant **otherwise than as an admission** (*illus.*: the captain's log; A's letter dated at Lahore — admissible for him).
4. **Oral admissions of a document's contents (Art. 35):** not relevant unless the party is entitled to give **secondary evidence** or the document's genuineness is in question.
5. **Without prejudice (Art. 36):** in civil cases, an admission made on an **express condition** that it is not to be given in evidence, or in circumstances showing that the parties so agreed (**settlement negotiations**), is not relevant.
6. **Effect (Art. 45):** admissions are **not conclusive** proof but may operate as **estoppels**. **Judicial admissions** in pleadings or at the hearing dispense with proof (Art. 113; O. XII CPC) and are **binding**; **extra-judicial** admissions are evidence that may be **explained or rebutted**.

## 6.2 Confession — Articles 37 to 43

A **confession** is not defined in the Order; it is an **admission by an accused** person **of the offence charged**, or of **all the facts constituting it** — "a statement made by an accused admitting the offence, or at any rate substantially all the facts which constitute the offence" (*Pakala Narayana Swami v. Emperor*, PC 1939). An exculpatory statement is **not** a confession.

{w:14,86}
|Art. 37|**Irrelevant** if caused by an **inducement, threat or promise** (i) having reference to the **charge**, (ii) proceeding from a **person in authority**, and (iii) sufficient to give the accused **reasonable grounds** to suppose he would **gain an advantage or avoid an evil of a temporal nature**|
|Art. 38|**No confession made to a police officer** shall be proved against an accused — whatever its circumstances|
|Art. 39|**No confession made while in police custody**, unless in the **immediate presence of a Magistrate**, shall be proved against the person — subject to Art. 40|
|Art. 40|**Discovery** — when a fact is **discovered** in consequence of information from an accused **in police custody**, so much of the information as **relates distinctly to the fact discovered** may be proved, whether or not it amounts to a confession (e.g., "I buried the pistol under the tree" → the pistol recovered)|
|Art. 41|A confession made **after the impression** of the inducement, threat or promise has been **fully removed** is relevant|
|Art. 42|A confession otherwise relevant does **not** become irrelevant because made under a **promise of secrecy**, by **deception**, when **drunk**, in answer to questions, or **without warning** — **but this does not apply to Hudood trials** (proviso)|
|Art. 43|A confession by one of several accused **tried jointly for the same offence** is **proof against the maker** and may be considered as **circumstantial evidence** against the others (*illus.*: A says "B and I murdered C" — may be considered against B if jointly tried)|
|Art. 44|All accused persons, **including an accomplice**, are liable to **cross-examination** (when they give evidence)|

### Kinds of confession

1. **Judicial** — made before a **Magistrate** under **s. 164 CrPC** with all safeguards, or before the trial court (plea of guilty); high value if **voluntary and true**.
2. **Extra-judicial** — made to a **private person** (a relative, a respectable of the area); regarded as a **weak type of evidence**, easy to fabricate, which needs **strong corroboration**, especially when made to a person with whom the accused had no reason to confide (*Sajid Mumtaz v. Basharat*, 2006 SCMR 231; verify).
3. **Retracted** — later withdrawn; may found a conviction if found voluntary and true, but **corroboration** in material particulars is required as a rule of prudence.

# Chapter 7 · Statements of persons who cannot be called (Arts. 46 to 53)

## 7.1 Why these are exceptions to the hearsay rule

Ordinarily oral evidence must be **direct** (Art. 71 — Topic 2). But when the maker of a statement is **dead, cannot be found, has become incapable** of giving evidence, or cannot be produced without **unreasonable delay or expense**, Art. 46 makes his statement relevant in **eight** situations where it carries a **circumstantial guarantee** of truth.

## 7.2 The eight cases — Article 46

1. **Dying declaration (Art. 46(1))** — a statement as to the **cause of his death** or the **circumstances of the transaction** resulting in his death, in cases where the cause of his death is in question — **whether or not he was under expectation of death**, and in **any** proceeding (civil or criminal).
2. **In the course of business (46(2))** — entries in account books, receipts, letters, dated documents made in the ordinary course of business or duty.
3. **Against interest (46(3))** — a statement against the maker's **pecuniary or proprietary** interest, or which would expose him to prosecution or damages.
4. **Opinion as to a public right or custom (46(4))** — made **before** any controversy arose.
5. **Relationship (46(5))** — by a person with **special means of knowledge** (a family member), made **before** the dispute arose (*ante litem motam*).
6. **Family affairs in wills, deeds, pedigrees, tombstones, portraits (46(6))**.
7. **In a document relating to a transaction** by which a right or custom was created or asserted (46(7)).
8. **Feelings or impressions of a number of persons** expressed at the time (46(8) — the remarks of a crowd viewing a caricature).

## 7.3 Dying declarations — principles

1. **No oath and no cross-examination** — so the court must be satisfied that it is **true, voluntary**, and made in a **fit state of mind**.
2. A dying declaration, if found genuine and truthful, **can be the sole basis** of conviction; but as a **rule of prudence** courts look for **corroboration**, and treat it with caution where the deceased had **enmity** with the accused, was **unable to see** the assailant, or was **tutored** by relatives.
3. **Best form** — recorded by a **Magistrate** (or a Justice of the Peace — CrPC s. 22B; burn victims — s. 174A) in the **declarant's own words**, after a **doctor's certificate** of fitness; question-and-answer form; read over.
4. A **written FIR** lodged by a person who later dies of his injuries may be treated as a dying declaration.
5. Statements recorded under **s. 161 CrPC** of a person who later dies are admissible under Art. 46(1) (CrPC s. 162(2)).

## 7.4 Electronic records — Article 46A (2002)

"Statements in the form of **electronic documents** generated, received or recorded by an **automated information system** while it is **in working order**, are relevant facts." (Bank statements produced by a computer system, call-data records, CCTV footage — subject to proof of authenticity under Arts. 73, 78A and 164.)

## 7.5 Former evidence — Article 47

Evidence given by a witness in a **judicial proceeding** is relevant in a **subsequent** proceeding (or a later stage of the same) to prove the facts it states, when the witness is **dead, cannot be found, is incapable, is kept out of the way by the adverse party**, or cannot be produced without unreasonable delay or expense — **provided** (i) the proceedings were between the **same parties** or their representatives; (ii) the adverse party had the **right and opportunity to cross-examine**; and (iii) the **questions in issue were substantially the same**. A criminal trial is a proceeding between the **prosecutor and the accused** (Explanation). (Compare CrPC s. 512 — Topic 3 of Criminal Law.)

## 7.6 Statements in special circumstances — Articles 48 to 53

1. **Art. 48** — entries in **books of account** regularly kept in the course of business are relevant, but **alone are not sufficient** to charge any person with liability.
2. **Art. 49** — entries in **public records** made by a public servant in the discharge of duty (birth registers, revenue records — *jamabandi*, *khasra girdawari*, mutations) — relevant, and carry a **presumption of truth** unless rebutted.
3. **Art. 50** — statements in published **maps, charts and plans** offered for sale or made under Government authority.
4. **Art. 51** — statements of public facts in **Acts or notifications**.
5. **Art. 52** — statements of **law** in law books of a country and **reports of rulings**.
6. **Art. 53** — where a statement is part of a **conversation, document or series of letters**, so much of the rest as is **necessary to understand** it must be proved.

# Chapter 8 · Judgments, opinions and character (Arts. 54 to 69)

## 8.1 Judgments of courts — Articles 54 to 58

1. **Art. 54** — a previous judgment, order or decree is relevant where it **bars a second suit or trial** (res judicata — s. 11 CPC; autrefois acquit — s. 403 CrPC).
2. **Art. 55** — **judgments in rem** — final judgments in **probate, matrimonial, admiralty or insolvency** jurisdiction conferring or taking away a **legal character** — are **conclusive proof** against **everyone** of that character.
3. **Art. 56** — judgments **in personam** relating to matters of a **public nature** are relevant but **not conclusive**.
4. **Art. 57** — other judgments are **not** relevant, unless their **existence** is a fact in issue or relevant under another provision (*illus.*: a judgment of conviction is not evidence of guilt in a later civil suit for damages; but a decree may be relevant to show possession).
5. **Art. 58** — any party may show that a relevant judgment was delivered by a court **without jurisdiction**, or obtained by **fraud or collusion**.

## 8.2 Opinion of experts — Articles 59, 60, 65

1. **Art. 59** — when the court has to form an opinion on **foreign law, science or art**, **identity of handwriting or finger impressions**, or the **authenticity and integrity of electronic documents** (2002), the opinions of persons **specially skilled** — including in **bomb disposal** (2023) and in the **functioning, programming and operations of information systems** — are relevant; such persons are **experts**. *Illus.:* doctors on the symptoms of poison; psychiatrists on **insanity**; handwriting experts.
2. **Art. 60** — facts bearing upon the experts' opinion (supporting or inconsistent) are relevant.
3. **Art. 65** — the **grounds** of an opinion are relevant.
4. **Weight** — expert evidence is **opinion evidence**, **corroborative** in nature; the court is **not bound** by it, especially where it conflicts with reliable ocular evidence; the court may compare handwriting **itself** (Art. 84). Medical evidence can **contradict** but cannot by itself **identify** the culprit.

## 8.3 Proving handwriting — the kinds of evidence (LHC 2017)

When the genuineness of a letter or signature is in dispute, it may be proved by:

1. **Direct evidence** — a person who **saw the document written or signed** (Art. 71);
2. **Admission** by the writer (Arts. 30–34; Art. 81 for execution of an attested document);
3. **Opinion of a person acquainted with the handwriting** (Art. 61) — one who has **seen him write**, has **received documents** purporting to be written by him in answer to his own, or in the ordinary course of business has had such documents habitually submitted to him;
4. **Expert opinion** of a handwriting or fingerprint expert (Art. 59);
5. **Comparison by the court** of the disputed writing with writing **admitted or proved** to be his, and the court may direct the person present to **write words** for comparison (Art. 84);
6. **Circumstantial evidence** — the contents, the paper, the ink, the course of correspondence, and **presumptions** (Art. 100 — documents thirty years old from proper custody; Art. 78A — electronic signature by security procedure).

## 8.4 Other opinions — Articles 61 to 64

1. **Art. 62** — opinions of persons likely to know of a **general right or custom**;
2. **Art. 63** — opinions as to **usages and tenets** of a body of men or family, the constitution of a religious or charitable foundation, the meaning of words used in particular districts;
3. **Art. 64** — **conduct** of a member of the family or a person with special means of knowledge showing his opinion as to **relationship** — but such opinion is **not sufficient to prove a marriage** in prosecutions for bigamy and similar offences or in divorce proceedings (proviso).

## 8.5 Character — Articles 66 to 69
