# Answer 8 · Setting aside ex parte orders and decrees; dismissal for default (LHC 2020)

:::glance LHC CIVIL JUDGE EXAM 2020 · CIVIL LAW-II · Q. 3 · 20 MARKS
Discuss all specific provisions of C.P.C. for setting aside ex parte orders, judgments and decrees. Dismissal of the suit in presence or absence of defendants.
*(Related: PU LLB 2007 — "Enumerate the difference between order for ex parte proceedings and an ex parte decree. Also suggest the remedies.")*
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](a8_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Two halves. (1) **Ex parte**: when the court proceeds ex parte (O. IX r. 6), the remedy **before** the decree (r. 7, "good cause") and **after** the decree (r. 13, "sufficient cause" / no due service), plus appeal, review and s. 12(2). (2) **Dismissal**: when the suit is dismissed with the defendant **present** (r. 8) or **absent** (rr. 2, 3, 5), and the remedies (restoration, fresh suit, bar of r. 9). Limitation (Arts. 163, 164) and appeals (O. XLIII) complete the answer.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — balancing hearing and diligence

Order IX regulates the **consequences of non-appearance**. It protects the right to be heard (*audi alteram partem*) while refusing help to the negligent (*vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt*). It provides for ex parte proceedings and decrees against an absent defendant, dismissal against an absent plaintiff, and remedies to undo both where there was good reason.

### 2. When the court proceeds ex parte — r. 6

Where the plaintiff appears and the defendant does not: "(a) if it is proved that the summons was **duly served**, the Court may proceed ex parte and pass decree without recording evidence; (b) if it is not proved that the summons was duly served, the Court shall direct a **second summons**…; (c) if … served … but not in sufficient time…, the Court shall **postpone** the hearing." The court must therefore first be satisfied about **due service** (O. V rr. 10-A, 17, 19, 20).

### 3. Remedy against an ex parte order — r. 7

"Where the Court has adjourned the hearing of the suit ex parte, and the defendant, at or before such hearing, appears and assigns **good cause** for his previous non-appearance, he may, upon such terms as the Court directs as to costs or otherwise, be heard in answer to the suit as if he had appeared on the day fixed for his appearance." The defendant joins the proceedings from that stage; he cannot always reopen what was done in his absence.

### 4. Remedy against an ex parte decree — r. 13

#### i) The provision

"In any case in which a decree is passed ex parte against a defendant, he may apply to the Court by which the decree was passed for an order to set it aside; and if he satisfies the Court that the summons was **not duly served**, or that he was **prevented by any sufficient cause** from appearing when the suit was called on for hearing, the Court **shall** make an order setting aside the decree … upon such terms as to costs, payment into Court or otherwise as it thinks fit."

#### ii) The two provisos

(a) If the decree cannot be set aside against one defendant only, it may be set aside against all. (b) "No decree passed ex parte shall be set aside merely on the ground of any **irregularity in the service** of summons, if the Court is satisfied, for reason to be recorded, that the defendant had **knowledge of the date of hearing** in sufficient time to appear."

#### iii) Sufficient cause

A cause beyond the party's control, without negligence and with bona fides — illness, accident, mistake of counsel. It is construed **liberally** to advance substantial justice (*G.P. Srivastava v. R.K. Raizada*, (2000) 3 SCC 54), but not to reward carelessness (*Parimal v. Veena*, (2011) 3 SCC 545).

#### iv) Procedure and limitation

Notice to the opposite party is compulsory (r. 14). Limitation: **Article 164** of the Limitation Act — **30 days** from the date of the decree, or, where the summons was not duly served, from the date of **knowledge** of the decree; **s. 5** of the Limitation Act applies (r. 13(2)). An order **rejecting** the application is appealable (O. XLIII r. 1(d)).

### 5. Other remedies against an ex parte decree

1. **Appeal** under s. 96 — the Islamabad text expressly says "An appeal may lie from an original decree passed ex-parte" (s. 96(2), Act XIX of 2023).
2. **Review** under s. 114 / O. XLVII — error apparent on the record or new evidence.
3. **Section 12(2)** — where the decree was obtained by **fraud or misrepresentation** (e.g., a false address to avoid service) or **without jurisdiction**.

Both r. 13 and appeal may be pursued, but once the appeal is decided on **merits**, the trial decree merges into the appellate decree (*Bhanu Kumar Jain v. Archana Kumar*, (2005) 1 SCC 787).

### 6. Ex parte order and ex parte decree distinguished

{w:22,39,39}
|Basis|Ex parte order|Ex parte decree|
|Stage|During the suit|At its end|
|Remedy|r. 7 — appear and show **good cause**|r. 13 — **sufficient cause** or no due service; appeal; review; s. 12(2)|
|Effect of success|Defendant heard from that stage|Decree set aside; suit restored|
|Appeal|None|Rejection appealable — O. XLIII r. 1(d)|

### 7. Dismissal in the absence of the defendant — rr. 2, 3 and 5

- **r. 2:** summons not served because the plaintiff **did not pay** process fee — suit may be dismissed (not if the defendant appears anyway).
- **r. 3:** **neither party** appears — suit may be dismissed.
- **Remedy (r. 4):** the plaintiff may bring a **fresh suit** (subject to limitation) **or** apply for **restoration** on sufficient cause.
- **r. 5:** summons returned unserved and the plaintiff fails for **three months** to apply for fresh summons — the court **shall** dismiss the suit against that defendant (unless diligence, evasion or other sufficient cause is shown); **fresh suit** allowed (r. 5(2)).

### 8. Dismissal in the presence of the defendant — rr. 8 and 9

#### i) r. 8

"Where the defendant appears and the plaintiff does not appear when the suit is called on for hearing, the Court **shall** make an order that the suit be dismissed, unless the defendant admits the claim, or part thereof, in which case the Court shall pass a decree against the defendant upon such admission."

#### ii) r. 9

The plaintiff is **precluded from bringing a fresh suit** on the same cause of action (see also s. 12(1)); but he may apply to **set aside the dismissal** on **sufficient cause**, with notice to the other side; s. 5 Limitation Act applies. Limitation: **Article 163** — **30 days** from dismissal. Rejection of the application is appealable (O. XLIII r. 1(c)).

### 9. Distinguish O. XVII r. 3

Where the plaintiff was given time to produce evidence and defaulted, the court may "proceed to decide the suit forthwith" (O. XVII r. 3). That is a decision **on merits** — a **decree** appealable under s. 96 — not a dismissal for default under O. IX. The remedy is appeal, not restoration.

### 10. Illustration

Bashir is sued for Rs. 3 million. The process server affixes the summons without naming identifiers; the court does not examine him (O. V r. 19); the registered summons returns "addressee left". An ex parte decree follows. Bashir learns of it four months later when his account is attached. Service was **not due**; limitation under Art. 164 runs from **knowledge**; the second proviso does not apply (no proof he knew the date). The decree should be **set aside** under r. 13 on such terms as to costs as the court thinks fit, and the suit restored for trial.

### 11. Conclusion

Order IX gives every absent party a **way back** if his absence was not his fault — r. 7 (good cause) during the suit, r. 13 (sufficient cause or no due service) after an ex parte decree, r. 4 and r. 9 after dismissal — while making the negligent plaintiff pay the price of r. 9's bar on a fresh suit. Appeal (s. 96(2)), review and s. 12(2) supplement these remedies. The guiding principle remains that cases should, as far as possible, be decided **on merits** after hearing both sides.

## Why this answer scores

- **Both halves** of the question are answered — ex parte remedies and dismissals (with and without the defendant).
- The **exact grounds** of r. 13, both **provisos**, the **limitation articles** (163, 164) and **appeals** (O. XLIII) are given.
- The **comparison table** answers the related LLB question directly.
- **Islamabad's s. 96(2)** and the **O. XVII r. 3** distinction show depth beyond the textbook.

# Answer 9 · Frame the appropriate issues — recovery suit (LHC 2017)

:::glance LHC CIVIL JUDGE EXAM 2017 · CIVIL LAW-II · Q. 3 · 20 MARKS
Ahmed supplied certain goods worth Rs. 2.00 million received by Zahid on behalf of his employer, namely, Babar. However, on account of the non-payment of the outstanding amount (Rs. 2.00 million) within the stipulated period, Ahmed filed a recovery suit against Zahid. In a written statement, Zahid controverted the contents of the plaint and asserted that said amount was outstanding against Babar. He also raised preliminary objections regarding maintainability of suit, limitation, deficient court fee, non-joinder of parties, false and vexatious claim, and exemplary costs. Dismissal of the suit was also prayed for. In light of the above facts, frame the appropriate issues.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](a9_map.png)

## Decoding the question

This is the **practical skill** the IHC syllabus promises to test. The examiner wants (1) a brief statement of the **law of issues** (O. XIV r. 1), (2) an analysis of the **pleadings** — what is affirmed, denied and newly pleaded, (3) **correctly worded issues** with the **onus** (OPP / OPD), and (4) a short **note** explaining choices (e.g., why "exemplary costs" is not a separate issue; which issues can be tried first). Write it as a judge would record it.

## The model answer

### 1. The law — what an issue is

"Issues arise when a material proposition of fact or law is affirmed by the one party and denied by the other" (O. XIV r. 1(1)). "Material propositions are those propositions of law or fact which a plaintiff must allege in order to show a right to sue or a defendant must allege in order to constitute his defence" (r. 1(2)). "Each material proposition affirmed by one party and denied by the other shall form the subject of a distinct issue" (r. 1(3)). The court frames issues from the pleadings, statements of the parties and documents (r. 3).

### 2. Analysis of the pleadings

#### i) Plaintiff's material propositions

(a) Ahmed **supplied goods worth Rs. 2,000,000**; (b) the goods were **received by Zahid**; (c) payment was due within a **stipulated period** and was **not made**; (d) **Zahid is liable** to pay.

#### ii) Defendant's denials and new pleas

(a) Denies liability: the goods were received **on behalf of his employer Babar**, against whom the amount is outstanding — a plea of **agency**; (b) preliminary objections: **maintainability**, **limitation**, **deficient court fee**, **non-joinder** (of Babar), suit **false and vexatious**, and **exemplary costs**.

#### iii) Admitted facts

Zahid does not deny that the goods were supplied or received by him; his case is that he received them **for Babar**. (Even so, the supply and outstanding amount should be put in issue because he "controverted the contents of the plaint" generally.)

### 3. Issues framed

:::issues In the Court of Civil Judge 1st Class, Islamabad · Civil Suit No. ___ · Ahmed v. Zahid (Recovery of Rs. 2,000,000)
1. Whether the suit is not maintainable in its present form? **OPD**
2. Whether the suit is barred by limitation? **OPD**
3. Whether the suit has not been properly valued for the purposes of court fee and jurisdiction, and the court fee affixed is deficient? **OPD**
4. Whether the suit is bad for non-joinder of Babar, a necessary party? **OPD**
5. Whether the plaintiff supplied goods worth Rs. 2,000,000 which were received by the defendant, and the said amount is outstanding and recoverable from the defendant? **OPP**
6. Whether the defendant received the goods only as an employee / agent for and on behalf of his disclosed principal (employer) Babar and is, therefore, not personally liable to pay the price? **OPD**
7. Whether the suit is false and vexatious to the knowledge of the plaintiff and the defendant is entitled to special costs under section 35B CPC? **OPD**
8. Relief.
:::

### 4. Note on the issues — why framed this way

#### i) Onus

The **plaintiff** must prove his own case (supply, receipt, non-payment) — Issue 5, **OPP**. The **defendant** must prove every special plea he raised — agency, limitation, valuation, non-joinder, falsity — **OPD**. "Whoever desires any Court to give judgment as to any legal right or liability dependent on the existence of facts which he asserts, must prove that those facts exist" (Art. 117, Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984).

#### ii) Issue 6 — the real controversy

Under **s. 230 of the Contract Act 1872**, an agent cannot, in the absence of a contract to that effect, personally enforce contracts entered into on behalf of his principal, **nor is he personally bound by them** — subject to exceptions (e.g., where the principal is undisclosed or the agent agreed to be personally liable). Whether Zahid acted for a **disclosed** principal is therefore decisive.

#### iii) Issue 4 — non-joinder

If Babar is the real buyer, he is a **necessary party** (no effective decree for the price can be passed against him in his absence). Under O. I r. 9 the suit is not defeated by non-joinder of a proper party, but non-joinder of a **necessary** party not cured is fatal; the plaintiff may apply under O. I r. 10(2) to implead Babar.

#### iv) Issue 2 — limitation

The suit is for the **price of goods sold and delivered**. Limitation is **three years** (Limitation Act, First Schedule, Art. 52 — from delivery where no credit period is fixed; Art. 53 where a fixed period of credit is agreed — from expiry of that period). If the dates are admitted, the issue can be decided **first** as an issue of law under **O. XIV r. 2**.

#### v) Why no separate issue on "exemplary costs"

Costs are not a cause of action and need no issue. The plea is covered by **Issue 7** (special costs under **s. 35B**) and by the court's power to award **actual costs** under **s. 35** (Islamabad text) at the end of the case.

#### vi) Issue 1 — maintainability

The defendant pleaded it generally. In the judgment, the court will decide it together with Issues 4 and 6 (a suit against an agent of a disclosed principal may be held not maintainable against him).

### 5. Further directions after framing issues

"Parties to file **list of witnesses** and **certificate of readiness** within **seven days** (O. XVI r. 1). Plaintiff's evidence on the next date. The suit will be fixed for evidence of the plaintiff first, as the onus of the main issue (Issue 5) is on him; defendant's evidence thereafter."

### 6. Conclusion

The issues capture **every material proposition** in dispute: the plaintiff's claim (Issue 5), the defendant's agency defence (Issue 6), the legal objections (Issues 1–4), and the plea of false claim (Issue 7), with the onus placed on the party who asserts. Framed this way, the trial will focus on the true controversy — **who is liable for the price: the employee who received the goods, or his employer?**

## Why this answer scores

- It starts with the **law** (O. XIV r. 1) in two lines and then does what a judge does: **analyses the pleadings**.
- The issues are **precise, separate and correctly onused** (OPP / OPD), ending with "**Relief**".
- The **note** explains each choice and links the procedure to **substantive law** — s. 230 Contract Act and the Limitation Act — which impresses examiners.
- It knows what **not** to frame (no issue on "exemplary costs"; costs under ss. 35, 35B).

# Answer 10 · Write an order on an application under Order VII rule 11 (model question, IHC pattern)

:::glance MODEL QUESTION · IHC SYLLABUS PATTERN ("give the facts of a typical case … write a judgment") · 20 MARKS
Ali Raza filed a suit for **declaration and permanent injunction** in the court of a Civil Judge at Islamabad against the **Capital Housing Authority (CHA)**, a statutory body under the (hypothetical) Capital Housing Authority Act. He pleaded that Plot No. 22, Street 9, Sector I-14/2, was allotted to him in 2019; he paid all instalments; on 03.02.2026 the CHA cancelled the allotment **without any show-cause notice or hearing**, alleging non-payment, and re-allotted the plot to another person. He prayed for a declaration that the cancellation is illegal and void, and an injunction restraining the CHA from creating third-party rights.
The CHA filed an application under **Order VII rule 11 CPC** for rejection of the plaint on the grounds that (i) section 40 of the CHA Act provides that "no order passed by the Authority under this Act shall be called in question in any court", and the Act provides an appeal to the Chairman CHA within 30 days; and (ii) the plaint does not disclose a cause of action. The plaintiff resisted the application.
**Write a reasoned order on the application.**
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](a10_map.png)

## Decoding the question

The examiner wants a **court order** — in the right format — that applies the law of **O. VII r. 11** (only the plaint is seen) and **s. 9** (ouster clauses do not protect orders without jurisdiction, mala fide or against natural justice) to the facts. The order must decide each ground, give **reasons**, and end with a clear operative part. Remember: in Islamabad, s. 151 powers must be exercised "after recording reasons"; s. 1(4) asks the court to deal with cases "justly and fairly".

## The model answer

:::order IN THE COURT OF CIVIL JUDGE 1st CLASS, ISLAMABAD (WEST)
Civil Suit No. ___ of 2026 · Ali Raza v. Capital Housing Authority and another · Suit for declaration and permanent injunction

__ORDER__ (on the application of defendant No. 1 under Order VII rule 11 CPC)

1. **The application.** Through this order I shall decide the application of defendant No. 1, the Capital Housing Authority (CHA), under Order VII rule 11 CPC for rejection of the plaint.

2. **The plaint in brief.** The plaintiff asserts that Plot No. 22, Street 9, Sector I-14/2, was allotted to him in 2019; that he paid all instalments; and that on 03.02.2026 the CHA cancelled the allotment, alleging non-payment, without issuing any show-cause notice or affording a hearing, and re-allotted the plot. He seeks a declaration that the cancellation is illegal and void and an injunction against the creation of third-party rights.

3. **Grounds of the application.** The CHA contends that (i) the suit is barred by section 40 of the CHA Act, which provides that no order of the Authority "shall be called in question in any court", and that the plaintiff should have availed the departmental appeal; and (ii) the plaint discloses no cause of action.

4. **Reply.** The plaintiff contends that the cancellation was made without notice and hearing, is void, and is not protected by the ouster clause; and that the plaint discloses a complete cause of action.

5. **Arguments heard; record perused.**

6. **The law on Order VII rule 11.** The rule requires rejection where the plaint "does not disclose a cause of action" (clause (a)) or "where the suit appears from the statement in the plaint to be barred by any law" (clause (d)). At this stage the court looks **only at the averments in the plaint** and the documents annexed to it, assuming them to be correct; the defence is irrelevant (*Saleem Bhai v. State of Maharashtra*, (2003) 1 SCC 557). The plaint is read as a whole and meaningfully (*T. Arivandandam v. T.V. Satyapal*, (1977) 4 SCC 467). Rejection is a drastic power, to be used only where the bar or the absence of a cause of action is clear on the face of the plaint.

7. **Ground (i) — bar of section 40 of the CHA Act.** Under section 9 CPC, civil courts try all suits of a civil nature "excepting suits of which their cognizance is either expressly or impliedly barred". Exclusion of jurisdiction is not readily inferred and ouster clauses are strictly construed (*Secretary of State v. Mask & Co.*, AIR 1940 PC 105). It is settled that even where a statute ousts the civil court, the court retains jurisdiction to examine an order which is **without jurisdiction, mala fide, coram non judice** or passed in violation of the principles of natural justice, because such an order is not an order "under the Act" (*Abbasia Cooperative Bank v. Hakeem Hafiz Muhammad Ghaus*, PLD 1997 SC 3). The plaint specifically avers that the cancellation was made **without show-cause notice or hearing**, despite payment of all instalments. Taking these averments as true, the impugned order, if proved, is one made in violation of *audi alteram partem* and would fall outside the protection of section 40. The availability of a departmental appeal does not bar a civil suit against a void order. Whether the averments are true is a matter for evidence. The bar is therefore **not apparent from the plaint**.

8. **Ground (ii) — cause of action.** The plaint pleads the allotment, full payment, the cancellation without notice, and re-allotment to a third person, with dates. These facts, if proved, entitle the plaintiff to the declaration and injunction prayed for under sections 42 and 54 of the Specific Relief Act 1877. The plaint therefore **discloses a cause of action**.

9. **Conclusion.** The plaint neither fails to disclose a cause of action nor appears from its own statements to be barred by any law. The objections raised by the CHA involve disputed questions of fact (whether instalments were paid; whether notice was issued) which can be decided only after evidence. The application under Order VII rule 11 CPC is **dismissed**. The objection of jurisdiction shall, however, remain open and an appropriate issue shall be framed on it, to be decided with the other issues after evidence. Costs of the application shall abide the event.

10. Defendant No. 1 to file its written statement within thirty days (Order VIII rule 1 CPC). To come up on ___ for written statement and framing of issues.

Announced in open court · Dated ___ · Civil Judge 1st Class, Islamabad (West) (seal)
:::

### Points of law used in the order

1. **O. VII r. 11(a) and (d)** — look only at the plaint (*Saleem Bhai*; *Arivandandam*).
2. **s. 9 CPC** — presumption in favour of jurisdiction; strict construction of ouster clauses (*Mask & Co.*).
3. **Ouster clauses** do not protect orders without jurisdiction, mala fide, coram non judice or against natural justice (*Abbasia Cooperative Bank*, PLD 1997 SC 3; *Dhulabhai*, AIR 1969 SC 78).
4. **Disputed facts** mean the plaint cannot be rejected; frame an issue instead (O. XIV r. 1; r. 2 only if decidable without evidence).
5. **Specific Relief Act ss. 42 and 54** — declaration and perpetual injunction (Civil Law-II).

### Alternative outcome — when the plaint would be rejected

If the plaint itself had admitted that the plaintiff **was given notice and a hearing**, and had only attacked the CHA's **assessment of the facts** (e.g., that the default was minor), the order would be within the statute; the civil court could not sit in appeal over it, and the bar of section 40 would be apparent from the plaint. The plaint would then be **rejected under O. VII r. 11(d)**, leaving the plaintiff to his statutory appeal.

## Why this answer scores

- It is written **in the form of a real court order** — heading, title, numbered paragraphs, operative part, date and signature — exactly what the IHC syllabus says will be tested.
- It applies the **correct test** for O. VII r. 11 (plaint only) and the **correct law on ouster clauses**, each with authority.
- It **decides every ground** separately and gives **reasons**.
- It shows judicial maturity: the jurisdiction question is **kept open** as an issue, and the **alternative outcome** shows the examiner the candidate understands where the line falls.
