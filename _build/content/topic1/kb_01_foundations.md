# Chapter 1 · Civil procedure: the big picture

## 1.1 What is "civil procedure"?

The law has two kinds of rules.

- **Substantive law** tells us *what our rights and duties are*. The Contract Act tells us when a promise is binding; the Transfer of Property Act tells us how ownership passes. Substantive law creates and defines rights.
- **Procedural (adjective) law** tells us *how to enforce those rights in a court*. Where do I file my case? How do I tell the other side? What happens if he does not come? How is the case proved and decided? The **Code of Civil Procedure 1908** is the main procedural law for civil cases.

A simple picture: substantive law is the **destination**; procedural law is the **road** that takes you there. If the road is blocked or badly built, you never reach the destination, however strong your right is.

:::eg The two laws working together
Ahmed lends Bilal Rs. 500,000. Bilal refuses to repay. *Substantive* law (Contract Act, s. 73) gives Ahmed a right to recover the money and compensation. *Procedural* law (CPC) tells Ahmed to file a **plaint** (O. VII) in the court of the **lowest grade competent** to try it (s. 15), at the place where Bilal lives or where the cause of action arose (s. 20); the court will **summon** Bilal (s. 27, O. V), Bilal will file a **written statement** (O. VIII), the court will frame **issues** (O. XIV), record evidence, and pass a **judgment and decree** (s. 33). Without the procedure, Ahmed's right is only words on paper.
:::

## 1.2 Procedure is the handmaid of justice

The single most important principle of civil procedure is that **rules of procedure exist to help justice, not to defeat it**. A party should not lose a good case because of a small technical slip, if the slip can be cured without injustice to the other side.

:::case Imtiaz Ahmad v. Ghulam Ali (PLD 1963 SC 382)
__Context.__ A question arose whether a procedural defect should defeat a party's substantive right.

__Held.__ The Supreme Court of Pakistan (per Cornelius C.J.) said that the proper place of procedure in any system of administration of justice is to help and not to thwart the grant to the people of their rights. Procedure is the handmaid and not the mistress of justice.

__Ratio decidendi.__ Courts should interpret and apply procedural rules so as to advance substantive justice. A procedural lapse that does not cause real prejudice should be cured (for example by amendment, extension of time or costs), not used to throw out a case.

__Use in exam.__ Quote this whenever a question involves a technical objection: misjoinder (O. I r. 9), amendment of pleadings (O. VI r. 17), late written statement (O. VIII r. 1), deficiency of court fee (s. 149), enlargement of time (s. 148).
:::

:::case Sangram Singh v. Election Tribunal, Kotah (AIR 1955 SC 425)
__Held.__ A code of procedure is designed to facilitate justice and further its ends; it is not a penal enactment for punishment and penalties. Too technical a construction of sections that leaves no room for reasonable elasticity of interpretation should be guarded against. Persons should not be shut out from being heard unless the law clearly says so.

__Ratio.__ Procedural provisions should be read so that, as far as possible, both parties get a full hearing on the merits.
:::

But the principle has a limit. Procedure is not *optional*. Where a rule is **mandatory** and the law itself states the consequence of breaking it (for example, limitation, or rejection of a plaint that shows no cause of action), the court must apply it. Also, a party who **deliberately** ignores procedure — for example by seeking adjournment after adjournment — cannot hide behind the "handmaid" principle. The modern CPC (ss. 35A, 35B and O. XVII r. 3) punishes such conduct.

:::why Why have procedure at all?
1. **Fairness (natural justice).** Procedure guarantees that each side knows the other side's case and gets a chance to answer it — *audi alteram partem*.
2. **Certainty.** Everyone knows in advance where to sue, how long they have, and what to file.
3. **Efficiency.** Clear steps prevent endless litigation and save the time of courts and parties.
4. **Finality.** Rules like res judicata (s. 11) ensure that a dispute, once decided, stays decided.
:::

## 1.3 The structure of the CPC

The CPC has two parts that work together.

{w:28,72}
|Part|What it contains|
|**The body of the Code** — 158 sections (plus s. 159 added for Islamabad in 2023)|The *principles*: jurisdiction (s. 9), res judicata (s. 11), place of suing (ss. 15–20), execution (ss. 36–74), appeals (ss. 96–112), review (s. 114), revision (s. 115), inherent powers (s. 151). The sections can be changed only by the legislature (Parliament for Islamabad).|
|**The First Schedule** — 51 Orders, each divided into rules|The *detailed working procedure*: parties (O. I), plaint (O. VII), written statement (O. VIII), default (O. IX), issues (O. XIV), injunctions (O. XXXIX), appeals (O. XLI), review (O. XLVII). Under **ss. 121–122**, the High Court can amend these rules for courts under it, so long as they do not conflict with the body of the Code.|

:::tip Section vs rule — how to cite
Write **"section 11 CPC"** or **"s. 11 CPC"** for a section. Write **"Order VII rule 11 CPC"** or **"O. VII r. 11 CPC"** for a rule in the First Schedule. Never write "section 11 of Order VII". Examiners notice.
:::

When a section and a rule appear to conflict, the **section prevails** (s. 121 read with s. 122: rules must not be inconsistent with the body of the Code). The sections lay down the jurisdiction; the rules describe the manner in which it is exercised.

## 1.4 The primary objective of the Code (s. 1(4)) — Islamabad

:::ict Section 1(4) CPC — inserted for Islamabad by Act VII of 2020
> "(4) The primary objective of this Code is to enable the Courts to— (a) deal the cases justly and fairly; (b) encourage parties to alternate dispute resolution procedure if it considers appropriate; (c) save expends and time both of Courts and litigants; and (d) enforce compliance with provision of the Code."

This is an **overriding objective** clause, similar to Rule 1.1 of the English Civil Procedure Rules 1998. It tells the judge to read every other provision of the Code in the light of these four aims. In an answer on any procedural question in the IHC paper, you can strengthen your argument by saying that your reading "advances the primary objective of the Code under s. 1(4)".
:::

Related Islamabad provisions that serve the same objective:

- **s. 89A — Alternate dispute resolution:** the court may, with the consent of the parties, adopt mediation, conciliation or any other ADR method "with the object of securing expeditious disposal of a case".
- **s. 128(3) — Case management:** the High Court "shall make rules … for case management and scheduling conferences binding the parties to follow the schedules and timelines prescribed therein" (inserted by Act VII of 2020).
- **ss. 35, 35A, 35B — Actual costs, adjournment costs and special costs** (Act XVII of 2017) — see Chapter 9.
- **s. 151 — inherent powers "to be exercised after recording reasons"** (Act VII of 2020).

## 1.5 Procedural law and pending cases

A general rule of interpretation is that **no person has a vested right in a particular procedure**. Therefore a new procedural law usually applies even to pending cases (it is "retrospective" in that sense), unless it takes away a vested right (such as an existing right of appeal) or the law itself says otherwise. A **right of appeal**, however, is a substantive right; it vests in a party on the day the suit is filed and is governed by the law in force on that day.

:::ict Saving clause of 2023 — s. 159 CPC
The Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act 2023 (Act XIX of 2023) substituted **s. 159 (Saving of proceedings)**: *"All proceedings instituted prior to the commencement of the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 2023 … shall be deemed to be the proceedings of, and dealt in accordance with, the provisions of this Code which existed prior to the aforesaid commencement."* The Explanation says "proceedings" includes suit, appeal, review, revision, execution applications and any incidental matter. So in Islamabad, a suit filed before the 2023 Act came into force continues under the pre-2023 provisions. This is an **express exception** to the usual rule that procedural changes apply to pending cases.
:::

## 1.7 Maxims that run through the whole of civil procedure

:::maxim Ubi jus ibi remedium — where there is a right, there is a remedy
The law does not leave a right without a way to enforce it. This maxim is the foundation of **s. 9 CPC**: civil courts have jurisdiction over *all* suits of a civil nature unless a law bars them. A person who has a civil right can always find a court, unless the legislature has clearly sent him elsewhere.
:::

:::maxim Audi alteram partem — hear the other side
No one should be condemned unheard. This is why summons must be served (O. V), why an ex parte decree can be set aside if the summons was not duly served (O. IX r. 13), and why a court must give notice before it adds a party or decides against someone.
:::

:::maxim Actus curiae neminem gravabit — an act of the court shall prejudice no one
If a delay or mistake is caused by the court itself (for example, the file was misplaced, or the court wrongly returned a plaint), the party must not suffer for it. This maxim supports restoration of cases, extension of time (s. 148) and restitution (s. 144).
:::

:::maxim Interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium — it is in the public interest that litigation should end
Courts are a public resource. Endless repetition of the same dispute wastes them. This maxim is the public-policy basis of **res judicata (s. 11)**, **s. 12** and the law of limitation.
:::

:::maxim Nemo debet bis vexari pro una et eadem causa — no one should be vexed twice for the same cause
The private-justice basis of res judicata: a defendant who has already won should not have to defend the same claim again.
:::

:::maxim Lex non cogit ad impossibilia — the law does not compel the impossible
If a party could not possibly comply (for example, a summons was never served, or a natural calamity closed the court), the law excuses him. This supports "sufficient cause" under O. IX rr. 9 and 13 and s. 5 of the Limitation Act.
:::

:::maxim Vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt — the law helps the vigilant, not those who sleep on their rights
A party who is careless and delays loses the help of the law: limitation, dismissal for default (O. IX), O. XVII r. 3, and refusal of discretionary relief.
:::

:::recap Chapter 1 in eight lines
- Substantive law gives rights; procedural law (CPC) is the road to enforce them.
- Procedure is the **handmaid, not the mistress, of justice** — *Imtiaz Ahmad v. Ghulam Ali* (PLD 1963 SC 382).
- But mandatory rules with stated consequences must be applied; deliberate abuse is punished.
- The CPC = 158 sections (principles) + First Schedule of 51 Orders (working rules). Sections prevail over rules.
- High Courts may amend the rules (ss. 121–122); Parliament amends the Code for Islamabad.
- **s. 1(4)** (Islamabad, 2020): primary objective — justice, fairness, ADR, saving time and cost, compliance.
- **s. 159** (Islamabad, 2023): pending proceedings continue under the pre-2023 law.
- Key maxims: *ubi jus ibi remedium*, *audi alteram partem*, *actus curiae neminem gravabit*, *interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium*.
:::

# Chapter 2 · Key definitions (section 2)

Definitions are the vocabulary of the Code. Questions such as "What is a decree? How does it differ from an order and a judgment?" are among the most frequently asked. Each definition below is explained in plain words with its essential elements.

## 2.1 Decree — s. 2(2)

> "'decree' means the formal expression of an adjudication which, so far as regards the Court expressing it, conclusively determines the rights of the parties with regard to all or any of the matters in controversy in the suit and may be either preliminary or final. It shall be deemed to include the rejection of a plaint, the determination of any question within section 144 and an order under rule 60, 98, 99, 101 or 103 of Order XXI, but shall not include— (a) any adjudication from which an appeal lies as an appeal from an order, or (b) any order of dismissal for default."

### The five essential elements of a decree

1. **An adjudication.** The court must have *decided* a matter judicially. A purely administrative act (for example, adjourning a case or sending a file to another room) is not an adjudication.
2. **In a suit.** The adjudication must be given in a *suit*, which begins with a plaint (s. 26). A decision in a proceeding that is not a suit is not a decree, unless a law specially says so (for example, the "deemed decrees" listed in the definition).
3. **Determining the rights of the parties** — the substantive rights in dispute (ownership, possession, money, status), not just procedural rights.
4. **Conclusively** — so far as that court is concerned, the decision is final; the court cannot reopen it (subject to appeal, review or revision).
5. **Formal expression** — the decision must be formally expressed in the manner required (a decree sheet drawn up under O. XX rr. 6–7).

:::eg Spotting a decree
(a) Court decides that Kamran owns House No. 12, G-9/1, and must be given possession: **decree** (final).
(b) Court decides a suit for partition: the shares of each co-owner are declared and a commission is ordered to divide the property: **preliminary decree**. Later, the court approves the actual division by metes and bounds: **final decree**.
(c) Court rejects a plaint because it shows no cause of action (O. VII r. 11): **deemed decree** — appealable as a decree.
(d) Court dismisses the suit because the plaintiff was absent (O. IX r. 8): **not a decree** (dismissal for default is excluded); the remedy is an application to restore under O. IX r. 9.
(e) Court refuses a temporary injunction (O. XXXIX): **order**, not a decree; appeal lies as an appeal from an order (O. XLIII r. 1(r)).
:::

### Kinds of decree

- **Preliminary decree** — "when further proceedings have to be taken before the suit can be completely disposed of" (Explanation). Examples: partition, accounts, mortgage, partnership dissolution, mesne profits (O. XX rr. 12–18).
- **Final decree** — "when such adjudication completely disposes of the suit".
- **Partly preliminary and partly final** — e.g., a decree for possession of land (final part) with an inquiry into mesne profits (preliminary part).
- **Deemed decree** — things the Code *treats* as decrees: (i) rejection of a plaint; (ii) determination of a question under s. 144 (restitution); (iii) orders under O. XXI rr. 60, 98, 99, 101, 103 (objections and resistance in execution).

:::trap Old textbooks and s. 47
Indian books say a decree includes "the determination of any question within section 47". In **Pakistan**, the Law Reforms Ordinance 1972 removed s. 47 from the definition. Orders under s. 47 (questions between parties in execution) are now **appealable as orders** — see s. 104(1)(ff). Do not copy the Indian definition.
:::

## 2.2 Judgment — s. 2(9) and Order — s. 2(14)

> "'judgment' means the statement given by the Judge of the grounds of a decree or order."

> "'order' means the formal expression of any decision of a Civil Court which is not a decree."

- A **judgment** is the *reasoning* — the statement of the grounds. Under O. XX r. 4(2), a judgment of a trial court must contain a concise statement of the case, the points for determination, the decision on them and the **reasons** for the decision.
- A **decree** is the *operative result* in a suit — what the court actually orders, drawn up in a decree sheet.
- An **order** is every other formal decision of a civil court, e.g., an order granting an adjournment, appointing a commission, granting a temporary injunction or allowing amendment.

{w:18,27,27,28}
|Point|Judgment|Decree|Order|
|Meaning|Statement of the **grounds** of a decree or order|Formal expression of an adjudication that **conclusively determines rights** in a suit|Formal expression of any decision that is **not a decree**|
|Where|In every decided suit and many applications|Only in a **suit** (or deemed decree)|In suits, appeals, applications and miscellaneous proceedings|
|Number|One judgment|Usually one (preliminary + final possible)|Many orders in one suit|
|Appeal|No appeal against "a judgment" as such; appeal is against the decree/order|First appeal **always** lies unless barred (s. 96); second appeal on law (s. 100)|Appeal only if listed in **s. 104 or O. XLIII r. 1**; otherwise revision (s. 115) or challenge in appeal from decree (s. 105)|
|Example|Reasons why the suit is decreed|"Suit decreed for Rs. 2 million with costs"|"Interim injunction granted"; "plaint returned"|

:::why Why does the distinction matter?
Because the **remedy** depends on it. If a decision is a decree, a first appeal lies as of right under s. 96, and the full record is re-examined. If it is an order, an appeal lies only if the law specifically lists it (s. 104, O. XLIII r. 1); otherwise the aggrieved party must use revision (s. 115) or raise the point in an appeal against the final decree (s. 105). Choosing the wrong remedy wastes time and can make you lose limitation.
:::

## 2.3 Other important definitions

:::eg Intermeddler — a favourite short note (LHC 2017)
Rashid dies leaving a shop in Blue Area. His nephew Faisal, who is not an heir, takes the keys, runs the shop and collects rent from a sub-tenant. A creditor of Rashid sues to recover a debt from the estate. Faisal can be impleaded as Rashid's **legal representative**, because s. 2(11) includes "any person who intermeddles with the estate of the deceased". He is liable only to the extent of the estate that came into his hands — not personally (see s. 52 CPC).
:::

:::eg Mesne profits
Usman wrongfully occupies Zainab's flat for 2 years. The flat could have been let at Rs. 60,000 per month. Zainab can claim mesne profits of about Rs. 1,440,000 with interest, even if Usman lived there himself and actually earned nothing, because mesne profits include what he "might with ordinary diligence have received". If Usman added a new kitchen and the rental value rose, the extra rent due to *his* improvement is excluded.
:::

:::recap Chapter 2 in seven lines
- **Decree** = formal expression + adjudication + in a suit + conclusively determines rights in controversy. Preliminary, final, partly both; deemed decrees.
- Rejection of plaint, s. 144 questions and O. XXI rr. 60, 98, 99, 101, 103 orders are **deemed decrees**; dismissal for default and appealable orders are **not decrees**.
- In Pakistan, s. 47 questions are **not** decrees (removed in 1972; appeal under s. 104(1)(ff)).
- **Judgment** = grounds; **order** = any formal decision that is not a decree.
- Decree → first appeal as of right (s. 96); order → appeal only if listed (s. 104 / O. XLIII), else revision.
- **Legal representative** includes an **intermeddler**.
- **Mesne profits** = actual or reasonably obtainable profits of wrongful possession + interest − profits from the possessor's own improvements.
:::
