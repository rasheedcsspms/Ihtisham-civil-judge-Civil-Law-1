# Chapter 3 · The civil courts of Islamabad (Civil Courts Ordinance 1962)

## 3.1 Why this law matters

The CPC tells us *how* a civil court works. It does not create the courts. The courts — District Judges, Additional District Judges and Civil Judges — are created and given their powers by the **West Pakistan Civil Courts Ordinance 1962** (W.P. Ordinance II of 1962). The CPC itself now says so for Islamabad:

> "3. Subordination of Courts. For the purposes of this Code, the hierarchy and sub-ordination of Civil Courts shall be the same as prescribed in the West Pakistan Civil Courts Ordinance, 1962 (W.P. Ordinance No. II of 1962)." (s. 3 CPC, substituted by Act VII of 2020)

The Ordinance is short (28 sections) and is fully examinable as a separate item of the syllabus. KP High Court's 2026 Civil Law-I paper (same syllabus as IHC) asked two questions on it: *"What are the different types of jurisdictions of civil courts, and how are these exercised?"* and *"Explain the concept and forums of different civil appeals briefly as provided in the Civil Courts Ordinance, 1962."*

:::ict How a West Pakistan law applies in Islamabad
The Ordinance was made in 1962 for the Province of West Pakistan, which then included the area that is now the Islamabad Capital Territory. When One Unit was dissolved in 1970, existing laws continued in force in each area. For the ICT, the Ordinance continues as a **federal law** and is amended by **Parliament** — the most recent example being the **Civil Courts (Amendment) Act 2025 (Act XV of 2025)**, which substituted s. 18(1) "in its application to the Islamabad Capital Territory". The Punjab now calls its own version the "Punjab Civil Courts Ordinance 1962" and amends it separately; those amendments do not apply in Islamabad.
:::

## 3.2 Classes of civil courts — s. 3

> "3. Classes of Courts. Besides the Courts of Small Causes established under the Provincial Small Cause Courts Act, 1887, and the Courts established under any other enactment for the time being in force, there shall be the following classes of Civil Courts, namely:— (a) the Court of the District Judge; (b) the Court of the Additional District Judge; and (c) the Court of the Civil Judge."

So there are **three classes** of ordinary civil courts. Courts created by special laws (Family Courts, Rent Controllers, Banking Courts, Consumer Courts and so on) are *in addition* to these.

## 3.3 Section by section

### Civil districts — s. 4

Government may, by notification, divide the area into **civil districts**, fix their limits and determine their headquarters. The Islamabad High Court Act 2010, s. 6(4), provides for **two Sessions Divisions** in the ICT — Islamabad **East** and Islamabad **West** — each with its own District & Sessions Judge.

### District Judges — s. 5

Government shall, **in consultation with the High Court**, appoint as many District Judges as it thinks necessary and post one to each district. One person may be District Judge of two or more districts.

### Additional District Judges — s. 6

> "(2) An Additional District Judge shall discharge such functions of a District Judge as the District Judge may assign to him, and in the discharge of those functions he shall exercise the same powers as the District Judge."

An ADJ has **no independent jurisdiction of his own**. He exercises the powers of the District Judge in the cases the District Judge assigns to him. That is why an appeal "to the District Judge" can be heard by an ADJ if the District Judge assigns it (see also s. 18(2)).

### Original jurisdiction of District Judges — s. 7

> "Except as otherwise provided by any enactment for the time being in force, the Court of the District Judge shall have jurisdiction in original civil suits without limit as regards the value."

The District Judge is the **principal civil court of original jurisdiction** — the "District Court" of s. 2(4) CPC. In practice, however, most suits are filed before Civil Judges because of **s. 15 CPC**: every suit must be instituted "in the Court of the lowest grade competent to try it".

### Civil Judges — s. 8

Government, in consultation with the High Court, fixes the number of Civil Judges, makes rules for their recruitment (the very examination you are sitting) and appoints them.

### Pecuniary limits of Civil Judges — s. 9

> "The jurisdiction to be exercised in original civil suits as regards the value by any person appointed to be a Civil Judge shall be determined by the High Court either by including him in a class or otherwise as it thinks fit."

The Ordinance itself fixes **no money limit** for a Civil Judge. The **High Court** fixes it by notification — traditionally by placing judges in classes (Civil Judge 1st, 2nd or 3rd Class), each with a value limit. A Civil Judge may also be given unlimited pecuniary jurisdiction (this is the "Senior Civil Judge" in practice).

:::ict Pecuniary jurisdiction in Islamabad — the moving story of s. 6 CPC
- The Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act 2020 (Act VII of 2020) changed s. 6 CPC for Islamabad and tied the forum to the **value of the suit** (with a threshold of Rs. 50 million). The IHC itself issued a notice that, as a result of the 2020 Act, its own pecuniary jurisdiction in original civil matters had changed.
- The Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act 2023 (Act XIX of 2023) then **substituted s. 6 again** in its general form: *"Save in so far as is otherwise expressly provided, nothing herein contained shall operate to give any Court jurisdiction over suits the amount or value of the subject matter of which exceeds the pecuniary limits, if any, of its ordinary jurisdiction."* Section 7 stands omitted.
- **Result today:** the money limits of Civil Judges in Islamabad are those fixed by the **Islamabad High Court under s. 9 of the Ordinance**. District Judges have unlimited value jurisdiction (s. 7). Before you sit the paper, check the latest IHC notification on Civil Judges' powers; in your answer, state the legal mechanism (s. 9 Ordinance + s. 6 CPC), which is what the examiner is testing.
:::

### Local limits — s. 10

The High Court defines the local limits of each Civil Judge. If it posts a Civil Judge to a district without saying more, **the whole district** is his local limit.

### Small Cause Court powers — s. 11

The High Court may, by notification, confer on any Civil Judge the jurisdiction of a Judge of a **Court of Small Causes** (under the Provincial Small Cause Courts Act 1887) for suits up to a value "not exceeding one thousand rupees". (A historic figure; small claims today are mostly dealt with under special laws.)

### Succession Act proceedings — s. 12

Notwithstanding the Succession Act 1925 (where succession certificates and letters of administration are District Court work), the High Court may authorise a **Civil Judge** to take cognizance of such proceedings, or a District Judge may transfer them to a Civil Judge. The District Judge can withdraw them back, except those specifically transferred by the High Court. This is why **succession certificate** petitions are heard by Civil Judges in Islamabad.

### Places of sitting — s. 13

The High Court may fix the place where any court is held — even **outside** its local limits. Otherwise a court may sit anywhere within its limits.

### Control of courts — s. 14

> "(1) All Civil Courts in the areas to which this Ordinance extends shall be subordinate to the High Court, and, subject to the general superintendence and control of the High Court, the District Judge shall have control over all Civil Courts within the local limits of his jurisdiction. (2) The posting of Civil Judges shall be made by the High Court."

This is the **chain of control**: High Court → District Judge → Civil Judges. It is matched by Article 203 of the Constitution (each High Court shall supervise and control all courts subordinate to it).

### Distribution of business — s. 15

> "Notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, every District Judge may by written order direct that any civil business cognizable by his Court and the Courts under his control shall be distributed among such Courts in such manner as he thinks fit: Provided that no direction … shall empower any Court to exercise any powers or deal with any business beyond the limits of its jurisdiction."

The District Judge is the **administrative head** of the district. He decides which court gets which kind of case (for example, by a "distribution order" assigning family cases, rent cases or succession cases to particular courts). But he cannot, by a distribution order, *create* jurisdiction that a court does not have.

### Delegation by District Judges — s. 16

With the **previous sanction of the High Court**, a District Judge may delegate to a Civil Judge, for a specified part of the district, his powers under ss. 14 and 15 of the Ordinance and the powers of the District Court under **s. 24 CPC** (transfer and withdrawal).

## 3.4 Jurisdiction in civil appeals — ss. 17 and 18

### Appeals from District Judges and ADJs — s. 17

> "17. (1) Save as otherwise provided by any enactment for the time being in force, an appeal from a decree or order of a District Judge or Additional District Judge exercising original jurisdiction shall lie to the High Court. (2) An appeal shall not lie to the High Court from a decree or order of an Additional District Judge in any case in which, if the decree or order had been made by the District Judge, an appeal would not lie to that Court."

### Appeals from Civil Judges — s. 18 (as amended for Islamabad in 2025)

:::ict Section 18(1) after the Civil Courts (Amendment) Act 2025 (Act XV of 2025)
> "18. Appeals from Civil Judges. [Save as aforesaid, an appeal from a decree or order of a Civil Judge shall lie to the District Judge.]"

The footnote in the official text reads: *"Subs. by Act No. XV of 2025, s. 2 (Its application to the Islamabad Capital Territory)."* Before this amendment, s. 18(1) split appeals from Civil Judges by **value** — high-value appeals went to the High Court and the rest to the District Judge. In Islamabad, **all** appeals from decrees and appealable orders of Civil Judges now lie to the **District Judge**, whatever the value. Following the amendment, the IHC transferred pending appeals of this kind to the District Judges (IHC notices refer to a Division Bench decision of 23.06.2025 in Civil Reference No. 01/2025).

This is a fresh change. Expect a question such as *"Explain the forums of civil appeals under the Civil Courts Ordinance 1962 as applicable to Islamabad."*
:::

> "(2) Where the function of receiving any appeals which lie to the District Judge under the last preceding sub-section has been assigned to an Additional District Judge, the appeal may be preferred to the Additional District Judge. (3) The High Court may, by notification, direct that appeals lying to the District Judge from all or any of the decrees or orders passed in any original suit by any Civil Judge shall be referred to such other Civil Judge as may be mentioned in the notification, and the appeals shall thereupon be preferred accordingly, and the Court of such Civil Judge shall be deemed to be a District Court for the purposes of all appeals so preferred."

:::eg Can a Civil Judge hear an appeal? (LHC General Law 2023)
Question: *"Is it lawful for a Civil Judge to act as District Court for the purposes of appeal(s) preferred against the decree/order passed in any original suit by any Civil Judge?"*
Answer: **Yes, but only under s. 18(3).** Ordinarily appeals from a Civil Judge lie to the District Judge (s. 18(1)) or to an ADJ to whom the function is assigned (s. 18(2)). However, the **High Court, by notification**, may direct that such appeals be referred to *another Civil Judge* named in the notification. That Civil Judge's court is then "deemed to be a District Court" for those appeals. Without such a notification, a Civil Judge has no appellate jurisdiction, and any appeal decided by him would be **coram non judice** (before a judge without jurisdiction) and void.
:::

## 3.5 Supplementary provisions — ss. 19 to 28

- **s. 19 — Continuance of powers:** an officer invested with powers in one area keeps them when posted to an equal or higher office in a like area, unless the High Court directs otherwise.
- **s. 20 — Petition-writers:** the High Court, in consultation with the Board of Revenue, may make rules licensing petition-writers.
- **s. 21 — Temporary vacancy of the District Judge:** on death or illness of the District Judge, the ADJ (or, if none, the **senior-most Civil Judge** at headquarters) takes charge for urgent work — filing of suits and appeals, receiving pleadings, execution of processes — and is designated "ADJ / Civil Judge in charge of the district".
- **s. 22 — Delegation when the District Judge is away** on tour or leave: the same officers may be given his emergent duties.
- **s. 23 — Temporary vacancy of a Civil Judge:** the District Judge may empower another Civil Judge to do the work of the vacant court; the registers of the two courts must be kept **separately**.
- **s. 24 — Suits against the State and public officers** (see 3.6 below).
- **s. 25 — Holidays:** the High Court prepares the list of court holidays (published in the Gazette). Read with O. XVII r. 4 CPC: if a date falls on a holiday, parties must appear on the next working day.
- **s. 26 — Seal of the court:** circular, two inches, with the court's title in English and Urdu and a crescent and star.
- **s. 27 — Rules:** the High Court, with the approval of Government, may frame rules (supervision and inspection, sittings, process fees, pleaders' fees, record-keeping, copies).
- **s. 28 — Repeal and savings:** repealed the Punjab Courts Act 1918, Sind Courts Act 1926, NWFP Courts Regulation and others; pending suits stood transferred to the competent court; old Subordinate Judges became Civil Judges.

## 3.6 Suits against Pakistan, a Province or a public officer — s. 24

> "24. (1) No Civil Court not having jurisdiction in original suits without limit as regards value and no Court of Small Causes shall receive, entertain or register any suit in which Pakistan or any of its Provinces or any public officer as defined in clause (17) of section 2 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, in his official capacity is a party. (2) In every such case the plaintiff shall be referred to the Court of the Civil Judge having jurisdiction in original suits without limit as regard value and such suit shall be instituted only in the Court of such Civil Judge and shall be heard at the headquarters of the district."

### In plain words

If **the Federation, a Province or a public officer in his official capacity** is a party, the suit can be filed **only** before a **Civil Judge with unlimited pecuniary jurisdiction**, and it must be heard at **district headquarters**. Ordinary Civil Judges with limited powers cannot even register such a suit — they must "refer" the plaintiff to the right court.

### Exceptions — s. 24(3)

The section does **not** apply merely because a public officer is a party in his capacity as (a) a member of a local authority, or (b) a curator, guardian, manager or representative of a private person or estate (under O. XXXII r. 4(4) CPC, s. 195 Succession Act, the Lunacy Act, ss. 7, 18 or 42 Guardians and Wards Act, or Court of Wards laws). It also does not apply to suits about the affairs of a Government Railway.

:::why Why s. 24?
Suits against the State often involve public money, policy and large values. The law wants them tried by **senior, experienced judges** at a central place, where the Government's law officers are available. It also avoids conflicting decisions by many small courts on the same government action.
:::

:::tip Link to s. 80 CPC
A suit against the Government or a public officer for an act done in his official capacity also needs a **two-month notice under s. 80 CPC** before filing (Topic 2). In a problem question, mention both: the **forum** (s. 24 Ordinance) and the **pre-condition** (s. 80 CPC).
:::

## 3.7 The Islamabad High Court Act 2010 — the top of the pyramid

- **s. 3:** the IHC consists of a Chief Justice and other Judges (now **twelve** other Judges after Act XXXII of 2024); principal seat at Islamabad.
- **s. 4 — Jurisdiction:** the IHC has, in respect of the ICT, "original, appellate, revisional and other jurisdiction, as under the Constitution or the laws in force immediately before the commencement of this Act, is exercisable in respect of the said territory by the Lahore High Court" (as substituted in 2016).
- **s. 6 — Other courts:** all civil, criminal and revenue courts, tribunals and special courts in the ICT continue, and from the appointed date fall under the **jurisdiction, supervision and control of the IHC**; the IHC was to establish a separate subordinate judiciary for the ICT (the service you are joining); there are **two Sessions Divisions**.
- **s. 8 — Practice and procedure:** laws and rules on practice and procedure applicable to the **Sindh High Court** and courts subordinate to it (including the Sindh Chief Court Rules) apply *mutatis mutandis* to the IHC and its subordinate courts, until varied by the IHC's own rules.
- **s. 9:** cases about the ICT pending in the Lahore High Court (including its Rawalpindi Bench) stood transferred to the IHC.

:::recap Chapter 3 in ten lines
- Three classes of civil courts: **District Judge, Additional District Judge, Civil Judge** (s. 3). Section 3 CPC (Islamabad, 2020) adopts this hierarchy.
- **ADJ** exercises the District Judge's powers in matters assigned to him (s. 6).
- **District Judge**: original jurisdiction **without limit as to value** (s. 7).
- **Civil Judge**: value limits fixed by the **High Court** (s. 9); local limits by the High Court (s. 10).
- High Court may give Civil Judges Small Cause powers (s. 11) and Succession Act work (s. 12).
- District Judge **controls** and **distributes business** among courts but cannot create jurisdiction (ss. 14–15).
- Appeals: DJ/ADJ original → **High Court** (s. 17). Civil Judge → **District Judge** in all cases in Islamabad (s. 18(1), **Act XV of 2025**); ADJ if assigned (s. 18(2)); notified Civil Judge (s. 18(3)).
- Suits against **Pakistan, a Province or a public officer (official capacity)** → only a **Civil Judge with unlimited value jurisdiction**, at district headquarters (s. 24).
- IHC Act 2010: IHC has LHC's former jurisdiction over ICT (s. 4); controls ICT courts (s. 6); Sindh practice and procedure laws apply (s. 8).
- Remember s. 15 CPC: sue in the **lowest grade** competent court.

# Chapter 4 · Jurisdiction

## 4.1 What is jurisdiction?

**Jurisdiction** is the *legal authority of a court to hear and decide a case*. The word comes from Latin *juris* (law) + *dictio* (saying): the power "to say what the law is" in a given case. A court that decides a case without jurisdiction acts without authority, and its decision is a **nullity** — it binds no one and can be challenged at any stage, even in execution or in collateral proceedings.

:::case Kiran Singh v. Chaman Paswan (AIR 1954 SC 340)
__Facts.__ A suit was undervalued and filed in a lower court; the appeal therefore went to the District Court instead of the High Court. The losing party later argued that the whole appellate decree was void.

__Held.__ It is a fundamental principle that a decree passed by a court without jurisdiction is a **nullity**, and its invalidity can be set up whenever and wherever it is sought to be enforced — even in execution or collateral proceedings. A defect of jurisdiction, whether pecuniary, territorial or of subject matter, strikes at the very authority of the court. **However**, objections to *territorial* and *pecuniary* jurisdiction stand on a different footing: by statute (s. 21 CPC; s. 11 of the Suits Valuation Act 1887) they cannot be raised in appeal or revision unless taken at the earliest stage *and* there has been a consequent failure of justice.

__Ratio.__ (1) Lack of **inherent** (subject-matter) jurisdiction makes a decree void; consent, waiver or acquiescence cannot cure it. (2) Objections about **place of suing** and **valuation** are technical; the legislature has decided that they shall not upset a decree on the merits unless they caused real injustice.
:::

:::maxim Consensus facit jus? No — consent cannot confer jurisdiction
Parties cannot, by agreement, give a court jurisdiction that the law has not given it. *Example:* parties agree that their family dispute shall be decided by an ordinary Civil Judge instead of the Family Court; the Civil Judge still has no jurisdiction, because the Family Courts Act gives exclusive jurisdiction to the Family Court. **But** where two or more courts *both* have territorial jurisdiction, parties may agree to sue in only one of them — that is not conferring jurisdiction; it is choosing among courts that already have it.
:::

## 4.2 Kinds of jurisdiction

## 4.3 Pecuniary jurisdiction — s. 6 CPC

> "Save in so far as is otherwise expressly provided, nothing herein contained shall operate to give any Court jurisdiction over suits the amount or value of the subject matter of which exceeds the pecuniary limits, if any, of its ordinary jurisdiction." (s. 6, as substituted by Act XIX of 2023)

### Key points

1. Each court can try suits only **up to its money limit**. The CPC does not fix these limits; the Civil Courts Ordinance and High Court notifications do (Chapter 3).
2. The **plaintiff's valuation** in the plaint ordinarily decides jurisdiction (O. VII r. 1(i)). The court will accept it unless it is clearly arbitrary or deliberately inflated / deflated to choose a court.
3. If the court finds the suit is *undervalued*, it asks the plaintiff to correct the valuation (O. VII r. 11(b)); if the corrected value is beyond its limit, it **returns** the plaint (O. VII r. 10).
4. Objections to over- or under-valuation are **not** allowed in appeal unless raised at the first opportunity and unless they caused prejudice on the merits (s. 11 Suits Valuation Act 1887; principle in *Kiran Singh*).
5. **Set-off** cannot exceed the pecuniary limits of the court (O. VIII r. 6).

## 4.4 Section 9 — Courts to try all civil suits unless barred

> "9. The Courts shall (subject to the provisions herein contained) have jurisdiction to try all suits of a civil nature excepting suits of which their cognizance is either expressly or impliedly barred.
> Explanation.— A suit in which the right to property or to an office is contested is a suit of a civil nature, notwithstanding that such right may depend entirely on the decision of questions as to religious rites or ceremonies."

This is the **charter of civil court jurisdiction** and one of the most important sections of the Code (asked in LHC ASJ 2020: *"Courts to try all civil suits unless barred. Discuss all aspects."*).

### Two conditions

A civil court has jurisdiction under s. 9 if:

1. the suit is **of a civil nature**; and
2. its cognizance is **not barred**, either expressly or impliedly.

### (a) "Suit of a civil nature"

A suit is of a civil nature if the **principal question** in it relates to the determination of a **civil right** — a private right concerning property, office, status, contract or tort — and its enforcement. The word "civil" is used in contrast to criminal, political or purely religious/social matters.

- **Civil nature:** right to property; right to an office (e.g., a mutawalli, an imam's office carrying fees, a trustee); right to worship in a public mosque; right to vote; right to salary; right to damages; right to fees or offerings attached to an office; specific performance; declaration of title; injunction; recovery of money.
- **Not civil nature:** purely religious rites with no right to property or office attached (e.g., a suit merely to declare who should lead prayers on a particular day, where no office or emolument is involved); questions of caste dignity or social honour alone; political questions; matters of pure honour.

The **Explanation** says that a suit about a right to property or office remains a civil suit *even if* the decision depends entirely on religious rites or ceremonies. So the court will decide a religious question if it is necessary to decide a civil right (for example, whether a person was validly appointed mutawalli according to the founder's religious conditions).

:::case Most Rev. P.M.A. Metropolitan v. Moran Mar Marthoma (AIR 1995 SC 2001)
__Held.__ The expansive language of s. 9 — "shall … have jurisdiction to try all suits of a civil nature" — means that the court is **bound** to entertain every civil suit unless it is barred. The word "civil" pertains to private rights and remedies of a citizen, as distinguished from criminal, political etc. The Explanation was added to make clear that even where the dispute about property or office depends on religious questions, the civil court must decide it.

__Ratio.__ "Civil nature" is wider than "civil proceeding". The test is whether a **civil right** is the principal subject matter. Once it is, the court has a duty — not merely a discretion — to try the suit.
:::

:::case Ganga Bai v. Vijay Kumar (AIR 1974 SC 1126)
__Held.__ There is an **inherent right** in every person to bring a suit of a civil nature and, unless the suit is barred by statute, one may, at one's peril, bring a suit of one's choice. It is no answer to a suit, howsoever frivolous the claim, that the law confers no such right to sue. A suit for its maintainability requires no authority of law; it is enough that no statute bars it.

__Ratio.__ The right to sue is the **rule**; the bar is the **exception** and must be shown by the person who asserts it.
:::

### (b) "Expressly or impliedly barred"

**Express bar** — a statute *says in words* that civil courts shall not entertain a matter. Examples: s. 11 of the West Pakistan Land Revenue Act 1967 (matters for revenue officers); s. 5 read with the Schedule of the Family Courts Act 1964 (exclusive jurisdiction of the Family Court); finality clauses in special laws ("no order under this Act shall be called in question in any court").

**Implied bar** — no words of exclusion, but the scheme of a statute shows the legislature meant the matter to be decided only by the special forum. Typical signs: the statute **creates a new right** (not existing at common law) and in the same statute provides a **complete machinery** (forum + appeal + revision) to enforce it; or the subject is against **public policy** for civil courts (e.g., a suit to enforce a gambling debt, or a suit about an act of State).

:::why Why the presumption in favour of civil court jurisdiction?
1. *Ubi jus ibi remedium*: every right needs a remedy; civil courts are the ordinary guardians of rights.
2. Article 10A of the Constitution (fair trial and due process) and the general principle of access to justice.
3. Special tribunals are creatures of statute with limited powers; they can make mistakes outside their power. Someone must be able to correct such *jurisdictional* errors — that "someone" is the civil court (or the High Court in writ jurisdiction).
4. Therefore **exclusion of civil court jurisdiction is not readily inferred**; the person claiming the bar must prove it, and statutes ousting jurisdiction are **strictly construed**.
:::

:::case Secretary of State v. Mask & Co. (AIR 1940 PC 105)
__Held.__ The exclusion of the jurisdiction of civil courts is not to be readily inferred; such exclusion must be either explicitly expressed or clearly implied. Even where jurisdiction is excluded, the civil courts have jurisdiction to examine into cases where the provisions of the Act have **not been complied with**, or the statutory tribunal has **not acted in conformity with the fundamental principles of judicial procedure**.

__Ratio.__ Ouster clauses protect only orders made **within** the statute. An order outside the statute is not protected.
:::

:::case Dhulabhai v. State of Madhya Pradesh (AIR 1969 SC 78)
The Indian Supreme Court (Hidayatullah C.J.) laid down **seven propositions** on the exclusion of civil court jurisdiction. In short:
1. Where a statute gives **finality** to a special tribunal's orders, civil court jurisdiction is excluded **if there is an adequate remedy** to do what the civil courts would normally do; but the civil court can still examine cases where the provisions of the Act were not complied with or the tribunal did not follow the fundamental principles of judicial procedure.
2. Where there is an **express bar**, examine the scheme of the Act to see whether the remedies provided are adequate.
3. Challenge to the **vires** (constitutionality) of a provision cannot be brought before the tribunal under that very Act; a civil suit (or writ) may lie.
4. Where a provision is already declared unconstitutional, a suit lies for refund etc.
5. Where the statute creates a **special right and a special remedy**, the remedy in that statute is generally exclusive.
6. Questions of correctness of assessment, apart from constitutionality, are for the authorities under the Act.
7. Exclusion is **not readily inferred** unless the above conditions apply.
:::

### The Pakistani position on ouster clauses

The superior courts of Pakistan follow the same approach. Even when a special statute contains a clause excluding civil court jurisdiction, the civil court **retains jurisdiction** to examine whether the special authority acted:

1. **without jurisdiction** (coram non judice) — e.g., an officer not authorised by the Act made the order;
2. **mala fide** (in bad faith) — e.g., to harass or for a collateral purpose;
3. in **violation of the principles of natural justice** — e.g., without notice or hearing;
4. **not in accordance with the Act** at all — an order that the statute does not authorise.

:::case Abbasia Cooperative Bank v. Hakeem Hafiz Muhammad Ghaus (PLD 1997 SC 3)
__Held.__ The Supreme Court of Pakistan held that where the jurisdiction of a civil court is ousted by a statute, the ouster is not absolute: the civil court can still examine the acts or orders of the special forum if they are **without jurisdiction, mala fide or coram non judice**. Ouster clauses protect only acts done **under** the statute and within its terms; they cannot protect acts which are a nullity in law.

__Ratio.__ An order that is void (made without jurisdiction, in bad faith, or by a forum not properly constituted) is no order "under the Act" and is therefore outside the protection of an ouster clause; the civil court's general jurisdiction under s. 9 revives to that extent.
:::

:::eg Applying s. 9 — five quick cases
1. **Rent dispute in Islamabad** — eviction of a tenant from a shop in F-10 Markaz: the Islamabad Rent Restriction Ordinance 2001 gives exclusive jurisdiction to the **Rent Controller**; a civil suit for eviction is **impliedly / expressly barred**.
2. **Dower and maintenance** — Family Courts Act 1964 (Schedule) makes the **Family Court** exclusive; a civil suit is barred.
3. **Correction of mutation** in revenue record — matters for revenue officers under the Land Revenue Act; but a suit for **declaration of title** to land lies in the civil court (title is a civil right).
4. **Order of an authority cancelling an allotment without notice** — even if the statute says its orders are final, a civil suit lies because the order violates natural justice (*Mask & Co.*; *Abbasia Cooperative Bank*).
5. **Suit to declare that the plaintiff is the rightful imam of a mosque** with salary and residence attached: civil nature (right to an **office**), even though the question depends on religious qualifications (Explanation to s. 9).
:::

:::trap Common mistake
Do not say "the civil court has no jurisdiction because a special law exists". You must show **either** an express bar in words **or** a clear implied bar from the scheme of the special law, **and** that the act complained of was done **within** that special law. Then discuss the exceptions (without jurisdiction / mala fide / natural justice).
:::

## 4.5 Place of suing — ss. 15 to 20

Sections 15–20 answer two questions: **which grade** of court (s. 15), and **which place** (ss. 16–20).

### Lowest grade — s. 15

> "15. Every suit shall be instituted in the Court of the lowest grade competent to try it."

This is a rule of **procedure** for the convenience of courts (to keep higher courts free); it does **not** take away the jurisdiction of a higher court. If a suit is filed in a higher court which also has jurisdiction, the decree is not void; the court should **return** the plaint for presentation to the lower court, but a decree passed by it is not a nullity.

### Immovable property — ss. 16 to 18

> "16. Subject to the pecuniary or other limitations prescribed by any law, suits— (a) for the recovery of immoveable property with or without rent or profits, (b) for the partition of immoveable property, (c) for foreclosure, sale or redemption in the case of a mortgage of or charge upon immoveable property, (d) for the determination of any other right to or interest in immoveable property, (e) for compensation for wrong to immoveable property, (f) for the recovery of moveable property actually under distraint or attachment, shall be instituted in the Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the property is situate, or, in the case of suits referred to in clause (c), at the place where the cause of action has wholly or partly arisen: Provided that a suit to obtain relief respecting, or compensation for wrong to, immoveable property held by or on behalf of the defendant may, where the relief sought can be entirely obtained through his personal obedience, be instituted either in the Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the property is situate … or in the Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the defendant actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain."

**Rule:** suits about land go where the land is (*forum rei sitae*). The Pakistani text adds that **mortgage suits (clause (c))** may also be filed where the cause of action arose (added in 1962).

**Proviso:** if the relief can be obtained entirely through the **defendant's personal obedience** (e.g., specific performance of an agreement to sell, where the court orders the defendant to execute a sale deed; or an injunction restraining him personally), the suit may also be filed where the defendant resides or works. The rationale is the equitable maxim *aequitas agit in personam* — equity acts on the person.

- **s. 17 — Property in several jurisdictions:** the suit may be filed in any court within whose limits **any portion** of the property is situate, if the entire claim is within its pecuniary limits.
- **s. 18 — Uncertain limits:** if it is uncertain in which of two courts the property lies, either court may record a statement of uncertainty and proceed; its decree is valid. If no statement was recorded, an appellate or revisional court will not entertain the objection unless there was no reasonable ground for uncertainty **and** a failure of justice.

### Wrongs to the person or movables — s. 19

> "19. Where a suit is for compensation for wrong done to the person or to moveable property, if the wrong was done within the local limits of the jurisdiction of one Court and the defendant resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain, within the local limits of the jurisdiction of another Court, the suit may be instituted at the option of the plaintiff in either of the said Courts."

*Illustrations in the Code:* A, residing in Karachi, beats B in Quetta — B may sue in Quetta or Karachi. A, residing in Karachi, publishes in Quetta statements defamatory of B — B may sue in Quetta or Karachi.

### All other suits — s. 20 (the residuary rule)

> "20. Subject to the limitations aforesaid, every suit shall be instituted in a Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction— (a) the defendant, or each of the defendants where there are more than one, at the time of the commencement of the suit, actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain; or (b) any of the defendants, where there are more than one, … provided that in such case either the leave of the Court is given, or the defendants who do not reside … acquiesce in such institution; or (c) the cause of action, wholly or in part, arises."

- **Explanation I:** a person with a permanent home in one place and a temporary residence in another is deemed to reside at **both** places for a cause of action arising at the temporary residence.
- **Explanation II:** a **corporation** is deemed to carry on business at its **sole or principal office** in Pakistan, or, for a cause of action arising where it has a **subordinate office**, at that place.

:::eg s. 20 in practice (LLB 2008: "defendant resides in Karachi, cause of action arose at Lahore — which court?")
Nadeem (Islamabad) sells cloth to Tariq (Karachi). The contract was made in Lahore, delivery was in Lahore and payment was due in Islamabad. Tariq does not pay. Nadeem may sue: (i) at **Karachi**, where the defendant resides (s. 20(a)); or (ii) at **Lahore**, where the contract was made and delivery took place (part of the cause of action, s. 20(c)); or (iii) arguably at **Islamabad**, where payment was due (non-payment at the place of payment is part of the cause of action). The plaintiff, as *dominus litis* (master of the suit), chooses among the courts that have jurisdiction.
:::

:::maxim Dominus litis — the plaintiff is the master of the suit
The plaintiff chooses whom to sue, for what relief and (among competent courts) where. The court will not force him to sue a person he does not want to sue — but if a **necessary party** is missing, the suit may fail (Chapter 10). His choice of forum among several competent courts is subject to s. 22 (transfer) and to the bar on forum-shopping by abuse.
:::

:::eg Cause of action — what it means
**Cause of action** is the bundle of essential facts that the plaintiff must prove, if traversed, to get a judgment in his favour. It does not include the evidence. In a suit on a contract, the cause of action includes: the making of the contract, its terms, the plaintiff's performance, the defendant's breach and the resulting loss. Any court where **any part** of this bundle arose has jurisdiction under s. 20(c).
:::

### Agreements choosing one of several competent courts

If two or more courts have jurisdiction, the parties may validly agree that suits shall be filed **only in one of them** (an exclusive jurisdiction clause). That is not against s. 28 of the Contract Act, because it does not *oust* jurisdiction absolutely — it only restricts the choice. But parties **cannot** agree to confer jurisdiction on a court that has none, because consent cannot confer jurisdiction.

## 4.6 Objections to jurisdiction — s. 21 and s. 99

> "21. No objection as to the place of suing shall be allowed by any appellate or revisional Court unless such objection was taken in the Court of first instance at the earliest possible opportunity and in all cases where issues are settled at or before such settlement, and unless there has been a consequent failure of justice."

Three conditions must **all** be met before an appellate or revisional court will entertain an objection about **territorial** jurisdiction:

1. the objection was taken in the **trial court**;
2. at the **earliest possible opportunity**, and in any case **at or before the settlement of issues**; and
3. there has been a **consequent failure of justice**.

Section 99 adds: no decree shall be reversed or substantially varied, nor any case remanded, in appeal on account of any misjoinder of parties or causes of action or any error, defect or irregularity in any proceedings, **not affecting the merits of the case or the jurisdiction of the Court**.

:::why Why are territorial objections treated as "technical"?
Territorial rules exist for the convenience of parties and witnesses — not to protect any public interest. A defendant who keeps quiet, takes his chances on the merits and loses, should not be allowed to undo the whole trial by suddenly pointing out that the suit should have been filed 50 kilometres away. Hence s. 21 treats the objection as **waived** unless raised early and unless it caused real injustice. Contrast **subject-matter** jurisdiction, which cannot be waived because it concerns the legislature's allocation of judicial power.
:::

{w:26,37,37}
|Point|Territorial / pecuniary defect|Subject-matter (inherent) defect|
|Effect on decree|Not void; at most irregular|**Void, a nullity**|
|Can parties waive / consent?|Yes, by not objecting in time|**No** — consent cannot confer jurisdiction|
|When can it be raised?|Trial court, at earliest opportunity, before issues (s. 21; s. 11 SVA)|**At any stage**, even in appeal, execution or collateral proceedings|

:::recap Chapter 4 in ten lines
- Jurisdiction = legal authority to decide; decree without (inherent) jurisdiction is a **nullity** (*Kiran Singh*).
- Kinds: subject-matter, pecuniary, territorial, original, appellate, revisional, exclusive / concurrent.
- s. 6 (2023): no court may exceed its pecuniary limits; limits come from the Ordinance and High Court.
- **s. 9:** civil courts try all **suits of a civil nature** unless **expressly or impliedly barred**; Explanation — religious questions do not take away civil nature where property or office is in issue.
- Right to sue is the rule; bar is the exception, **strictly construed** (*Ganga Bai*, *Mask & Co.*, *Dhulabhai*).
- Even under ouster clauses, civil courts examine acts **without jurisdiction, mala fide, coram non judice or against natural justice** (*Abbasia Cooperative Bank*, PLD 1997 SC 3).
- s. 15: lowest grade; s. 16: immovable property where situate (mortgage suits also where cause of action arose); proviso — personal obedience → defendant's residence.
- s. 17: property in several jurisdictions; s. 18: uncertainty; s. 19: wrongs to person / movables → either place.
- s. 20: defendant's residence / business / work, or where **cause of action** wholly or partly arises; corporations — principal office or subordinate office.
- s. 21: territorial objection waived unless taken in trial court at earliest opportunity (before issues) **and** failure of justice; s. 99: no reversal for irregularities not affecting merits or jurisdiction.
:::
