# Chapter 5 · Stay of suit — res sub judice (section 10)

## 5.1 The idea

*Res sub judice* means "a matter under judicial consideration". If the same matter between the same parties is **already pending** in a competent court, a second court must **not proceed with the trial** of a later suit on that matter. The first suit goes on; the second waits.

> "10. No Court shall proceed with the trial of any suit in which the matter in issue is also directly and substantially in issue in a previously instituted suit between the same parties, or between parties under whom they or any of them claim litigating under the same title where such suit is pending in the same or any other Court in Pakistan having jurisdiction to grant the relief claimed, or in any Court beyond the limits of Pakistan established or continued by the Federal Government and having like jurisdiction, or before the Supreme Court.
> Explanation.— The pendency of a suit in a foreign Court does not preclude the Courts in Pakistan from trying a suit founded on the same cause of action."

:::why Why s. 10?
1. To prevent **two courts of concurrent jurisdiction** from trying the same matter at the same time and giving **conflicting decisions**.
2. To protect the defendant from being **harassed by multiple suits** on the same matter.
3. To save **judicial time and public money**.
4. It is the "pending" twin of res judicata: s. 10 stops a parallel trial; s. 11 stops a repeat trial after a decision.
:::

## 5.2 Conditions — all must exist

1. **Two suits** — a *previously instituted* suit and a *subsequently instituted* suit.
2. The **matter in issue** in the later suit is **directly and substantially in issue** in the earlier suit. (The whole subject matter need not be identical, but the matter in issue must be the same in substance; incidental overlap is not enough.)
3. **Same parties**, or parties under whom they or any of them claim.
4. **Litigating under the same title** — in the same capacity (e.g., both times as owner, not once as owner and once as trustee).
5. The earlier suit is **pending**.
6. The court of the earlier suit has **jurisdiction to grant the relief claimed** in the later suit.
7. The earlier suit is pending **in Pakistan**, or in a court beyond Pakistan established or continued by the Federal Government, or before the Supreme Court.

## 5.3 Effects and important points

- **Only the trial is barred, not the institution.** The later suit can be filed and registered; the court simply does not *proceed with the trial*.
- **Interim orders are allowed.** The court where the later suit is pending may still grant a temporary injunction, appoint a receiver, or make other interlocutory orders (s. 94, O. XXXIX), because these are not "trial".
- **A decree passed in breach of s. 10 is not a nullity.** Section 10 is a rule of procedure; a decree passed in breach can be set aside in appeal, but is not void, and can be executed if it stands.
- **Parties may waive s. 10.** Because it is for the benefit of the parties, they can agree that the later suit be tried.
- **Consolidation.** Under its inherent power (s. 151), a court can consolidate both suits and try them together, which is often the best practical solution.
- **"Trial"** has been read as the hearing on merits. In a **summary suit (O. XXXVII)**, the court can still decide whether to grant leave to defend, because that is not "trial" of the suit (*Indian Bank v. Maharashtra State Co-operative Marketing Federation*, (1998) 5 SCC 69).
- **Foreign court:** the pendency of a suit in a foreign court is **no bar** (Explanation). The Pakistani court may proceed. (The foreign judgment, once given, may operate as res judicata under s. 13 if conclusive.)

:::eg Res sub judice in practice
Farhan files Suit A in the Civil Court, Islamabad (West), for a declaration that he owns Plot 45, I-8/4, by virtue of a sale agreement and payment. Six months later, the seller Saeed files Suit B in the Civil Court, Islamabad (East), against Farhan for a declaration that the sale agreement is void and for possession. The matter directly and substantially in issue — **validity of the agreement and title to Plot 45** — is the same in both suits; parties are the same; both courts are competent. **The court in Suit B must stay its trial** under s. 10. It may still pass an interim order to protect the plot. The better course: either court may request transfer under s. 24, or the District Judge may withdraw both and send them to one court for **consolidated** trial.
:::

:::trap Do not confuse
Section 10 applies to **suits**. For pending **appeals**, courts apply the same principle under s. 151. Section 10 does not apply where the **earlier** suit is pending in a court that **cannot grant** the relief claimed in the later suit (e.g., the earlier forum is a Rent Controller and the later suit claims declaration of title).
:::

# Chapter 6 · Res judicata (section 11) and the related bars

## 6.1 The idea

*Res judicata* means "a matter already decided". Once a competent court has **finally decided** a matter between parties, the same parties (or those claiming under them) cannot fight the same matter again in another suit.

> "11. No Court shall try any suit or issue in which the matter directly and substantially in issue has been directly and substantially in issue in a former suit between the same parties, or between parties under whom they or any of them claim, litigating under the same title, in a Court competent to try such subsequent suit or the suit in which such issue has been subsequently raised, and has been heard and finally decided by such Court."

The section is followed by **six Explanations** in the Pakistani text (India later added two more in 1976; those are **not** part of our law).

:::maxim The three maxims behind res judicata
- **Nemo debet bis vexari pro una et eadem causa** — no one should be vexed twice for the same cause (*private justice*).
- **Interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium** — it is in the interest of the State that there should be an end to litigation (*public policy*).
- **Res judicata pro veritate accipitur** — a judicial decision must be accepted as correct (*finality and respect for judgments*).
:::

:::case Sheoparsan Singh v. Ramnandan Prasad Singh ((1916) 43 IA 91, Privy Council)
__Held.__ Sir Lawrence Jenkins explained that the rule of res judicata, while founded on ancient precedent, is dictated by a wisdom which is for all time. It is a rule of universal law pervading every well-regulated system of jurisprudence, and rests on the twin considerations of public policy (an end to litigation) and private justice (no one should be twice vexed). Section 11 is not exhaustive; the general principle can apply beyond the four corners of the section.

__Ratio.__ Res judicata is a **rule of public policy**, not merely a technical bar; courts should apply it in substance, not defeat it by technicalities.
:::

## 6.2 The conditions — the five pillars

![Res judicata — the five conditions of s. 11](rj_conditions.png)

1. **Matter directly and substantially in issue** in the later suit was **directly and substantially in issue** in the former suit — actually (alleged by one party and denied or admitted by the other — Explanation III) or **constructively** (Explanation IV).
2. **Same parties**, or parties under whom they or any of them **claim** (privies — e.g., purchasers, heirs, assignees).
3. **Same title** — the parties litigated in the same capacity.
4. **Competent court** — the court that decided the former suit was competent to try the **subsequent** suit (or the suit in which the issue is now raised). Competence is judged **irrespective of any right of appeal** (Explanation II). Competence means pecuniary as well as subject-matter competence.
5. **Heard and finally decided** — the matter was decided **on the merits**, after hearing, and the decision has become **final**.

### Understanding "directly and substantially in issue"

A matter is *directly and substantially* in issue if it was **essential** to the decision — the court *had* to decide it to reach its result. A matter that was only **collaterally or incidentally** in issue (mentioned on the way, but not necessary to the decision) does **not** operate as res judicata.

:::eg Direct vs incidental
Kashif sues Waqar for rent of a shop, claiming to be the owner-landlord. Waqar denies Kashif's ownership. The court decides that Kashif is the landlord and decrees the rent. Later Kashif sues Waqar for possession of the shop on the basis of ownership. Is "ownership" res judicata? It depends: if ownership was **necessary** to the rent decree and was framed as an issue and decided on evidence, it may be res judicata. If the court decided only that a landlord–tenant relationship existed (which can exist without ownership), title was **incidental**, and is not barred. Always ask: *was the finding necessary for the decree?*
:::

### "Former suit" — Explanation I

> "The expression 'former suit' shall denote a suit which has been decided prior to the suit in question whether or not it was instituted prior thereto."

What matters is **which suit was decided first**, not which was filed first. If Suit 2 (filed later) is decided first, it is the "former suit" for res judicata in Suit 1.

### Competence irrespective of appeal — Explanation II

If the first court was competent, it does not matter that its decision was (or was not) appealable to a different court than the second suit would go to.

### Pakistani text — no "court of limited jurisdiction" Explanation

India added Explanation VIII in 1976, so that a decision of a court of limited jurisdiction (like a small causes court) on an issue can operate as res judicata in a later suit even if that court could not try the later suit. **Pakistan has not added it.** So in Pakistan, if the former court was **not competent to try the subsequent suit** (for example, because its pecuniary limit was lower), the s. 11 bar does not strictly apply, though the general principle of res judicata or estoppel may still be argued.

## 6.3 Constructive res judicata — Explanation IV

> "Explanation IV.— Any matter which might and ought to have been made ground of defence or attack in such former suit shall be deemed to have been a matter directly and substantially in issue in such suit."

This is **constructive res judicata**. A party must bring forward **all** his grounds of attack or defence in one go. If he could have, and should have, raised a ground in the first suit but did not, he cannot raise it in a second suit.

:::case Henderson v. Henderson ((1843) 3 Hare 100)
__Held.__ Where a matter becomes the subject of litigation, the court requires the parties to bring forward their **whole case**, and will not (except in special circumstances) permit the same parties to open the same subject of litigation in respect of matter which might have been brought forward as part of the subject in contest, but which was not brought forward only because they have, from negligence, inadvertence or even accident, omitted part of their case.

__Ratio.__ The bar covers not only points actually decided, but every point which properly belonged to the subject of litigation and which the parties, exercising reasonable diligence, might have brought forward. This is the origin of **constructive res judicata**.
:::

:::eg Constructive res judicata
Mumtaz sues to set aside a gift deed executed by his father on the ground of **undue influence**. He loses. He then files a fresh suit to set aside the same gift deed on the ground of **fraud and want of possession**. All these grounds were available when he filed the first suit and "might and ought to" have been raised. The second suit is barred by **Explanation IV**.
:::

:::trap Constructive res judicata vs O. II r. 2
- **Explanation IV, s. 11** concerns **grounds** of attack or defence (legal arguments, facts in support).
- **O. II r. 2** concerns **reliefs / claims** arising from the same cause of action. If the plaintiff omits to sue for part of his *claim* (e.g., sues for one month's rent when three months were due), he cannot later sue for the omitted part.
Both serve the same policy, but answer them separately.
:::

## 6.4 The other Explanations

- **Explanation III** — the matter must have been "alleged by one party and either denied or admitted, expressly or impliedly, by the other". An issue may arise from an implied admission too.
- **Explanation V** — "Any relief claimed in the plaint, which is not expressly granted by the decree, shall, for the purposes of this section, be deemed to have been refused." If a plaintiff asked for possession and mesne profits, and the decree is silent on mesne profits, mesne profits are deemed refused; he cannot sue for them again (his remedy was appeal or review).
- **Explanation VI** — **representative suits**: "Where persons litigate bona fide in respect of a public right or of a private right claimed in common for themselves and others, all persons interested in such right shall … be deemed to claim under the persons so litigating." A decision in a bona fide representative suit (e.g., under O. I r. 8, or a suit about a public right of way) binds all persons interested.

## 6.5 Where res judicata applies — and where it does not

{w:40,60}
|Situation|Does res judicata apply?|
|**Different stages of the same suit**|**Yes.** A decision at an earlier stage binds the court and parties at later stages (*Satyadhyan Ghosal*, below). But an interlocutory order that is not final (e.g., an interim injunction) does not bar reconsideration on changed facts.|
|**Execution proceedings**|**Yes**, on the general principle. A judgment-debtor who did not raise an objection to executability when he could have is barred from raising it later (constructive res judicata).|
|**Writ petitions**|**Yes**, a decision on merits in a writ petition bars a fresh writ or suit on the same matter (*Daryao v. State of U.P.*, AIR 1961 SC 1457). A writ dismissed *in limine* or withdrawn, without a speaking decision on merits, does not.|
|**Between co-defendants**|**Yes**, if (i) there was a conflict of interest between them, (ii) it was necessary to decide that conflict to give the plaintiff relief, and (iii) the question was finally decided.|
|**Ex parte decree**|**Yes**, an ex parte decree is a decision of the court after hearing the plaintiff; it operates as res judicata unless set aside.|
|**Compromise / consent decree**|Not strictly under s. 11 (no adjudication on merits), but the parties are bound by **estoppel** created by the consent decree.|
|**Dismissal for default** (O. IX r. 8)|Not res judicata (no decision on merits), but a fresh suit is barred by **O. IX r. 9** and **s. 12(1)**.|
|**Suit withdrawn without permission** (O. XXIII r. 1)|Not res judicata, but fresh suit barred by **O. XXIII r. 1(3)** and **s. 12(1)** (Topic 2).|
|**Decision without jurisdiction**|**No.** A decision by a court lacking jurisdiction is a nullity and cannot operate as res judicata.|
|**Pure question of law** unrelated to facts, e.g., a wrong view of a court's jurisdiction or of a statute later declared invalid|**No**, a decision on a pure question of law, especially one that would *confer or take away jurisdiction* contrary to statute, does not bind in a later case on a different cause of action (*Mathura Prasad v. Dossibai*, AIR 1971 SC 2355). A decision on a **mixed question of law and fact** does bind.|
|**Findings against the successful party**, which were not necessary for the decree|**No.** The party had no right of appeal against them, so they are not res judicata.|

:::case Satyadhyan Ghosal v. Deorajin Debi (AIR 1960 SC 941)
__Facts.__ In a landlord's eviction proceedings, the tenant claimed protection of a new rent law. The trial court rejected it; the High Court, in revision, held the tenant was protected and remanded the case. The landlord did not appeal that order to the Supreme Court at that stage. After remand, when the final decree was passed against him, he sought to challenge the earlier (interlocutory) High Court decision.

__Held.__ The principle of res judicata applies **between two stages of the same litigation**: if a court decides a matter at an earlier stage, it cannot be reopened at a later stage of the same proceeding. However, an **interlocutory order which was not appealable** (or could not be challenged separately) does not prevent a party from challenging it in an appeal from the final decree — which is what s. 105 CPC allows.

__Ratio.__ (1) Res judicata operates between different stages of the same suit. (2) A party is not barred from challenging an interlocutory order, which he could not or need not have appealed separately, when appealing from the final decree.
:::

:::case Arjun Singh v. Mohindra Kumar (AIR 1964 SC 993)
__Held.__ An order under O. IX r. 7 (refusing to let a defendant join an adjourned ex parte hearing for want of "good cause") does **not** operate as res judicata on a later application under O. IX r. 13 (to set aside the ex parte decree for "sufficient cause"), because the two inquiries are different and the earlier order was not a final decision of the matter. Interlocutory orders that merely regulate procedure do not attract res judicata.

__Ratio.__ Res judicata applies only to decisions that **finally** determine an issue; orders regulating the course of proceedings can be revisited.
:::

## 6.6 How a court decides a plea of res judicata

1. Res judicata is a plea that must be **specifically raised in the written statement** (O. VIII r. 2) — it is a "new fact" that shows the suit is not maintainable.
2. The party pleading it must **prove** the former suit by producing **certified copies of the plaint, written statement, issues and judgment / decree** of the former suit. The judgment alone is not always enough; the pleadings show what was "directly and substantially in issue".
3. It is usually a **mixed question of law and fact**; it may be decided as a **preliminary issue** under O. XIV r. 2 only if it can be decided on the record without evidence.
4. If res judicata is clear from the plaint itself (for example, the plaint admits a previous decree on the same matter), the plaint may be **rejected under O. VII r. 11(d)** as "barred by law".

:::tip Writing an answer on res judicata — a six-part plan
(1) Meaning + maxims; (2) text of s. 11; (3) five conditions, each explained with an example; (4) Explanations I–VI, especially IV (constructive res judicata, *Henderson*); (5) application — stages of same suit (*Satyadhyan Ghosal*), writs (*Daryao*), co-defendants, ex parte and consent decrees, exceptions (no jurisdiction; pure question of law — *Mathura Prasad*); (6) distinguish from res sub judice and estoppel; conclusion on public policy.
:::

## 6.7 Res judicata compared

|Point|Res sub judice (s. 10)|Res judicata (s. 11)|
|Meaning|Matter **pending** decision|Matter **already decided**|
|Purpose|Prevent **parallel** trials and conflicting decisions|Prevent **repeat** litigation; finality|
|Bars|**Trial** of the later suit (institution allowed)|**Trial** of the later suit or issue|
|Stage|While the former suit is **pending**|After the former suit is **decided**|
|Former / previous suit|**Previously instituted** suit|Suit **decided first** (Explanation I) — may have been instituted later|
|Effect of breach|Decree not void; irregular|Decree in breach can be set aside; plea must be raised|

|Basis|A **decision of a court**|A **representation by a party** relied on by the other|
|Effect|Ousts the **jurisdiction** of the court to try the matter|A rule of **evidence**: a party cannot say the opposite of his representation|
|Who is bound|Parties and privies to the decision|The person who made the representation and his privies|
|Nature|Public policy — end to litigation|Equity — prevent injustice from inconsistent conduct|

## 6.8 Bar of further suit by rules — s. 12(1)

> "12. (1) Where a plaintiff is precluded by rules from instituting a further suit in respect of any particular cause of action, he shall not be entitled to institute a suit in respect of such cause of action in any Court to which this Code applies."

The *rules* in the First Schedule sometimes forbid a fresh suit. Section 12(1) gives that prohibition the force of the Code in every court.

{w:30,70}
|Rule|When a fresh suit is barred|
|O. II r. 2(2)–(3)|Plaintiff omitted or relinquished part of his claim, or omitted a relief without the court's leave, arising from the same cause of action|
|O. IX r. 9|Suit dismissed because the plaintiff did not appear (O. IX r. 8)|
|O. XXII r. 9|Suit abated or dismissed on death / insolvency etc. (Topic 2)|
|O. XXIII r. 1(3)|Suit withdrawn without the court's permission to file a fresh suit (Topic 2)|

Note: Dismissal under **O. IX rr. 2, 3 and 5** does **not** bar a fresh suit (subject to limitation) — r. 4 and r. 5(2) expressly allow it.

## 6.9 Foreign judgments — ss. 13 and 14

> "13. A foreign judgment shall be conclusive as to any matter thereby directly adjudicated upon between the same parties or between parties under whom they or any of them claim litigating under the same title except— (a) where it has not been pronounced by a Court of competent jurisdiction; (b) where it has not been given on the merits of the case; (c) where it appears on the face of the proceedings to be founded on an incorrect view of international law or a refusal to recognise the law of Pakistan in cases in which such law is applicable; (d) where the proceedings in which the judgment was obtained are opposed to natural justice; (e) where it has been obtained by fraud; (f) where it sustains a claim founded on a breach of any law in force in Pakistan."

> "14. The Court shall presume, upon the production of any document purporting to be a certified copy of a foreign judgment, that such judgment was pronounced by a Court of competent jurisdiction, unless the contrary appears on the record; but such presumption may be displaced by proving want of jurisdiction."

:::why Why recognise foreign judgments at all?
Comity of nations and practical justice: people and businesses cross borders, and it would be unjust and wasteful to make them litigate everything twice. But Pakistan protects its own public policy, its law and natural justice through the six exceptions.
:::

:::eg Foreign judgment
A Dubai court decrees a claim of AED 200,000 in favour of a Dubai company against Imran, a Pakistani who appeared and contested. The company sues Imran on the judgment in Islamabad. Under s. 13 the Dubai judgment is **conclusive** as to the debt unless Imran shows one of the six exceptions — e.g., that he was never served (natural justice, clause (d)), or that the judgment was given ex parte without considering the merits (clause (b)), or that it enforces an interest-based claim that is illegal in Pakistan (clause (f) — arguable). Execution of foreign decrees directly is governed by s. 44A (reciprocating territories) — Topic 2.
:::

:::recap Chapter 5 and 6 in ten lines
- **s. 10:** later suit's **trial** stayed if same matter directly and substantially in issue in a **pending, previously instituted** suit between same parties, same title, in a competent court in Pakistan. Institution and interim orders allowed; decree in breach not void; foreign pending suit no bar.
- **s. 11:** matter directly and substantially in issue + same parties + same title + competent court + heard and finally decided = barred.
- Maxims: *nemo debet bis vexari*; *interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium*; *res judicata pro veritate accipitur*.
- Explanation I: "former suit" = decided first; II: competence irrespective of appeal; III: alleged and denied / admitted; **IV: constructive res judicata** (*Henderson v. Henderson*); V: relief not granted = refused; VI: representative suits.
- Applies between stages of same suit (*Satyadhyan Ghosal*), to writs (*Daryao*), between co-defendants (3 conditions), to ex parte decrees.
- Does not apply to: decisions without jurisdiction; pure questions of law affecting jurisdiction (*Mathura Prasad*); findings unnecessary for the decree; mere interlocutory orders (*Arjun Singh*).
- Plead it in the written statement; prove with certified copies of former **pleadings, issues and judgment**.
- **s. 12(1):** fresh suit barred where rules bar it — O. II r. 2, O. IX r. 9, O. XXII r. 9, O. XXIII r. 1.
- **s. 13:** foreign judgment conclusive except 6 cases (no jurisdiction; not on merits; wrong international law / refusal of Pakistani law; natural justice; fraud; breach of Pakistani law). **s. 14:** presumption of competence.
- Pakistan has **no Explanation VII–VIII**; competence to try the subsequent suit is still required.
:::

# Chapter 7 · Section 12(2): challenging a decree for fraud, misrepresentation or want of jurisdiction

## 7.1 The provision — a Pakistani innovation

> "12. (2) Where a person challenges the validity of a judgment, decree or order on the plea of fraud, mis-representation or want of jurisdiction, he shall seek his remedy by making an application to the Court which passed the final judgment, decree or order and not by a separate suit."

This sub-section was added by the **Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Ordinance X of 1980**. It has **no equivalent in the Indian CPC**, so Indian textbooks do not cover it. It is a favourite question in Pakistani judicial exams (LHC 2023 asked about s. 12(2) and a precept; LLB papers ask "A obtains a decree by fraud against B. Can B file a suit for its cancellation?").

:::why Why was s. 12(2) introduced?
Before 1980, a person defrauded by a decree had to file a **separate suit** to set it aside — a new round of litigation through trial, appeal and second appeal, taking years, while the decree was being executed. Section 12(2) gives a **quick, single remedy** in the **same court** that passed the decree. That court knows the record best and can examine quickly whether it was deceived. It also prevents abuse: a separate suit is now **barred**.
:::

## 7.2 Ingredients

1. **"Where a person challenges"** — *any person*: a party to the suit or a **stranger** to it whose rights are affected by the decree (for example, a true owner whose property was decreed to someone else behind his back).
2. **"the validity of a judgment, decree or order"** — not only decrees, but any final judgment or order, including a compromise decree or an ex parte decree.
3. **Only three grounds:** (a) **fraud**, (b) **misrepresentation**, or (c) **want of jurisdiction**. Other grounds (for example, the judge appreciated evidence wrongly) are for appeal, not s. 12(2).
4. **Forum:** "the Court which passed the **final** judgment, decree or order". If the decree of the trial court was **merged** into an appellate or revisional decision **on merits**, the application lies to the **appellate / revisional court** that passed the final order. If the appeal was not decided on merits (e.g., dismissed as withdrawn), the trial court's decree remains the final one.
5. **"and not by a separate suit"** — the separate suit is **barred**. A plaint seeking to set aside a decree on these grounds may be rejected or converted / returned, as the case requires.

## 7.3 What counts as "fraud", "misrepresentation" and "want of jurisdiction"?

- **Fraud** here means fraud **on the court** — deliberate deception that prevents the court from knowing the true facts or prevents the other party from presenting his case. Typical examples: suppressing the defendant's correct address so that he is never served, and obtaining an ex parte decree; impersonating the defendant; filing a forged compromise; obtaining a decree against a person known to be dead. **Mere false evidence** in a contested trial (intrinsic fraud) is generally a matter for appeal, not s. 12(2), because the other side had the chance to expose it.
- **Misrepresentation** — false representation of a material fact to the court that led to the decree (e.g., falsely stating that the property is not mortgaged, or that the defendant had agreed).
- **Want of jurisdiction** — the court had no jurisdiction (subject-matter, pecuniary or territorial in a way that makes the decree void), or the decree was passed against a dead person, or without service where service was essential.

:::maxim Fraus omnia corrumpit — fraud unravels everything
A judgment obtained by fraud is a nullity in the eyes of the law; it cannot be allowed to stand, whatever the stage. Section 12(2) gives this maxim a procedural channel.
:::

:::maxim Nullus commodum capere potest de injuria sua propria — no one can take advantage of his own wrong
A decree-holder who obtained the decree by deceiving the court cannot keep its benefit.
:::

## 7.4 Procedure on a s. 12(2) application

1. The application must **specify the particulars** of the fraud or misrepresentation (O. VI r. 4: particulars of fraud must be given). Vague allegations are not enough.
2. The procedure for suits applies "as far as it can be made applicable" (**s. 141 CPC**). The court issues notice to the other side and calls for a reply.
3. **Framing issues and recording evidence is not automatic.** The settled view of the superior courts is that the court has discretion: if the allegations raise a genuine factual controversy that cannot be resolved from the record, the court should frame issues and record evidence; if the record itself answers the allegations (e.g., the applicant's own signature on the summons), the court may decide summarily with reasons.
4. **Limitation:** the CPC fixes none; the superior courts generally apply the residuary **Article 181 of the Limitation Act** (three years from when the right to apply accrues, ordinarily from **knowledge** of the decree / fraud).
5. **Remedy against the order:** an order allowing or dismissing a s. 12(2) application is an "order", not listed in s. 104 / O. XLIII; the usual remedy is **revision under s. 115** (or the remedy available against that court's orders if the order is passed by an appellate court).

:::eg Precept and s. 12(2) — the LHC 2023 question
*Question:* A decree is passed by a Civil Court at Lahore. A **precept** (s. 46 CPC) is sent to the Civil Court at Hyderabad to attach the judgment-debtor's property there. Does the Hyderabad court have the same powers as the decree-passing court? Where does a s. 12(2) application lie?
*Answer:* A **precept** is only a direction to another court to **attach** property for a limited period (s. 46(2): two months unless extended), pending transfer of the decree for execution; the precept court acts as an agent of the decree-passing court and **does not** acquire the full powers of an executing court under s. 42 unless the decree itself is transferred to it under s. 39. A s. 12(2) application challenges the **decree**, so it lies to the court "which passed the final judgment, decree or order", i.e., the **Lahore court** (or the appellate court at Lahore if the decree merged in an appellate decision on merits) — **not** Hyderabad.
:::

:::trap Common mistakes on s. 12(2)
- Saying a separate suit lies for fraud — **barred** since 1980.
- Filing in the executing court instead of the court that passed the **final** decree.
- Using s. 12(2) to re-argue evidence — it is limited to fraud, misrepresentation, want of jurisdiction.
- Forgetting that a **stranger** can also apply.
:::

:::recap Chapter 7 in five lines
- s. 12(2) (added 1980, Pakistan only): challenge to validity of a judgment, decree or order on **fraud, misrepresentation or want of jurisdiction** → **application** to the court that passed the **final** decision; **no separate suit**.
- Any person aggrieved — party or stranger — may apply.
- Fraud = fraud on the court (extrinsic), not merely false evidence; particulars must be pleaded.
- Procedure via s. 141; issues and evidence at the court's discretion where facts are disputed; Art. 181 Limitation Act (3 years) generally applied; revision lies against the order.
- Maxims: *fraus omnia corrumpit*; *nullus commodum capere potest de injuria sua propria*.
:::

# Chapter 8 · Transfer and withdrawal of suits (sections 22 to 25)

## 8.1 Two kinds of transfer

1. **s. 22 — Transfer at the defendant's request where several courts have jurisdiction.** Where a suit could have been filed in any of two or more courts and is filed in one, **any defendant**, after notice to the other parties, may "at the earliest possible opportunity and in all cases where issues are settled at or before such settlement" apply to have it transferred to another competent court. The court decides where the suit shall proceed.
2. **s. 24 — General power of transfer and withdrawal.** The **High Court or the District Court**, on application of any party (after notice and hearing) **or of its own motion** (without notice), may at **any stage**: (a) transfer any suit, appeal or other proceeding pending before it to a subordinate competent court; or (b) **withdraw** any suit, appeal or proceeding pending in a subordinate court and (i) try it itself, (ii) transfer it to another competent subordinate court, or (iii) retransfer it to the court from which it was withdrawn.

### To which court does a s. 22 application go? — s. 23

- If the courts are subordinate to the **same appellate court** — to that appellate court (e.g., the District Judge).
- If they are subordinate to **different appellate courts but the same High Court** — to the High Court.
- If they are subordinate to **different High Courts** — to the High Court within whose limits the suit was filed.

### Other points

- **s. 24(2):** the transferee court may **retry** the case or proceed **from the point** at which it was transferred, subject to special directions.
- **s. 24(3):** Additional and Assistant Judges are deemed subordinate to the District Court (so the District Judge can transfer cases to and from ADJs).
- **s. 24A:** on transfer on a party's application, the transferring court fixes a **date for appearance** before the new court; on transfer otherwise, parties appear before the old court on the date fixed and are then directed to the new court.
- **s. 25** (power of the Provincial Government to transfer suits) has been **omitted** — only courts can transfer cases.
- **Islamabad:** under **s. 16 of the Civil Courts Ordinance**, a District Judge may, with the previous sanction of the High Court, delegate his s. 24 powers to a Civil Judge for a specified area.

## 8.2 Grounds on which transfer is granted

Transfer is a **discretionary** power, exercised judicially. Common grounds:

1. **Reasonable apprehension of bias** — not proof of actual bias, but facts from which a reasonable person would fear he will not get a fair hearing (e.g., the judge is related to the opponent, or has expressed a view on the merits).
2. **Convenience of parties and witnesses**, the location of the property and the evidence.
3. **Avoiding conflicting decisions** — to bring connected cases before one court for **consolidated** trial.
4. **Balance of convenience** and expeditious disposal.

Mere suspicion, a single adverse interim order, or a wish to delay is **not** enough.

:::maxim Justice should not only be done but should manifestly and undoubtedly be seen to be done
From *R v. Sussex Justices, ex parte McCarthy* [1924] 1 KB 256 (Lord Hewart C.J.). This is the guiding principle of transfer on the ground of apprehended bias: the test is the **reasonable apprehension of a reasonable litigant**, not the judge's own sense of his integrity.
:::

:::maxim Nemo judex in causa sua — no one should be a judge in his own cause
The second limb of natural justice (with *audi alteram partem*). A judge with any personal interest in the dispute must not hear it.
:::

:::recap Chapter 8 in five lines
- s. 22: defendant may seek transfer among competent courts — early, before issues; forum under s. 23.
- s. 24: High Court / District Court may transfer or withdraw any suit, appeal or proceeding at any stage, on application or *suo motu*.
- Transferee court may retry or continue (s. 24(2)); appearance date fixed (s. 24A); s. 25 omitted.
- Grounds: reasonable apprehension of bias, convenience, consolidation; not mere suspicion.
- Maxims: justice must be seen to be done; *nemo judex in causa sua*. Islamabad: s. 16 Ordinance delegation.
:::

# Chapter 9 · Institution of suits, summons, discovery and costs (sections 26 to 35C)

## 9.1 Institution of a suit — s. 26

> "26. Every suit shall be instituted by presentation of a plaint or in such other manner as may be prescribed." (as substituted by Act XIX of 2023)

A suit **begins** when the plaint is **presented** to the court or to an officer appointed for this purpose (O. IV r. 1). The date of presentation is the date of institution — important for **limitation** (s. 3 Limitation Act), for res sub judice ("previously instituted") and for court fees.

:::ict Sections 26A–26D and 27A — added and then omitted
The official federal text shows "[26A–26D. * * *]" and "[27A. * * *]" with the note "Subs. and Omitted by Act No. XIX of 2023, s. 3". In other words, provisions that had been introduced for Islamabad (front-loading of documents and procedural steps at the time of filing) were **omitted in 2023**. Do not quote them as current law. If asked about Islamabad reforms, say: the 2020 Act introduced case-management and front-loading reforms; the 2023 Act rolled back several of them while keeping s. 1(4), s. 3, s. 128(3) and the reasons requirement in s. 151; s. 159 saves pending proceedings.
:::

## 9.2 Summons — ss. 27 to 29

- **s. 27:** "Where a suit has been duly instituted, a summons may be issued to the defendant to appear and answer the claim and may be served in manner prescribed." The detailed manner is in **Order V** (Chapter 12).
- **s. 28:** service in another province through the court there, under that province's rules.
- **s. 29:** summonses and other processes of courts outside Pakistan may be served by Pakistani courts as if their own, if the foreign court was established by the Federal Government or the province has notified it.

## 9.3 Discovery and attendance — ss. 30 to 32

- **s. 30 — Power to order discovery and the like:** the court may, of its own motion or on application, (a) make orders about **interrogatories**, admission of documents and facts, and discovery, inspection, production, impounding and return of documents; (b) issue summons to persons to give evidence or produce documents; (c) order any fact to be proved by **affidavit**. Details in Orders XI–XIII and XVI (Chapter 17).
- **s. 31:** ss. 27–29 apply to summonses to witnesses.
- **s. 32 — Penalty for default:** to compel attendance of a person summoned under s. 30, the court may (a) issue a warrant of arrest, (b) attach and sell his property, (c) impose a **fine up to Rs. 2,000**, (d) order him to furnish security for appearance and, in default, commit him to prison.

## 9.4 Judgment, decree and interest — ss. 33 to 34B

- **s. 33:** "The Court, after the case has been heard, shall pronounce judgment, and on such judgment a decree shall follow." (Details in O. XX — Topic 2.)
- **s. 34 — Interest:** in a money decree the court may award (i) interest **before suit** (as adjudged — e.g., under the contract), (ii) interest **pendente lite** (from the date of suit to the decree) at a reasonable rate, and (iii) **future interest** (from decree to payment). If the decree is silent on future interest, it is **deemed refused**, and no separate suit lies (s. 34(2)).
- **s. 34A — Interest on public dues:** if a suit was filed to avoid paying public dues, the court may, while dismissing it, order interest at **2% above the bank rate**; if recovery of public dues was unjustified, the court may order the State to pay interest at the same rate.
- **s. 34B — Banking company dues:** decrees for loans by a banking company carry return from decree to payment at the contracted rate or 2% above bank rate (interest-bearing), or contracted mark-up / rental, or profit-sharing return, as the case may be.

## 9.5 Costs — ss. 35 to 35C (Islamabad text)

:::ict Section 35 — actual costs (substituted by Act XVII of 2017)
> "35. (1) Subject to such conditions and limitations, as may be prescribed, and to the provisions of any law for the time being in force,— (i) a party to any proceedings shall, before the announcement of final order, judgment or decree, file in the prescribed form, detail of actual costs of litigation, including but not limited to court fee, stamp fee, fee paid to counsel and all other ancillary or incidental expenses thereto; (ii) the Court shall award the actual costs of litigation under clause (i) to the successful party with markup not exceeding the prescribed limit per annum, as notified by the State Bank of Pakistan, at the time of passing the order, judgement or decree; (iii) the costs other than those mentioned in clause (i) shall be in the discretion of the Court; and (iv) the Court shall have full power to determine out of what property such costs are to be paid and recovered and to give all necessary directions for the purpose aforesaid. (2) The fact the Court has no jurisdiction in respect of the proceedings shall be no bar to the exercise of such powers under this section."

**In plain words:** in Islamabad, the winner is entitled to his **actual** costs of litigation — court fee, stamps, lawyer's fee and other expenses — with mark-up within the State Bank limit, provided he files the details in the prescribed form **before** the final decision. Other costs remain discretionary. This is a big change from the old rule, where costs were entirely discretionary and usually nominal.
:::

### Adjournment costs — s. 35A

> "35A. If, on the date fixed for hearing in any proceedings, a party to the proceedings or any other person, despite service of notice, fails to appear or comply with any order of the Court or mandatory provision of the Code or any other law for the time being in force, seeks an adjournment for such purpose, the Court shall, for sufficient cause and reasons to be recorded, grant such adjournment on the condition that such party or person shall pay to the other party, costs of adjournment which shall **not be less than five thousand Rupees per adjournment** or such higher amount as may be prescribed from time to time:
> Provided that if the Court is satisfied that the adjournment being sought is on account of unavoidable reasons beyond the control of the party concerned, which reasons shall be recorded by the Court, the Court may grant adjournment without imposing adjournment cost:
> Provided further that if the Court finds that there is a tendency of seeking repeated adjournments by any party on such grounds, the Court may not grant adjournment without imposing the adjournment costs as the Court may deem fit."

- An order under s. 35A is **appealable** under **s. 104(1)(f)**, but only on the ground that **no order, or an order for a smaller amount, ought to have been made** (proviso to s. 104(1)).

### Special costs — s. 35B

> "35B. (1) If in any proceedings, the Court finds that any averment made by any party is false or vexatious to the knowledge of such party, the Court shall award special costs to the opposite party against whom such averment has been made. (2) The amount of any special costs awarded … shall be taken into account in any subsequent suit for damages or compensation in respect of such claim or defence."

### General — s. 35C

> "35C. The Government shall not be liable to costs under section 35, 35A and 35B. Explanation.— In sections 35, 35A and 35B, the expression 'proceedings' includes suit, appeal, review, revision, execution or any other proceedings and any matter incidental thereto."

:::why Why such strong cost provisions?
Delay is the biggest problem of civil justice in Pakistan. Costs give the court a practical tool: a party who wastes time or makes false claims **pays for it**. Actual costs also make litigation fairer: an honest winner should not be out of pocket for having to defend his rights. The provisions serve the "primary objective" in s. 1(4) — saving expense and time.
:::

### Compensation for frivolous interim relief — s. 95 (link)

Where an arrest, attachment or temporary injunction under s. 94 was obtained on insufficient grounds, or the plaintiff's suit fails and there was no reasonable ground for it, the court may award the defendant compensation **up to Rs. 10,000** (within its pecuniary limits). Covered fully in Topic 2.

- Summons: s. 27 (details O. V); s. 28 other provinces; s. 29 foreign summonses.

- s. 34 interest: pre-suit, pendente lite, future (silence = refused); s. 34A public dues (2% above bank rate); s. 34B banking dues.
