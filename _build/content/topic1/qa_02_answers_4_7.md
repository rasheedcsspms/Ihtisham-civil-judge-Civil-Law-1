# Answer 4 · Res judicata and res sub judice; constructive res judicata (LLB standard)

:::glance PUNJAB UNIVERSITY LLB (CPC) 2008 & 2009 · STANDARD JUDICIAL-EXAM QUESTION · 20 MARKS
Define and distinguish between the two legal terms *res sub judice* and *res judicata*. Explain the doctrine of constructive res judicata with illustrations.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](a4_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Three tasks: **define** each doctrine (ss. 10 and 11), **distinguish** them point by point (a table is ideal), and **explain constructive res judicata** (Explanation IV) with illustrations. Give the conditions of each doctrine, the policy (maxims), and leading cases. A comparison table near the end earns quick marks.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — two guards against repeated litigation

Both doctrines protect the courts and the parties from **multiplicity of litigation** and **conflicting decisions**. *Res sub judice* deals with a matter **pending** before a court; *res judicata* with a matter **already decided**. Section 10 CPC stops a **parallel** trial; s. 11 stops a **repeat** trial.

### 2. Res sub judice — s. 10

#### i) The provision

"No Court shall proceed with the trial of any suit in which the matter in issue is also directly and substantially in issue in a previously instituted suit between the same parties, or between parties under whom they or any of them claim litigating under the same title where such suit is pending in the same or any other Court in Pakistan having jurisdiction to grant the relief claimed …" (s. 10).

#### ii) Conditions

(1) Two suits, one **previously instituted**; (2) the **matter in issue** directly and substantially the same; (3) same parties or their privies; (4) litigating under the same title; (5) the earlier suit **pending**; (6) in a court in Pakistan (or a federally established court abroad, or the Supreme Court) **competent to grant the relief** claimed in the later suit.

#### iii) Effect

Only the **trial** of the later suit is stayed; it may be **instituted**, and **interim orders** may be passed. A decree in breach of s. 10 is **not a nullity**. The court may **consolidate** both suits under s. 151. A suit pending in a **foreign** court is no bar (Explanation).

### 3. Res judicata — s. 11

#### i) The provision

"No Court shall try any suit or issue in which the matter directly and substantially in issue has been directly and substantially in issue in a former suit between the same parties, or between parties under whom they or any of them claim, litigating under the same title, in a Court competent to try such subsequent suit or the suit in which such issue has been subsequently raised, and has been heard and finally decided by such Court" (s. 11).

#### ii) Conditions

(1) Matter **directly and substantially in issue** in both suits; (2) **same parties** or privies; (3) **same title**; (4) former court **competent** to try the later suit; (5) **heard and finally decided** on merits.

#### iii) Policy — three maxims

*Nemo debet bis vexari pro una et eadem causa* (no one twice vexed for the same cause), *interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium* (public interest in an end to litigation), and *res judicata pro veritate accipitur* (a decided matter is accepted as true). The Privy Council called it a rule of universal law, founded on public policy and private justice (*Sheoparsan Singh v. Ramnandan Prasad Singh*, (1916) 43 IA 91).

### 4. The Explanations to s. 11

- **I** — "former suit" is the suit **decided first**, whether or not instituted first.
- **II** — competence is judged **irrespective of a right of appeal**.
- **III** — the matter must have been **alleged by one party and denied or admitted** by the other.
- **IV** — **constructive res judicata** (below).
- **V** — relief claimed but **not expressly granted** is **deemed refused**.
- **VI** — persons litigating bona fide about a **public or common right** bind all interested (representative suits).

### 5. Constructive res judicata — Explanation IV

#### i) The rule

"Any matter which **might and ought to** have been made ground of defence or attack in such former suit shall be deemed to have been a matter directly and substantially in issue in such suit."

#### ii) Meaning and rationale

A party must bring his **whole case** forward at once. Grounds he could and should have raised, but did not, are treated as if raised and decided. Otherwise a litigant could split his case into many suits and harass the other side indefinitely.

#### iii) Source of the rule

*Henderson v. Henderson* ((1843) 3 Hare 100): the court requires parties "to bring forward their whole case", and will not allow the same subject to be reopened on matters which might have been brought forward but were omitted "from negligence, inadvertence, or even accident".

### 6. Illustrations of constructive res judicata

#### i) Grounds of attack

Rizwan sues to set aside his father's gift of a house in F-7 on the ground of **undue influence** and loses. He then sues to set aside the same gift on the ground of **fraud and non-delivery of possession**. Those grounds were available and ought to have been taken in the first suit; the second suit is barred.

#### ii) Grounds of defence

In a mortgagee's suit for recovery, the mortgagor does not plead that the mortgage was void for want of registration. After a decree, he files a suit for a declaration that the mortgage was void for the same reason. Barred — it "might and ought to" have been a ground of defence.

### 7. Where the doctrines apply — and where they do not

Res judicata applies between **stages of the same suit** (*Satyadhyan Ghosal v. Deorajin Debi*, AIR 1960 SC 941), to **writ petitions** decided on merits (*Daryao v. State of U.P.*, AIR 1961 SC 1457), between **co-defendants** where there was a conflict between them that had to be decided, and to **ex parte decrees**. It does **not** apply to decisions **without jurisdiction**, to a **pure question of law** wrongly decided on jurisdiction (*Mathura Prasad v. Dossibai*, AIR 1971 SC 2355), or to **interlocutory orders** regulating procedure (*Arjun Singh v. Mohindra Kumar*, AIR 1964 SC 993).

### 8. Distinction — res sub judice and res judicata

|Basis|Res sub judice (s. 10)|Res judicata (s. 11)|
|Status of the earlier matter|**Pending** in a court|**Decided** finally|
|Object|Prevent **parallel** trials and conflicting decisions|Prevent **repeat** litigation; give **finality**|
|What is barred|**Trial** of the later suit (institution allowed)|**Trial** of the later suit or issue|
|Which suit is "earlier"|The **previously instituted** suit|The suit **decided first** (Expl. I)|
|Constructive form|None|**Explanation IV**|
|Effect of breach|Decree not a nullity; irregular|Decree in breach liable to be set aside; plea must be taken|
|Foreign court|Pending foreign suit **no bar**|Foreign judgment may bind under **s. 13**|

### 9. Constructive res judicata and O. II r. 2 distinguished

Explanation IV concerns **grounds** (arguments, facts) of attack or defence; **O. II r. 2** concerns **claims or reliefs** omitted from the same cause of action ("he shall not afterwards sue in respect of the portion so omitted"). Both serve finality, but they are separate rules.

### 10. Procedure — raising and proving the plea

Res judicata must be **specifically pleaded** (O. VIII r. 2) and proved by **certified copies of the pleadings, issues and judgment** of the former suit; if apparent from the plaint, the plaint may be **rejected** as "barred by any law" (O. VII r. 11(d)).

### 11. Conclusion

Res sub judice and res judicata are two stages of one policy: **one matter, one decision**. Section 10 freezes a parallel trial while the first is pending; s. 11, with constructive res judicata in Explanation IV, forbids reopening what was decided — or what should have been raised — in the first suit. Together they serve the interest of the State in ending litigation and the right of the individual not to be vexed twice.

## Why this answer scores

- Both doctrines are **defined with the statutory text and conditions**, not just described.
- The **comparison table** answers the "distinguish" part efficiently.
- **Constructive res judicata** is explained with its source case (*Henderson*) and **two illustrations** (attack and defence), as the question demands.
- The answer shows depth by marking the **limits** of res judicata and separating Explanation IV from **O. II r. 2**.

# Answer 5 · Precept, executing court and the forum for s. 12(2) (LHC 2023)

:::glance LHC CIVIL JUDGE EXAM 2023 · CIVIL LAW-II · Q. 6 · 20 MARKS
Whether the court to which precept is sent enjoys equal powers as the court which had passed the decree originally? If precept has been sent to Civil Court at Hyderabad by the Civil Court at Lahore, where would the application under section 12(2) of the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 lie — before the Court at Lahore or Hyderabad? Support your answer with reasoning.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](a5_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Two linked questions. (1) **Precept** (s. 46): what powers does the precept court have, compared with the decree-passing court and with a **transferee executing court** (ss. 39, 42)? (2) **Section 12(2)**: which court is "the Court which passed the final judgment, decree or order"? The answer requires reading three provisions together — ss. 46, 42 and 12(2) — and applying them to the Lahore–Hyderabad facts.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — the issues

Two questions arise: (i) whether a court receiving a **precept** acquires the same powers as the court that passed the decree; and (ii) whether an application under **s. 12(2)** challenging the decree lies at **Lahore** (decree court) or **Hyderabad** (precept court).

### 2. Courts that may execute a decree

"A decree may be executed either by the Court which passed it, or by the Court to which it is sent for execution" (s. 38). The decree-passing court may **transfer** the decree for execution to another court where the judgment-debtor resides or has property (s. 39), including a court in another province under its rules (s. 40).

### 3. What a precept is — s. 46

#### i) The provision

"Upon the application of the decree-holder the Court which passed the decree may, whenever it thinks fit, issue a precept to any other Court which would be competent to execute such decree to **attach** any property belonging to the judgment-debtor and specified in the precept" (s. 46(1)).

#### ii) Its limited life

"Provided that no attachment under a precept shall continue for more than **two months** unless the period of attachment is extended by an order of the Court which passed the decree or unless before the determination of such attachment the decree has been transferred to the Court by which the attachment has been made and the decree-holder has applied for an order for the sale of such property" (proviso to s. 46(2)).

#### iii) Purpose

A precept is an **emergency, protective** measure: it freezes the judgment-debtor's property in another district quickly, before he can dispose of it, while the formal transfer of the decree is arranged.

### 4. Powers of a transferee executing court — s. 42

"The Court executing a decree **sent to it** shall have the **same powers** in executing such decree **as if it had been passed by itself**" (s. 42(1)). Sub-section (2) lists powers such as further transfer (s. 39), execution against legal representatives (s. 50(1)), correction of clerical errors (s. 152), recognising an assignment of the decree (O. XXI r. 16) and notice of attachment of another decree (O. XXI r. 53(1)(b)). These powers belong to a court to which the **decree itself** has been sent under s. 39.

### 5. Answer to question (i) — the precept court's powers are limited

#### i) Conclusion

The court receiving a precept does **not** enjoy equal powers with the decree-passing court. Its only function is to **attach** the property specified in the precept, "in the manner prescribed in regard to the attachment of property in execution of a decree" (s. 46(2)). It cannot sell the property, decide objections on the merits of the decree, or exercise the general powers of s. 42 — unless and until the **decree is transferred** to it under s. 39.

#### ii) Reasoning

Section 42 speaks of "a decree **sent to it**" for execution; a precept is not the decree but a **direction to attach**. The precept court acts as the **agent** of the decree-passing court, which keeps control (it alone can extend the two-month attachment).

### 6. Section 12(2) — the provision

"Where a person challenges the validity of a judgment, decree or order on the plea of **fraud, mis-representation or want of jurisdiction**, he shall seek his remedy by making an application to **the Court which passed the final judgment, decree or order** and not by a separate suit" (s. 12(2), added by Ordinance X of 1980).

### 7. Meaning of "the Court which passed the final judgment, decree or order"

#### i) The test

The application lies to the court whose decision is **final** on the merits — the trial court, or, if its decree **merged** into an appellate or revisional decision on merits, the appellate / revisional court.

#### ii) Why not the executing court

An **executing** court — still less a precept court — does not pass the decree; it only **enforces** it. It cannot go behind the decree (s. 47 confines it to questions of execution, discharge and satisfaction). Section 12(2) attacks the **validity of the decree itself**; only the court that created it (or the final court) can undo it.

### 8. Rationale of the forum rule

The decree court has the **record** and knows the proceedings in which the fraud is alleged to have occurred; a single forum avoids **conflicting decisions** and prevents the judgment-debtor from choosing a convenient court far from the decree-holder.

### 9. Application to the facts

#### i) The decree

The decree was passed by the **Civil Court at Lahore**. No appeal is stated; Lahore's decree is the "final" decree.

#### ii) The precept

The Hyderabad court received only a **precept** to attach property under s. 46. It has not passed any judgment, decree or order on the merits.

#### iii) Result

The application under s. 12(2) lies before the **Civil Court at Lahore**, not Hyderabad. (If the Lahore decree had been appealed and decided on merits by the appellate court at Lahore, the application would lie to that appellate court.)

### 10. Remedy if filed at Hyderabad

If the judgment-debtor files the s. 12(2) application at Hyderabad, that court should **return** it for presentation to the proper court at Lahore (applying O. VII r. 10 through s. 141), not decide it. The Hyderabad court may, however, receive **objections to attachment** under O. XXI r. 58, which concern the attachment itself, not the decree's validity.

### 11. Conclusion

(i) A precept court has **limited, delegated power** to attach property for two months; it does not have the powers of the decree court or of a transferee court under s. 42 unless the decree is transferred to it. (ii) An application under **s. 12(2)** lies to the court that passed the **final** judgment or decree — on these facts, the **Civil Court at Lahore**.

## Why this answer scores

- It reads **ss. 38, 39, 42, 46 and 12(2)** together, which the question tests.
- It quotes the **two-month limit** in s. 46 and the "**sent to it**" wording of s. 42 — the textual keys to the answer.
- The reasoning on "**final** judgment" and **merger** shows depth.
- It gives a **clear, direct answer** to each part, as a judge would.

# Answer 6 · Amendment, rejection and return of plaint (SHC 2022 · KPHC 2026)

:::glance SINDH HIGH COURT 2022 · CIVIL LAW · Q. 3 · 20 MARKS
What are the grounds for the following: (a) amendment of plaint; (b) rejection of plaint; (c) return of plaint?
*(KP High Court 2026, Q. 2: "What is the difference between 'return of plaint' and 'rejection of plaint'? On what grounds court proceed in both cases? Elaborate.")*
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](a6_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Three tools to deal with a defective plaint: **amend** it (O. VI r. 17), **reject** it (O. VII r. 11) or **return** it (O. VII r. 10). For each: the provision, the **grounds**, the **principles** the courts apply, the **effect** and the **remedy**. The KP version adds "difference" — a comparison table answers it.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — three ways to deal with a defective plaint

A plaint (O. VII r. 1) begins the suit (s. 26). If it has a defect, the court may allow the plaintiff to **cure** it (amendment), **end** the suit at the threshold (rejection), or send the plaint to the **right court** (return). The choice depends on the nature of the defect.

### 2. Amendment of plaint — O. VI r. 17

#### i) The provision

"The Court may **at any stage** of the proceedings allow either party to alter or amend his pleadings in such manner and on such terms as may be just, and **all such amendments shall be made** as may be necessary for the purpose of determining the **real questions in controversy** between the parties."

#### ii) Grounds on which amendment is allowed

(1) It is **necessary to decide the real controversy**; (2) it causes **no injustice** to the other side that cannot be **compensated by costs**; (3) it is **bona fide**; (4) it avoids **multiplicity** of suits (e.g., adding a relief for possession after dispossession during the suit); (5) it corrects errors of description, dates or valuation.

#### iii) Grounds on which amendment is refused

(1) It **changes the nature of the suit** or sets up an entirely new and inconsistent case; (2) it would defeat a **right accrued by limitation**; (3) it **withdraws an admission** that benefits the other side; (4) it is **mala fide** or sought at a late stage merely to delay.

#### iv) Authority

*Pirgonda Hongonda Patil v. Kalgonda* (AIR 1957 SC 363): amendments should be allowed which are necessary to determine the real question and do not work injustice to the other side. *L.J. Leach & Co. v. Jardine Skinner* (AIR 1957 SC 357): even a time-barred amendment may be allowed in the interests of justice if the facts are already pleaded. The guiding maxim is that procedure is the **handmaid of justice** (*Imtiaz Ahmad v. Ghulam Ali*, PLD 1963 SC 382).

#### v) Time to amend — r. 18

If the party does not amend within the time fixed (or **fourteen days**), he cannot amend afterwards unless time is extended.

### 3. Rejection of plaint — O. VII r. 11

#### i) The four grounds

"The plaint shall be rejected in the following cases:— (a) where it does not disclose a **cause of action**; (b) where the relief claimed is **under valued**, and the plaintiff, on being required by the Court to correct the valuation within a time to be fixed by the Court, fails to do so; (c) where the relief claimed is properly valued, but the plaint is written upon paper **insufficiently stamped**, and the plaintiff, on being required … to supply the requisite stamp-paper …, fails to do so; (d) where the suit appears from the statement in the plaint to be **barred by any law**."

#### ii) Principles

(1) Only the **plaint** and documents annexed to it are considered; averments are **assumed true**; the written statement is irrelevant (*Saleem Bhai v. State of Maharashtra*, (2003) 1 SCC 557). (2) The plaint is read as a whole and meaningfully; **clever drafting** creating an illusion of a cause of action is to be "nipped in the bud" (*T. Arivandandam v. T.V. Satyapal*, (1977) 4 SCC 467). (3) The power may be exercised **at any stage**, even **suo motu**. (4) Under (b) and (c), the court must **first give time** to correct. (5) "Barred by any law" includes limitation, res judicata, s. 12(2) and statutory bars — if apparent from the plaint.

#### iii) Effect and remedy

The judge records an order with reasons (r. 12). Rejection is a **deemed decree** (s. 2(2)) — appealable under **s. 96**. Rejection does not of its own force bar a **fresh plaint** (r. 13).

### 4. Return of plaint — O. VII r. 10

#### i) The provision

"The plaint shall **at any stage** of the suit be returned to be presented to the Court in which the suit should have been instituted" (r. 10(1)). The judge endorses the dates of presentation and return, the name of the presenter and **brief reasons** (r. 10(2)).

#### ii) Grounds

The court **lacks territorial or pecuniary jurisdiction** (ss. 15–20 CPC; ss. 7, 9 Ordinance) — or, in some cases, subject-matter jurisdiction where another court can try the suit.

#### iii) Effect and remedy

The plaint is presented afresh in the proper court; time spent in the wrong court in **good faith** is excluded under **s. 14 of the Limitation Act**. An order returning a plaint is appealable under **O. XLIII r. 1(a)**.

### 5. Distinction — return and rejection

{w:22,39,39}
|Basis|Return (O. VII r. 10)|Rejection (O. VII r. 11)|
|Ground|Want of jurisdiction|No cause of action; undervaluation / stamp not cured; barred by law|
|Nature|**Order**|**Deemed decree**|
|Remedy|Appeal — O. XLIII r. 1(a)|Appeal — s. 96|
|Future|Same plaint in the proper court|Fresh plaint allowed (r. 13)|
|Limitation|s. 14 Limitation Act exclusion|No exclusion; must be within time|
|Merits|Not touched|Legal defect on the face of the plaint|

### 6. Relationship between the three

Amendment is the **first resort**: where a defect can be cured (e.g., undervaluation, a missing averment of cause of action, a misdescribed property), the court should allow amendment rather than reject. Rejection is a **drastic** remedy for incurable defects. Return is a **forum** remedy: the plaint is fine but in the wrong court.

### 7. Illustration

Nadia sues in the Civil Court, Islamabad (East), for possession of a flat in **Rawalpindi**, valuing the suit at Rs. 10,000 though the flat is worth Rs. 18 million. (1) The court first orders her to **correct the valuation** (r. 11(b)); (2) after correction, the court has no territorial jurisdiction because the property is in Rawalpindi (s. 16), so it **returns** the plaint (r. 10); (3) if her plaint had disclosed no right at all (for example, admitting that she had sold the flat), it would be **rejected** (r. 11(a)); (4) a wrong khasra number could be cured by **amendment** (O. VI r. 17).

### 8. Conclusion

The CPC gives the court graduated tools: **amend** to cure, **return** to redirect, **reject** to end a plaint that can never succeed. The overriding principle — reinforced in Islamabad by the primary objective in **s. 1(4)** — is to decide cases justly and on merits while preventing abuse of the court's process.

## Why this answer scores

- **All three** devices are covered with the **exact grounds** quoted from the bare Act.
- **Principles from case law** are given for amendment and rejection, not just the text.
- The **comparison table** answers the KP version of the question in seconds.
- The **single illustration** shows all three tools working on one set of facts.

# Answer 7 · Misjoinder, non-joinder; necessary and proper parties (KPHC 2026)

:::glance KP HIGH COURT 2026 · CIVIL LAW-I · Q. 3 · 20 MARKS
"No suit shall be defeated by reason of the misjoinder or non-joinder of parties, and the court may in every suit deal with the matter in controversy so far as regards the rights and interests of the parties actually before it." Explain how CPC, 1908, deals with different situations as to misjoinder, non-joinder, striking out, and proper and necessary parties to a suit.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](a7_map.png)

## Decoding the question

The quotation is **O. I r. 9**. The examiner wants the scheme of **Order I**: who may be joined (rr. 1, 3), what **misjoinder** and **non-joinder** mean, the **rule** that they do not defeat a suit, the **exception** for **necessary parties**, the power to **add or strike out** (r. 10), the time for **objections** (r. 13), and the **necessary vs proper** distinction with cases.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — the right parties for a complete decision

A suit must be framed "so as to afford ground for final decision upon the subjects in dispute and to prevent further litigation concerning them" (O. II r. 1). Order I tells us **who** must or may be before the court. The policy is to avoid **multiplicity of suits** while ensuring that no person is bound by a decree without being heard.

### 2. Joinder of plaintiffs and defendants — rr. 1 and 3

All persons may be joined as plaintiffs (r. 1) or defendants (r. 3) where (i) the right to relief arises from the **same act or transaction** or series of transactions, and (ii) a **common question of law or fact** would arise in separate suits. The court may order **separate trials** if joinder may embarrass or delay (r. 2); a defendant need not be interested in all reliefs (r. 5); a plaintiff in doubt may join defendants in the alternative (r. 7).

### 3. Misjoinder and non-joinder — meaning

#### i) Misjoinder

Joining a person who **ought not** to be a party — against whom no relief is claimed, or whose claim has no common transaction or question with the others.

#### ii) Non-joinder

**Omitting** a person who ought to have been joined.

### 4. The rule — O. I r. 9

"No suit shall be defeated by reason of the misjoinder or non-joinder of parties, and the Court may in every suit deal with the matter in controversy so far as regards the rights and interests of the parties actually before it." Defects of parties are **procedural**; the court corrects them (r. 10) and decides between those present.

### 5. The exception — non-joinder of a necessary party

The protection of r. 9 does **not** extend to a **necessary party**. If a person without whom no effective decree can be passed is not joined, and the plaintiff does not add him despite objection or opportunity, the suit **fails**. The court cannot pass a decree that binds or affects an absent person who is indispensable.

### 6. Necessary and proper parties

#### i) Necessary party

A person (a) against whom **some relief** is claimed in respect of the controversy, and (b) in whose absence **no effective decree** can be passed (*Kasturi v. Iyyamperumal*, (2005) 6 SCC 733). Examples: all **co-sharers** in a partition suit; the **vendee** in a suit to cancel a sale deed; the **principal** in a suit on a contract made by a disclosed agent.

#### ii) Proper party

A person whose presence is **not indispensable**, but who helps the court to **effectually and completely** adjudicate (O. I r. 10(2)) — e.g., a tenant in a suit for possession against the landlord-owner, or a subsequent purchaser with notice in a specific performance suit.

#### iii) The limit

A stranger claiming an **independent, adverse title** is neither necessary nor proper in a suit for specific performance, because the court will not convert a contract suit into a title suit (*Kasturi*). A person with only an **indirect or commercial** interest is not added (*Razia Begum v. Sahebzadi Anwar Begum*, AIR 1958 SC 886).

### 7. Striking out and adding parties — O. I r. 10

#### i) Wrong plaintiff — r. 10(1)

Where a suit is filed in the name of the wrong plaintiff through a **bona fide mistake**, the court may substitute or add the right plaintiff at any stage.

#### ii) The general power — r. 10(2)

"The Court may **at any stage** of the proceedings, either upon or without the application of either party, … order that the name of any party improperly joined … be **struck out**, and that the name of any person who ought to have been joined … or whose presence before the Court may be necessary in order to enable the Court effectually and completely to adjudicate upon and settle all the questions involved in the suit, be **added**."

#### iii) Safeguards

No one is added as a plaintiff **without his consent** (r. 10(3)); the plaint is **amended** and served on the added defendant (r. 10(4)); for **limitation**, the suit against an added defendant begins when he is **made a party** (r. 10(5) with s. 22 Limitation Act) — so a time-barred claim is not revived by adding him.

### 8. Objections — O. I r. 13

"All objections on the ground of non-joinder or misjoinder of parties shall be taken at the **earliest possible opportunity** and, in all cases where issues are settled, **at or before such settlement**, … and any such objection not so taken shall be deemed to have been **waived**." (Non-joinder of a necessary party, however, goes to the root and can be noticed later, because no effective decree is possible.)

### 9. Related rules

- **Representative suits** — O. I r. 8: numerous persons with the same interest; permission and notice; decree binds all (s. 11 Expl. VI).
- **Misjoinder of causes of action** — O. II rr. 3, 6, 7: separate trials; objection before issues.
- **Appeal** — no decree is reversed for misjoinder not affecting the merits or jurisdiction (s. 99).

### 10. Illustration

Shazia sues her brother Kamran alone for **partition** of their late father's house in G-6, omitting their two sisters. Kamran objects in his written statement. **Non-joinder of necessary parties**: no effective partition can be made without all co-sharers. The court should **add the sisters** under r. 10(2), even suo motu; if Shazia refuses to implead them, the suit must fail. If she had also joined her father's former tenant (no claim against him), that is **misjoinder**; his name will be **struck out** — the suit is not defeated (r. 9).

### 11. Conclusion

Order I combines **flexibility** with **fairness**: wrong or missing parties do not defeat a suit (r. 9), because the court can add or strike out parties at any stage (r. 10); objections must be taken early (r. 13). But the flexibility stops where justice requires a person's presence: without a **necessary party** no effective decree can be passed. The distinction between necessary and proper parties, settled in *Kasturi* and *Razia Begum*, is the key to applying r. 9 correctly.

## Why this answer scores

- It identifies the quotation as **O. I r. 9** and immediately states the **exception** — the heart of the question.
- **Every situation in the question** is covered: misjoinder, non-joinder, striking out, necessary and proper parties.
- Two **leading cases** give the tests for necessary parties and the limits of joinder.
- The **limitation point** (r. 10(5) and s. 22 Limitation Act) shows depth.
