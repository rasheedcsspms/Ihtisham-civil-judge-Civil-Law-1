# Chapter 10 · Parties to suits (Order I)

## 10.1 Who can be joined as plaintiffs and defendants

> "1. All persons may be joined in one suit as plaintiffs in whom any right to relief in respect of, or arising out of, the same act or transaction or series of acts or transactions is alleged to exist, whether jointly, severally or in the alternative, where, if such persons brought separate suits, any common question of law or fact would arise." (O. I r. 1)

> "3. All persons may be joined as defendants against whom any right to relief in respect of, or arising out of, the same act or transaction or series of acts or transactions is alleged to exist, whether jointly, severally or in the alternative, where, if separate suits were brought against such persons, any common question of law or fact would arise." (O. I r. 3)

**Two tests for joinder (plaintiffs or defendants):**

1. the right to relief arises out of the **same act or transaction** (or series of acts or transactions); **and**
2. a **common question of law or fact** would arise if separate suits were filed.

:::why Why allow joinder?
To avoid **multiplicity of suits** and **conflicting decisions**, and to save time and costs: one trial, one set of witnesses, one decision binding everybody concerned. Order II r. 1 states the same aim — every suit should be framed "so as to afford ground for final decision upon the subjects in dispute and to prevent further litigation concerning them."
:::

Other rules:

- **r. 2** — if joinder of plaintiffs may embarrass or delay the trial, the court may order **separate trials**.
- **r. 4** — judgment may be given for or against one or more of joint parties according to their rights / liabilities.
- **r. 5** — a defendant need not be interested in all the reliefs claimed.
- **r. 6** — the plaintiff may join all or any of the persons liable on the same contract (including parties to bills of exchange).
- **r. 7** — where the plaintiff is **in doubt** from whom he is entitled to redress, he may join two or more defendants so that the question of which of them is liable is decided.
- **r. 11** — the court may give the **conduct of the suit** to whichever person it considers proper.
- **r. 12** — one of several plaintiffs / defendants may be authorised by the others (in writing) to appear for them.

## 10.2 Necessary and proper parties

This distinction is not defined in the Code but has been developed by the courts. It is asked very often (LHC 2020; KP 2026).

{w:22,39,39}
|Point|Necessary party|Proper party|
|Meaning|A person **without whom no effective decree can be passed at all**|A person whose presence is **not indispensable** but who should be before the court to enable it to **effectually and completely** decide all questions|
|Test|(1) There must be a right to some relief against him; (2) no effective decree can be passed in his absence|His presence helps a complete and final adjudication; a decree can still be passed without him|
|Effect of absence|Suit **fails** (cannot be decreed) if he is not joined even after objection / opportunity|Suit does **not** fail; decree binds only parties present|
|Example|In a suit for partition, **all co-sharers**; in a suit to cancel a sale deed, the **vendee**; in a suit against a firm's property, the partners or firm|In a suit for specific performance of an agreement to sell, a person who claims to be a **subsequent purchaser** may be a proper party; a tenant in a suit for possession against the owner|

:::case Kasturi v. Iyyamperumal ((2005) 6 SCC 733)
__Facts.__ In a suit for specific performance of an agreement to sell, a third party who claimed **independent title and possession** of the land (not through the seller) applied to be added as a defendant.

__Held.__ The two tests for a **necessary party** are: (1) there must be a right to some relief against such party in respect of the controversy involved; and (2) no effective decree can be passed in the absence of such party. In a suit for specific performance, the necessary parties are the parties to the contract (and those who purchased from the seller with notice). A stranger claiming an **independent, adverse title** is not a necessary or even a proper party, because the plaintiff cannot be forced to fight a title dispute in a suit on contract — the scope of the suit cannot be enlarged into a title suit.

__Ratio.__ Courts add parties to decide the controversy **in the suit**, not to convert a suit into a different kind of litigation.
:::

:::case Razia Begum v. Sahebzadi Anwar Begum (AIR 1958 SC 886)
__Held.__ In a suit for **declaration of status** (a declaration that the plaintiff is the wife of the defendant), the court has discretion to add as a party a person who has a **direct interest** in the subject matter — i.e., whose legal rights would be affected by the declaration — but not one who has only a commercial or indirect interest. The power under O. I r. 10(2) is discretionary and must be exercised judicially.

__Ratio.__ "Interest" for joinder means a **legal** interest in the subject matter, not mere curiosity or a commercial stake in the outcome.
:::

## 10.3 Misjoinder and non-joinder — O. I r. 9

> "9. No suit shall be defeated by reason of the misjoinder or non-joinder of parties, and the Court may in every suit deal with the matter in controversy so far as regards the rights and interests of the parties actually before it."

- **Misjoinder** — a person who should not be a party has been joined (e.g., a person against whom no relief is claimed, or causes of action against different defendants that have nothing in common).
- **Non-joinder** — a person who should have been joined has been left out.

### The rule and its limit

The general rule is that misjoinder or non-joinder does **not** defeat a suit; the court can **add or strike out** parties under r. 10(2) and decide the suit between those present.

**But** — this rule does not save a suit where a **necessary party** is left out and is **not added** despite objection or opportunity. Without the necessary party no effective decree can be passed; the suit must fail. Pakistani superior courts consistently apply this distinction: non-joinder of a *proper* party is not fatal; non-joinder of a *necessary* party is fatal unless cured.

### Objections — O. I r. 13

> "13. All objections on the ground of non-joinder or misjoinder of parties shall be taken at the earliest possible opportunity and, in all cases where issues are settled, at or before such settlement, unless the ground of objection has subsequently arisen, and any such objection not so taken shall be deemed to have been waived."

(The waiver applies to technical objections. Non-joinder of a **necessary** party goes to the root and can be noticed by the court even later, because no effective decree can be passed.)

## 10.4 Adding, striking out or substituting parties — O. I r. 10

- **r. 10(1) — Wrong plaintiff:** where a suit has been filed in the name of the wrong person as plaintiff (or it is doubtful), the court may at **any stage**, if satisfied that it was a **bona fide mistake** and that it is necessary for determining the real matter in dispute, substitute or add any other person as plaintiff.
- **r. 10(2) — Adding or striking out any party:** the court may at **any stage**, on application or **of its own motion**, strike out the name of any party improperly joined, and add any person "who ought to have been joined … or whose presence before the Court may be necessary in order to enable the Court effectually and completely to adjudicate upon and settle all the questions involved in the suit".
- **r. 10(3):** no one can be added as a plaintiff (or as next friend of a plaintiff) **without his consent**.
- **r. 10(4):** where a defendant is added, the plaint shall be amended and copies of the amended plaint and summons served on the new defendant.
- **r. 10(5): limitation** — subject to **s. 22 of the Limitation Act**, proceedings against an added defendant are deemed to begin only **on service of summons** on him. (Under s. 22 Limitation Act, the suit is deemed instituted against the new party on the day he is **made a party**.) So adding a defendant after the limitation period cannot revive a time-barred claim against him.

:::maxim Dominus litis — but within limits
The plaintiff is the master of his suit and normally chooses whom to sue. But under O. I r. 10(2) the court may add a party even against the plaintiff's wishes if that party is **necessary** for a complete decision. The plaintiff cannot be compelled to claim relief against a person he does not wish to sue; if a necessary party is not added, he bears the risk of failure.
:::

## 10.5 Representative suits — O. I r. 8

> "8. (1) Where there are numerous persons having the same interest in one suit, one or more of such persons may, with the permission of the Court, sue or be sued, or may defend, in such suit, on behalf of or for the benefit of all persons so interested. But the Court shall in such case give, at the plaintiff's expense, notice of the institution of the suit to all such persons either by personal service or, where … such service is not reasonably practicable, by public advertisement … (2) Any person on whose behalf or for whose benefit a suit is instituted or defended under sub-rule (1) may apply to the Court to be made a party to such suit."

**Conditions:** (1) numerous persons; (2) the **same interest**; (3) **permission** of the court; (4) **notice** to all interested persons (personal or by advertisement).

**Effect:** the decree binds all persons represented (s. 11 Explanation VI), provided the suit was conducted bona fide.

:::eg Representative suit
Residents of Street 12, Sector G-10/2, want to stop a neighbour from converting a residential house into a marriage hall, which blocks the street. Two residents, with the court's permission and after public notice, sue on behalf of all 60 residents. The decree binds all of them; a resident who did not join cannot later sue on the same right (Explanation VI to s. 11). (A suit about a **public nuisance** affecting the general public is governed by **s. 91** — Advocate General, or two or more persons **with the leave of the Court**.)
:::

:::recap Chapter 10 in six lines
- Joinder test: **same transaction** + **common question** (O. I rr. 1, 3); separate trials possible (r. 2).
- **Necessary party**: no effective decree without him (right to relief + effective decree tests — *Kasturi*); **proper party**: presence helps complete adjudication.
- **r. 9:** misjoinder / non-joinder does not defeat a suit — **except** non-joinder of a necessary party not cured.
- **r. 10:** wrong plaintiff (bona fide mistake); add or strike out at any stage, even *suo motu*; consent for adding plaintiff; amended plaint served; limitation runs from the date of addition (s. 22 Limitation Act).
- **r. 13:** objections at earliest opportunity, before issues, else waived.
- **r. 8:** representative suit — numerous persons, same interest, permission, notice; decree binds all (s. 11 Expl. VI).
:::

# Chapter 11 · Frame of suit, agents and institution (Orders II, III and IV)

## 11.1 The whole claim in one suit — O. II r. 2

> "2. (1) Every suit shall include the whole of the claim which the plaintiff is entitled to make in respect of the cause of action; but a plaintiff may relinquish any portion of his claim in order to bring the suit within the jurisdiction of any Court. (2) Where a plaintiff omits to sue in respect of, or intentionally relinquishes, any portion of his claim, he shall not afterwards sue in respect of the portion so omitted or relinquished. (3) A person entitled to more than one relief in respect of the same cause of action may sue for all or any of such reliefs; but if he omits, except with the leave of the Court, to sue for all such reliefs, he shall not afterwards sue for any relief so omitted.
> Explanation.— For the purposes of this rule an obligation and a collateral security for its performance and successive claims arising under the same obligation shall be deemed respectively to constitute but one cause of action."

*Illustration (from the Code):* A lets a house to B at a yearly rent of Rs. 1,200. Rent for 1905, 1906 and 1907 is unpaid. A sues B in 1908 only for the rent of 1906. A shall not afterwards sue for the rent due for 1905 or 1907.

### Conditions for the bar

1. The **second suit** is based on the **same cause of action** as the first.
2. In the first suit the plaintiff **omitted or relinquished** part of his claim, or omitted a relief.
3. For an omitted relief — he did so **without the leave of the court**.

:::case Mohd. Khalil Khan v. Mahbub Ali Mian (AIR 1949 PC 78)
__Held.__ The Privy Council laid down the tests for O. II r. 2: (1) the correct test is whether the claim in the new suit is in fact founded upon a cause of action **distinct** from that which was the foundation of the former suit; (2) the "cause of action" means every fact which it would be necessary for the plaintiff to prove, if traversed, to support his right to judgment; (3) if the evidence to support the two claims is different, the causes of action are different; (4) the causes of action in the two suits may be considered the same if **in substance** they are identical; (5) the cause of action has no relation to the defence or the relief prayed for — it refers to the **media** on which the plaintiff asks the court to arrive at a conclusion in his favour.

__Ratio.__ O. II r. 2 bars a second suit only when the **cause of action is identical**. Different causes of action — even between the same parties and about the same property — may be sued upon separately.
:::

:::case Gurbux Singh v. Bhooralal (AIR 1964 SC 1810)
__Held.__ A plea under O. II r. 2 must be **proved**: the defendant must produce the **pleadings** of the former suit to show the identity of the causes of action. The court will not presume it.
:::

:::why Why O. II r. 2?
To stop **splitting of claims** and **double harassment**: a defendant should face all claims arising from one cause of action in one suit. It also stops a plaintiff from deliberately under-claiming to choose a lower court and then suing again for the balance.
:::

## 11.2 Joinder of causes of action — O. II rr. 3 to 7

- **r. 3(1):** a plaintiff may unite several causes of action against the **same defendant(s) jointly**; plaintiffs **jointly interested** may unite causes of action against the same defendant(s).
- **r. 3(2):** where causes are united, **jurisdiction depends on the aggregate value** at the date of the suit.
- **r. 4:** in a suit for **recovery of immovable property**, no other cause of action shall be joined without the court's leave, **except** (a) mesne profits or arrears of rent for that property, (b) damages for breach of the contract under which the property is held, and (c) claims based on the same cause of action. (A mortgagor or mortgagee in a foreclosure or redemption suit may ask for possession.)
- **r. 5:** claims by or against an executor, administrator or heir **as such** shall not be joined with claims by or against him **personally**, unless connected with the estate.
- **r. 6:** the court may order **separate trials** if causes cannot be conveniently tried together.
- **r. 7:** objections to **misjoinder of causes of action** — at the earliest opportunity, before settlement of issues, else **waived**.

:::eg Multifariousness
Saima sues (i) her tenant for arrears of rent of a flat in F-11 and (ii) an unrelated contractor for defective construction of a different house, in one suit. There is no common transaction or common question and the defendants are different — this is **misjoinder of parties and causes of action** (multifariousness). The court may order separate trials or ask her to elect; it will not dismiss the suit outright (O. I r. 9; O. II r. 6), and objections not taken before issues are waived (O. II r. 7).
:::

## 11.3 Recognised agents and pleaders — Order III

- **r. 1:** any appearance, application or act may be made or done by the party **in person**, by his **recognised agent**, or by a **pleader** — except where the law requires personal appearance.
- **r. 2 — recognised agents:** (a) persons holding a **power of attorney** authorising them to make appearances, applications and do acts on behalf of the party; (b) persons carrying on trade or business for parties not resident within the court's limits, in matters connected with that trade.
- **r. 3:** process served on a recognised agent is as effective as on the party.
- **r. 4:** a pleader's appointment must be in **writing** (vakalatnama), signed by the party and filed in court; it continues until revoked or the client / pleader dies, and covers all proceedings in the suit.
- **r. 5:** process served on the pleader is as effective as on the party.
- **r. 6:** a person may be appointed specially to accept service.

:::tip Power of attorney and evidence
A recognised agent (attorney) can file the suit, sign and verify pleadings (O. VI rr. 14–15, if acquainted with the facts) and conduct the case. But he can depose as a **witness** only about facts within **his own knowledge**; he cannot give evidence on the principal's behalf about facts only the principal knows. This is a frequent point in judgments on suits filed through overseas Pakistanis' attorneys.
:::

## 11.4 Institution of suits — Order IV

- **r. 1(1):** every suit shall be instituted by **presenting a plaint** to the court or an officer appointed by it.
- **r. 1(2):** every plaint shall comply with the rules in Orders VI and VII so far as applicable.
- **r. 2:** the court shall enter the particulars of every suit in a **register of civil suits**, numbered in each year in order of admission.

:::recap Chapter 11 in five lines
- **O. II r. 2:** whole claim in one suit; omitted portion or relief (without leave) cannot be sued for later — same cause of action required (*Mohd. Khalil Khan*); plead and prove former pleadings (*Gurbux Singh*).
- **O. II r. 3:** joinder of causes of action; jurisdiction on aggregate value.
- **O. II r. 4:** suits for immovable property — only mesne profits / rent, damages for breach of tenancy contract, same-cause claims, unless leave.
- **O. II r. 7:** misjoinder objections early, else waived.
- **O. III:** appearance in person, through recognised agent (power of attorney) or pleader (written appointment); **O. IV:** suit begins by presenting a plaint; register of suits.
:::

# Chapter 12 · Summons and service (Order V)

## 12.1 Why service matters

Service of summons is the **legal notice** that tells the defendant he has been sued and when to appear. Without proper service, any ex parte proceeding violates *audi alteram partem* and can be set aside (O. IX r. 13). Many judgments in Pakistan turn on whether service was proper.

## 12.2 The summons — rr. 1 to 8

- **r. 1(1):** when a suit has been duly instituted, a summons may be issued to the defendant to **appear and answer the claim** on a specified day — but not if the defendant appeared at presentation of the plaint and **admitted** the claim.
- **r. 1(2):** the defendant may appear (a) **in person**, (b) by a pleader duly instructed and able to answer all material questions, or (c) by a pleader accompanied by a person able to answer.
- **r. 1(3):** every summons is **signed** by the judge or authorised officer and **sealed**.
- **r. 2:** the summons is accompanied by a **copy of the plaint** (or a concise statement if permitted).
- **r. 3–4:** the court may order a party to appear **in person**, but only if he resides within the local limits, or within a specified distance (with exceptions).
- **r. 5:** the court determines whether the summons is for **settlement of issues** only, or for **final disposal**.
- **rr. 7–8:** the summons directs the defendant to produce the documents on which he relies; if for final disposal, to produce his witnesses.

## 12.3 Modes of service — rr. 9 to 30

![Modes of service of summons under Order V](service_modes.png)

1. **Personal / direct service** (rr. 10–16): by delivering or tendering a copy to the **defendant in person** wherever practicable, or to his **agent** empowered to accept service (r. 12); to the **manager or agent** carrying on business for him (r. 13); to the agent in charge of immovable property (r. 14); where the defendant is absent and there is no agent — to an **adult male member** of his family residing with him (r. 15 — not a servant). The person served signs an acknowledgment (r. 16).
2. **Service by post** (r. 10-A, Pakistani addition of 1972): **simultaneously** with the issue of summons, another copy is sent by **registered post, acknowledgment due**. An acknowledgment purporting to be signed by the defendant, or the postal employee's endorsement that he **refused** delivery, is **prima facie proof** of service.
3. **Affixation** (r. 17): if the defendant refuses to sign or cannot be found after **due and reasonable diligence**, and there is no agent or adult male member, the serving officer **affixes** a copy on the outer door or a conspicuous part of the house where the defendant ordinarily resides, carries on business or works for gain, and reports the circumstances and the names of the identifiers. Under **r. 19** the court examines the serving officer (or relies on his affidavit) and declares whether service was due.
4. **Substituted service** (r. 20) — ordered **by the court** when it is satisfied that the defendant is keeping out of the way to avoid service, or the summons cannot be served in the ordinary way. In Pakistan the court may order service by: (a) affixing at the house where the defendant last resided or worked; (b) **any electronic device of communication**, including telegram, telephone, phonogram, telex, fax, radio and television; (c) **urgent mail service or public courier**; (d) **beat of drum** in the locality; (e) **publication in the press**; or (f) **any other manner** the court thinks fit — and may use all or any of them **simultaneously**. Substituted service is "as effectual as if it had been made on the defendant personally" (r. 20(2)); the court fixes time to appear, **not ordinarily exceeding fifteen days** (r. 20(3)).
5. **Special cases:** service through another court where the defendant resides elsewhere (r. 21); in prison (r. 24); out of Pakistan with no agent — by post (r. 25); through political agents or foreign courts (r. 26); on public officers through the head of office (r. 27); on soldiers, sailors and airmen through the commanding officer (r. 28).
6. **Letter instead of summons** (r. 30): the court may substitute a letter for a summons for persons of rank who are entitled to such consideration.

:::why Why so many modes?
The aim is to make sure the defendant **actually knows** of the suit, while preventing a defendant from **defeating justice by hiding**. Personal service is the best evidence of knowledge; post and courier are reliable and quick; substituted service is the safety valve against evasion. Pakistan's additions (post, courier, electronic means, beat of drum) reflect modern communication and local practice.
:::

:::trap Proper service is a question of fact
Affixation (r. 17) without **diligent efforts** to find the defendant, without names of identifiers, or without the court's declaration under r. 19, is **not** due service. Publication in a newspaper with little local circulation may be held insufficient. In an exam problem on an ex parte decree, examine **how** service was effected before deciding whether the decree should be set aside.
:::

:::recap Chapter 12 in five lines
- Summons signed and sealed, with copy of plaint; to appear and answer — for issues or for final disposal.
- Personal service (defendant, agent, manager, adult male family member) → acknowledgment.
- **r. 10-A:** simultaneous **registered post A/D**; acknowledgment or refusal endorsement = prima facie service.
- **r. 17:** affixation after due diligence; **r. 19:** examination of serving officer and declaration.
- **r. 20 (Pakistan):** substituted service — affixation, electronic means, courier, beat of drum, press, any other mode, simultaneously; time to appear ordinarily ≤ 15 days.
:::

# Chapter 13 · Pleadings generally (Order VI)

## 13.1 What pleadings are and why they matter

> "1. 'Pleading' shall mean plaint or written statement." (O. VI r. 1)

Pleadings are the written statements of each party's case. They fix the **battlefield** of the suit: the court decides only the questions raised in the pleadings, and **no party can lead evidence on a case not pleaded** (*secundum allegata et probata* — a decision must be according to what is alleged and proved).

:::maxim Secundum allegata et probata — according to what is alleged and proved
A court decides a case on the basis of what the parties have **pleaded** and **proved**. Evidence beyond the pleadings is ignored, and a party cannot be given relief on a case he never set up. This is why careful pleading is essential.
:::

:::case Ram Sarup Gupta v. Bishun Narain Inter College (AIR 1987 SC 1242)
__Held.__ The object of pleadings is to give fair notice to each party of the case of the opponent so that he may meet it, and to define the issues. But pleadings should receive a **liberal construction**; no pedantic approach should be adopted to defeat justice on hair-splitting technicalities. If the parties knew the case and led evidence on it, the absence of a specific plea is not fatal.

__Ratio.__ Pleadings are read as a whole and in substance; the real test is whether the other side had **fair notice** and was **not taken by surprise**.
:::

## 13.2 The fundamental rule — r. 2

> "2. Every pleading shall contain, and contain only, a statement in a concise form of the material facts on which the party pleading relies for his claim or defence, as the case may be, but not the evidence by which they are to be proved, and shall, when necessary, be divided into paragraphs, numbered consecutively. Dates, sums and numbers shall be expressed in figures."

The four golden rules of pleading:

1. **Plead facts, not law.** State the facts; the court applies the law. (A *mixed* plea — e.g., "the suit is barred by limitation" — is allowed because it rests on facts.)
2. **Plead material facts only** — those necessary to make out the cause of action or the defence.
3. **Do not plead evidence** — state *that* the defendant agreed to sell, not *who saw it* and *what the witness will say*.
4. **Be concise** — numbered paragraphs, figures for dates and sums.

## 13.3 Other rules of pleading

- **r. 4 — Particulars:** where a party relies on **misrepresentation, fraud, breach of trust, wilful default or undue influence**, **particulars** (with dates and items) must be stated. A bald allegation of "fraud" is not enough.
- **r. 5:** the court may order a **further and better statement** or particulars.
- **r. 6 — Condition precedent:** its performance need not be pleaded; the party who says it was **not** performed must specify that.
- **r. 7 — Departure:** no pleading shall, **except by amendment**, raise a new ground of claim or allegation inconsistent with the party's previous pleadings.
- **r. 8 — Denial of contract:** a bare denial is a denial of the **making** of the contract, not of its legality or sufficiency.
- **r. 9:** the effect of a document is stated briefly, not set out in full.
- **r. 10:** malice, fraudulent intention, knowledge may be alleged as a fact without setting out the circumstances.
- **r. 11:** notice may be alleged as a fact.
- **r. 12:** an implied contract or relation may be alleged as a fact, with the circumstances from which it is inferred.
- **r. 13 — Presumptions of law:** need not be pleaded (e.g., consideration for a negotiable instrument — s. 118 Negotiable Instruments Act), unless specifically denied.
- **r. 14 — Signing:** every pleading is signed by the party **and** his pleader (if any); if the party cannot sign, by a person duly authorised.
- **r. 15 — Verification:** verified **on oath or solemn affirmation** (Pakistani addition, 1972) by the party or a person acquainted with the facts, stating which paragraphs are from **own knowledge** and which on **information believed to be true**, with date and place.
- **r. 16 — Striking out:** the court may strike out or amend matter that is **unnecessary, scandalous**, or tends to **prejudice, embarrass or delay** the fair trial.

## 13.4 Amendment of pleadings — rr. 17 and 18

> "17. The Court may at any stage of the proceedings allow either party to alter or amend his pleadings in such manner and on such terms as may be just, and all such amendments shall be made as may be necessary for the purpose of determining the real questions in controversy between the parties."

> "18. If a party who has obtained an order for leave to amend does not amend accordingly within the time limited for that purpose by the order, or if no time is thereby limited then within fourteen days from the date of the order, he shall not be permitted to amend after the expiration of such limited time … unless the time is extended by the Court."

(The Indian proviso of 2002 barring amendment after trial begins does **not** exist in the Pakistani / Islamabad text.)

### Two parts of r. 17

1. **Discretionary part** — "may at any stage … allow … on such terms as may be just".
2. **Mandatory part** — "all such amendments **shall** be made as may be necessary for the purpose of determining the **real questions in controversy**".

### Principles applied by the courts

**Amendment is ordinarily allowed** if:

- it is necessary to decide the **real controversy**;
- it does not cause **injustice** to the other side that cannot be compensated by **costs**;
- it is made in **good faith**.

**Amendment is ordinarily refused** if:

1. it **changes the nature or character of the suit** fundamentally, or introduces a **totally new and inconsistent case** (e.g., converting a suit for specific performance into a suit for recovery of title on a different basis);
2. it would **take away a legal right accrued** to the other side by lapse of **limitation** (e.g., adding a new claim that is now time-barred) — though even this may be allowed in special circumstances where the facts were already pleaded;
3. it **withdraws an admission** that gives the other side a valuable right (withdrawal of admissions is viewed very strictly);
4. it is **mala fide**, or sought at a very late stage merely to **delay**;
5. it is not necessary for deciding the real question.

:::case L.J. Leach & Co. v. Jardine Skinner & Co. (AIR 1957 SC 357)
__Held.__ The court has power to allow an amendment even after the claim has become **time-barred**, if it is required **in the interests of justice**, where the facts on which the new relief is based were already **in the plaint**. Limitation is a factor to be considered, not an absolute bar.
:::

:::case Pirgonda Hongonda Patil v. Kalgonda Shidgonda Patil (AIR 1957 SC 363)
__Held.__ All amendments ought to be allowed which satisfy the two conditions: (a) of **not working injustice** to the other side, and (b) of being **necessary for the purpose of determining the real questions** in controversy between the parties. Amendments should be refused only where the other party cannot be placed in the same position as if the pleading had been originally correct, but the amendment would cause him an injury which could not be compensated in costs.
:::

:::case Revajeetu Builders v. Narayanaswamy & Sons ((2009) 10 SCC 84)
__Held.__ The Supreme Court summarised the factors: (1) whether the amendment is imperative for proper and effective adjudication; (2) whether it is bona fide or mala fide; (3) whether it causes prejudice that cannot be compensated in money; (4) whether refusing it would lead to injustice or multiple litigation; (5) whether it **constitutionally or fundamentally changes the nature and character** of the case; (6) whether, as a general rule, a fresh suit on the amended claims would be **time-barred** on the date of the application.
:::

:::maxim Ex debito justitiae — as a matter of right in justice
Amendments that are necessary to decide the real controversy and cause no uncompensable injustice should be allowed as a matter of course; costs are the usual price for the opponent's inconvenience.
:::

:::eg Amendment — allowed or not?
(1) Plaint in a recovery suit wrongly states the loan was given on 12.03.2024; the receipt shows 12.03.2023 → **allowed** (clerical correction; real controversy).
(2) In a suit for permanent injunction, plaintiff seeks to add a prayer for **possession** because he was dispossessed **during** the suit → usually **allowed** (subsequent event; avoids multiplicity; O. VI r. 17 and O. VII r. 7).
(3) Defendant admitted execution of an agreement in his written statement and, after two years of trial, seeks to amend to deny execution → **refused** (withdrawal of a clear admission; prejudice).
(4) Plaintiff sued for specific performance of an agreement dated 2019; four years later he seeks to add a claim for **damages** for breach of a separate agreement of 2018 → **refused** (new cause of action; time-barred; changes the nature of the suit).
:::

:::recap Chapter 13 in six lines
- Pleading = plaint or written statement; decide only on what is **pleaded and proved** (*secundum allegata et probata*), read liberally (*Ram Sarup Gupta*).
- r. 2: **material facts**, not law, not evidence; concise, numbered paragraphs.
- r. 4: **particulars** of fraud, misrepresentation, undue influence etc.; r. 7: no departure except by amendment.
- rr. 14–15: signed by party and pleader; verified on oath, own knowledge vs information.
- r. 16: strike out unnecessary / scandalous / embarrassing matter.
- r. 17: amendment at any stage — real controversy, no uncompensable injustice, bona fide; refused if it changes the nature of the suit, defeats accrued limitation, withdraws admissions, or is mala fide (*Leach*, *Pirgonda*, *Revajeetu*). r. 18: amend within time fixed or 14 days.
:::

# Chapter 14 · The plaint (Order VII)

## 14.1 Contents of a plaint — rr. 1 to 8

> "1. The plaint shall contain the following particulars:— (a) the name of the Court in which the suit is brought; (b) the name, description and place of residence of the plaintiff; (c) the name, description and place of residence of the defendant, so far as they can be ascertained; (d) where the plaintiff or the defendant is a minor or a person of unsound mind, a statement to that effect; (e) the facts constituting the cause of action and when it arose; (f) the facts showing that the Court has jurisdiction; (g) the relief which the plaintiff claims; (h) where the plaintiff has allowed a set-off or relinquished a portion of his claim, the amount so allowed or relinquished; and (i) a statement of the value of the subject-matter of the suit for the purposes of jurisdiction and of court-fees, so far as the case admits."

- **r. 2 — money suits:** state the **precise amount** claimed (approximate if mesne profits or unsettled accounts).
- **r. 3 — immovable property:** a description sufficient to identify it — boundaries or survey / khasra numbers.
- **r. 4 — representative capacity:** show the plaintiff's actual interest and that he has taken steps to sue in that capacity.
- **r. 5:** show that the **defendant is interested** in the subject matter and liable to be called upon to answer.
- **r. 6 — limitation:** if the suit is filed after the limitation period, show the **ground of exemption** from limitation.
- **r. 7 — relief:** state the relief **specifically**, simply or in the alternative; general relief need not be asked (the court may grant relief to which the plaintiff is entitled on the pleadings, but not beyond the case pleaded).
- **r. 8:** reliefs founded on **separate and distinct grounds** must be stated separately.
- **r. 9:** list of documents; copies of the plaint for each defendant plus two extra; draft summons and process fee.
- **rr. 14–18 — documents:** a document **sued upon** must be produced with the plaint (r. 14(1)); others relied on must be **listed** (r. 14(2)); a document that should have been produced or listed but was not "shall not, without the leave of the Court, be received in evidence" (r. 18) — except documents used for cross-examination or to refresh memory.
- **rr. 19–26 (Pakistani, 1972):** an **address for service** must be filed with the plaint, within the local limits of the district where the suit is filed or where the party ordinarily resides; failure — the court may, after an opportunity, dismiss the suit (r. 21, as amended by SRO 579(I)/2014); service at that address is effective (r. 22: affixing and registered post); change of address by verified petition (r. 24); list of **legal representatives** in certain suits (r. 26).

## 14.2 Return of plaint — r. 10

> "10. (1) The plaint shall at any stage of the suit be returned to be presented to the Court in which the suit should have been instituted. (2) On returning a plaint the Judge shall endorse thereon the date of its presentation and return, the name of the party presenting it, and a brief statement of the reasons for returning it."

**When:** the court has **no jurisdiction** — territorial or pecuniary (or, in some cases, subject-matter, where another court can try it) — and the plaint should be presented to the court that has.

**Effect:**
- The plaint is returned, **not** rejected; the plaintiff presents it to the **proper court**.
- In the proper court, it is a **fresh institution** — the suit is treated as instituted on the date of re-presentation (subject to the court's discretion on prior proceedings).
- **Limitation:** the time spent prosecuting the suit in the wrong court **with due diligence and in good faith** is **excluded** under **s. 14 of the Limitation Act 1908**.
- **Appeal:** an order returning a plaint is **appealable** under **O. XLIII r. 1(a)**.

## 14.3 Rejection of plaint — r. 11

> "11. The plaint shall be rejected in the following cases:— (a) where it does not disclose a cause of action; (b) where the relief claimed is under valued, and the plaintiff, on being required by the Court to correct the valuation within a time to be fixed by the Court, fails to do so; (c) where the relief claimed is properly valued, but the plaint is written upon paper insufficiently stamped, and the plaintiff, on being required by the Court to supply the requisite stamp-paper within a time to be fixed by the Court, fails to do so; (d) where the suit appears from the statement in the plaint to be barred by any law."

- **r. 12:** the judge records an **order with reasons**.
- **r. 13:** rejection on these grounds does **not by itself** bar a **fresh plaint** on the same cause of action (subject to limitation and to the bar of the law that caused rejection).
- The rejection of a plaint is a **deemed decree** (s. 2(2)) → **appeal under s. 96**.

### Principles of O. VII r. 11 (settled law)

1. The court looks **only at the plaint** (and the documents filed with it, read as part of it), assuming the averments to be **true**. The defence in the written statement is **irrelevant** at this stage.
2. The plaint must be read **as a whole and meaningfully**, not by isolating sentences.
3. The power can be exercised **at any stage** of the suit, even **suo motu**, and even after evidence, if the defect becomes apparent from the plaint.
4. "Barred by **any law**" includes limitation, res judicata, s. 12(2) bar, statutory ouster of jurisdiction, lack of a mandatory notice (where the law makes it a condition), etc. — but only if the bar is apparent **from the plaint itself**.
5. If a part of the plaint discloses a cause of action, the plaint as a whole cannot be rejected (rejection is of the plaint, not of parts of it), though the court may strike out the offending part.
6. Rejection is a **drastic power**: where facts are disputed, the matter should go to trial.

:::case T. Arivandandam v. T.V. Satyapal ((1977) 4 SCC 467)
__Held.__ If on a meaningful — not formal — reading of the plaint it is **manifestly vexatious and meritless**, in the sense of not disclosing a clear right to sue, the trial court should exercise its power under O. VII r. 11, taking care to see that the ground mentioned is fulfilled. If **clever drafting** has created the illusion of a cause of action, the court should **nip it in the bud** at the first hearing by examining the party searchingly under O. X.

__Ratio.__ Courts must look at substance, not form; an illusory cause of action created by drafting does not save a plaint.
:::

:::case Saleem Bhai v. State of Maharashtra ((2003) 1 SCC 557)
__Held.__ For deciding an application under O. VII r. 11, the **averments in the plaint** are germane; the pleas taken by the defendant in the written statement are **wholly irrelevant** at that stage. The court can exercise the power at any stage of the suit — before registering the plaint, after issuing summons, or at any time before conclusion of the trial.
:::

## 14.4 Return vs rejection — the most asked comparison

{w:22,39,39}
|Point|Return of plaint (r. 10)|Rejection of plaint (r. 11)|
|Ground|Court **lacks jurisdiction**; suit should be in another court|No cause of action; undervaluation not corrected; insufficient stamp not supplied; suit barred by law|
|What happens to the plaint|Handed back with endorsement, to be presented in the **proper court**|Plaint is **rejected**; suit ends in that court|
|Nature of order|**Order**|**Deemed decree** (s. 2(2))|
|Remedy|**Appeal** under O. XLIII r. 1(a)|**Appeal** under s. 96 (as a decree)|
|Fresh suit|Same plaint presented in proper court (fresh institution)|**Fresh plaint** allowed (r. 13), subject to limitation and the bar|
|Limitation|Time in wrong court excluded — **s. 14 Limitation Act**|No exclusion as such; fresh plaint must be within limitation|
|Question decided|Only **forum**; merits untouched|A legal defect in the plaint itself|

:::eg Return or rejection? (LHC 2020 problem)
*Facts:* Kiran (widow) and her son Salman file a suit for **partition of property situated at Pakpattan** in the Civil Court at **Multan**, where Salman lives. Kiran objects to the jurisdiction and asks for return of the plaint; the court refuses and proceeds. What remedies does Kiran have — review, direct appeal to the Supreme Court, revision, or appeal?
*Answer:* A suit for partition of immovable property must be filed where the property is situated (**s. 16(b) CPC**). Multan has no territorial jurisdiction; the plaint should have been **returned** under O. VII r. 10. The order **refusing** to return the plaint is not listed in O. XLIII r. 1 (only an order *returning* a plaint is appealable — r. 1(a)), so **no appeal** lies against it. **No direct appeal** to the Supreme Court lies against an interlocutory order of a Civil Judge. **Review** (s. 114, O. XLVII) is possible only for an error apparent on the face of the record, discovery of new evidence or other sufficient reason — an error of law on jurisdiction may qualify as an error apparent, but it is a narrow remedy. The proper remedy is **revision under s. 115 CPC** before the District Court / High Court (a case decided, no appeal lies, and the court has **exercised a jurisdiction not vested in it**). She can also raise the objection in an appeal from the final decree (s. 105), having taken it at the earliest stage as s. 21 requires.
:::

:::recap Chapter 14 in six lines
- Plaint: O. VII r. 1(a)–(i) — court, parties, minority, cause of action and date, jurisdiction facts, relief, set-off / relinquishment, valuation.
- Money: precise amount (r. 2); land: boundaries / khasra (r. 3); limitation exemption (r. 6); relief specifically (r. 7).
- Documents: sued-upon document with plaint; others listed; else leave needed (rr. 14, 18). Address for service (rr. 19–26, Pakistani).
- **Return** (r. 10): no jurisdiction → proper court; order; appeal O. XLIII r. 1(a); s. 14 Limitation Act.
- **Rejection** (r. 11): no cause of action; undervaluation / stamp not cured; barred by law — read the **plaint only** (*Saleem Bhai*), substance over clever drafting (*Arivandandam*); deemed decree, appeal s. 96; fresh plaint allowed (r. 13).
:::

# Chapter 15 · Written statement, set-off and counter-claim (Order VIII)

## 15.1 Filing the written statement — r. 1

> "1. The defendant may, and, if so required by the Court, shall, at or before the first hearing or within such time as the Court may permit, present a written statement of his defence: Provided that the period allowed for filing the written statement shall not ordinarily exceed thirty days."

- The proviso (added in Pakistan in 1994) sets an outer limit of **thirty days**, but the word **"ordinarily"** shows it is **directory**, not mandatory. The court may allow a later written statement for good reason, usually on **costs** (s. 35A).
- If a defendant who is **required** to file a written statement fails to do so, the court may pronounce judgment against him or make such other order as it thinks fit (**r. 10**). This is a **discretionary** power; the court should still be satisfied that the plaint, read with any evidence it requires, justifies a decree. An order under r. 10 pronouncing judgment is appealable (O. XLIII r. 1(b)).

:::case Kailash v. Nanhku ((2005) 4 SCC 480)
__Held.__ The provision fixing the time for filing a written statement is a part of procedural law and is **directory**, not mandatory. It is meant to expedite trials, not to shut out a defence. In exceptional cases, for reasons to be recorded, the court can extend time beyond the limit, imposing costs where appropriate.

__Ratio.__ Procedural time limits that do not themselves state a penal consequence are construed as directory, consistent with the principle that procedure is the handmaid of justice.
:::

## 15.2 Rules of a good defence — rr. 2 to 5

- **r. 2 — New facts must be specially pleaded:** the defendant must raise all matters showing the suit is **not maintainable**, or that the transaction is **void or voidable**, and all defences which would otherwise **take the plaintiff by surprise** or raise new issues of fact — "as, for instance, **fraud, limitation, release, payment, performance, or facts showing illegality**". (Also res judicata, s. 10, O. II r. 2, lack of notice under s. 80, non-joinder.)
- **r. 3 — Denial to be specific:** a general denial is **not** sufficient; each allegation of fact not admitted must be dealt with **specifically**, except damages.
- **r. 4 — Evasive denial:** a denial must not be evasive; it must answer the **point of substance**. If it is alleged that the defendant received Rs. 1,000, he must deny receiving that sum **or any part of it**, or say how much he received.
- **r. 5 — Specific denial:** every allegation of fact in the plaint, **if not denied specifically or by necessary implication, or stated to be not admitted**, shall be **taken to be admitted** — except against a person under disability. The court may still require proof.

:::why Why insist on specific denial?
Issues arise only where one party **affirms** and the other **denies** a material proposition (O. XIV r. 1). If the defendant does not specifically deny a fact, there is nothing to try on that fact; the court can treat it as admitted and save time. Specific denial narrows the dispute and prevents surprise at trial.
:::

:::maxim Qui tacet consentire videtur — silence gives consent
In pleading, a fact not denied specifically is taken as admitted (O. VIII r. 5). (The maxim is applied cautiously; it is the rule's own words that matter.)
:::

## 15.3 Set-off — r. 6

> "6. (1) Where in a suit for the recovery of money the defendant claims to set-off against the plaintiff's demand any ascertained sum of money legally recoverable by him from the plaintiff, not exceeding the pecuniary limits of the jurisdiction of the Court, and both parties fill the same character as they fill in the plaintiff's suit, the defendant may, at the first hearing of the suit, but not afterwards unless permitted by the Court, present a written statement containing the particulars of the debt sought to be set-off. (2) The written statement shall have the same effect as a plaint in a cross-suit so as to enable the Court to pronounce a final judgment in respect both of the original claim and of the set-off …"

### Conditions of legal set-off

1. The suit is for the **recovery of money**.
2. The sum claimed by the defendant is an **ascertained** (definite) sum.
3. It is **legally recoverable** (e.g., not time-barred on the date of the suit).
4. It is recoverable **by the defendant** (all defendants, if more than one) **from the plaintiff** (all plaintiffs).
5. It does **not exceed the pecuniary jurisdiction** of the court.
6. Both parties fill the **same character** in both claims.
7. Presented **at the first hearing** (later only with permission).

*Illustrations in the Code:* (c) A sues B on a bill of exchange; B claims compensation for A's failure to insure his goods — **unascertained**, cannot be set off. (d) A sues B on a bill for Rs. 500; B holds a judgment against A for Rs. 1,000 — both definite, **may** be set off. (f) A and B sue C for Rs. 1,000; C cannot set off a debt due to him by A alone. (g) A sues B and C; B cannot set off a debt due to him alone by A.

### Equitable set-off

Courts also allow an **equitable set-off** outside r. 6 where the cross-claims arise out of the **same transaction** or are so connected that it would be inequitable to make the plaintiff recover without allowing the defendant's claim — even if the sum is **unascertained** (e.g., a contractor sues for the price of work; the employer claims damages for defective work under the same contract). Equitable set-off is a matter of the court's discretion.

{w:22,39,39}
|Point|Legal set-off (O. VIII r. 6)|Equitable set-off|
|Amount|Must be **ascertained**|May be **unascertained**|
|Transaction|May arise from a **different** transaction|Must arise from the **same** or closely connected transaction|
|Right|Defendant's **right**, if conditions met|Court's **discretion**|
|Court fee|Payable as on a plaint|Usually not payable as such|

## 15.4 Counter-claim in Pakistan

The Indian CPC was amended in 1976 to add O. VIII rr. 6A–6G (counter-claim). **The Pakistani / Islamabad CPC has no equivalent rules**; the Code mentions counter-claim only in **s. 128(2)(c)**, which allows High Courts to make rules on "procedure in suits by way of counterclaim". In practice, a defendant who has an independent claim against the plaintiff (not qualifying as set-off) may:

1. file a **separate suit** and seek its **consolidation** with the plaintiff's suit (s. 151); or
2. where the court permits, plead it as a **counter-claim treated as a cross-suit**, paying the proper **court fee**, to be tried with the suit — courts have allowed this to avoid multiplicity, using inherent powers; or
3. rely on a **special law** that expressly allows counter-claims (e.g., banking recovery laws).

In an exam answer, say clearly that the CPC itself does not provide for counter-claims, explain the set-off route, and mention the practical alternatives.

## 15.5 Other rules — rr. 7 to 13

- **r. 7:** several distinct grounds of defence or set-off are stated **separately**.
- **r. 8:** a **new ground of defence** that arises **after** the suit or the written statement may be raised.
- **r. 9 — Subsequent pleadings:** no pleading after the written statement (other than a defence to a set-off) **except with leave** of the court — e.g., a **replication** by the plaintiff, allowed only if the court permits.
- **r. 10:** failure to file a written statement when required → judgment or other order.
- **rr. 11–12:** address for service; failure → defence may be **struck out** after opportunity (SRO 579(I)/2014 added the opportunity).
- **r. 13:** list of the defendant's legal representatives in certain suits.

:::recap Chapter 15 in six lines
- Written statement at or before first hearing / time allowed; **30 days ordinarily** (directory — *Kailash v. Nanhku*); failure → r. 10 judgment (discretionary; appeal O. XLIII r. 1(b)).
- r. 2: plead new facts — fraud, limitation, release, payment, performance, illegality, non-maintainability.
- rr. 3–5: **specific, non-evasive** denial; facts not denied are **deemed admitted**.
- r. 6 legal set-off: money suit; ascertained; legally recoverable; same parties, same character; within pecuniary limits; first hearing; acts as a cross-plaint.
- Equitable set-off: same transaction; unascertained allowed; discretionary.
- No counter-claim rules in the Pakistani CPC (s. 128(2)(c) only); separate suit + consolidation, cross-suit on court fee, or special law. r. 9: replication only with leave.
:::

# Chapter 16 · Appearance and non-appearance; ex parte proceedings (Order IX)

## 16.1 The scheme of Order IX

Order IX tells the court what to do when, on a date of hearing, **one or both parties do not appear**. The court has to balance two things: the **right to be heard** (*audi alteram partem*) and the need to **stop delay** (*vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt*).

|Situation (rule)|What the court does|Remedy of the aggrieved party|
|Summons not served because **plaintiff did not pay** process fee (r. 2)|May **dismiss** the suit (not if defendant appears anyway)|Fresh suit (subject to limitation) **or** restoration on sufficient cause (r. 4)|
|**Neither party** appears (r. 3)|May **dismiss** the suit|Fresh suit (subject to limitation) **or** restoration (r. 4)|
|Summons returned unserved and plaintiff fails for **three months** to apply for fresh summons (r. 5)|**Shall** dismiss against that defendant, unless he shows diligent efforts, evasion or other sufficient cause|Fresh suit (subject to limitation) (r. 5(2))|

|**Defendant appears, plaintiff absent** (r. 8)|**Shall dismiss** the suit, unless defendant admits all or part of the claim (decree on admission for that part)|Plaintiff: **no fresh suit** (r. 9(1)); restoration on **sufficient cause** (r. 9), notice to other side, s. 5 Limitation Act applies|

## 16.2 Ex parte decree — and how to set it aside: r. 13

> "13. (1) In any case in which a decree is passed ex parte against a defendant, he may apply to the Court by which the decree was passed for an order to set it aside; and if he satisfies the Court that the summons was not duly served, or that he was prevented by any sufficient cause from appearing when the suit was called on for hearing, the Court shall make an order setting aside the decree as against him upon such terms as to costs, payment into Court or otherwise as it thinks fit, and shall appoint a day for proceeding with the suit:
> Provided that where the decree is of such a nature that it cannot be set aside as against such defendant only it may be set aside as against all or any of the other defendants also:
> Provided further that no decree passed ex parte shall be set aside merely on the ground of any irregularity in the service of summons, if the Court is satisfied, for reason to be recorded, that the defendant had knowledge of the date of hearing in sufficient time to appear on that date and answer the claim.
> (2) The provisions of section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1908, shall apply to applications under sub-rule (1)."

**Two grounds (either is enough):**

1. the summons was **not duly served**; or
2. the defendant was **prevented by sufficient cause** from appearing when the suit was called on for hearing.

**Limits:**

- **Second proviso:** a mere **irregularity** in service is not enough if the defendant actually **knew** of the date in time.
- **Notice** to the opposite party is compulsory (**r. 14**).
- **Limitation:** **Article 164, Limitation Act** — **30 days** from the date of the decree, or, where the summons was not duly served, from the date the applicant had **knowledge** of the decree; **s. 5** of the Limitation Act (condonation of delay) applies (r. 13(2)).
- An order **rejecting** an application under r. 13 is appealable (**O. XLIII r. 1(d)**). An order **allowing** it is not appealable; revision may lie.

### What is "sufficient cause"?

"Sufficient cause" means a cause beyond the party's control, showing he was **not negligent** and acted **bona fide** — illness (supported by evidence), accident, death in the family, transport breakdown, a mistake of counsel, wrong date noted by the clerk, natural calamity. The expression is construed **liberally** to advance substantial justice, but not to reward deliberate default.

:::case G.P. Srivastava v. R.K. Raizada ((2000) 3 SCC 54)
__Held.__ Under O. IX r. 13, an ex parte decree can be set aside if the defendant satisfies the court that he was prevented by "sufficient cause" from appearing. Sufficient cause is an expression that should receive a **liberal construction** so as to advance substantial justice when no negligence, inaction or want of bona fides is imputable to the defendant. The court must look at whether the defendant honestly intended to remain present and did his best to do so.
:::

:::case Parimal v. Veena ((2011) 3 SCC 545)
__Held.__ "Sufficient cause" means that the party had not acted in a negligent manner or there was a want of bona fide on its part in view of the facts and circumstances of the case, or that the party cannot be alleged to have been "not acting diligently" or "remaining inactive". However, the facts must establish a cause which **prevented** the party from appearing; a court should not set aside an ex parte decree merely out of sympathy if the defendant was careless.
:::

## 16.3 Ex parte order vs ex parte decree

{w:22,39,39}
|Point|Ex parte **order** (proceedings ex parte)|Ex parte **decree**|
|When|During the suit, when the defendant is absent on a hearing date despite service (r. 6(1)(a))|At the end, when the suit is decided in the defendant's absence|
|Remedy|Appear before the decree and show **good cause** (r. 7) — defendant is then heard "as if he had appeared on the day fixed", on terms|Application under **r. 13** (sufficient cause / no due service); **appeal** (s. 96(2)); review; **s. 12(2)** if obtained by fraud|
|Effect of remedy|Defendant joins from that stage; earlier proceedings are not undone automatically|Decree set aside; suit restored for trial|
|Test|"Good cause"|"Sufficient cause" or summons not duly served|

## 16.4 Remedies against an ex parte decree — all together

1. **Application under O. IX r. 13** to the same court (30 days, Art. 164).
2. **Appeal** under **s. 96** — in Islamabad **s. 96(2)** expressly says "An appeal may lie from an original decree passed ex-parte" (substituted by Act XIX of 2023). In appeal the defendant can argue that the decree is wrong on the plaintiff's own case or evidence.
3. **Review** under s. 114 / O. XLVII (error apparent, new evidence).
4. **s. 12(2) CPC** where the decree was obtained by **fraud or misrepresentation** (e.g., a false address given to avoid service) or **without jurisdiction**.

Both an application under r. 13 and an appeal may be pursued, but once the appeal is decided **on merits**, the trial court's decree merges into the appellate decree and the r. 13 application cannot continue (*see Bhanu Kumar Jain v. Archana Kumar*, (2005) 1 SCC 787 — both remedies are available but operate in different fields).

:::eg Ex parte decree — problem method
Bashir is sued for recovery of Rs. 3 million in Islamabad. The process server reports that he affixed the summons on Bashir's house in G-11 because "the defendant was not available"; no names of identifiers are given and the court does not examine the process server. The registered A/D summons comes back with the remark "addressee left". The court proceeds ex parte and decrees the suit without evidence. Bashir learns of the decree four months later when his bank account is attached.
*Analysis:* (1) Service under O. V r. 17 requires due diligence, identifiers and a r. 19 declaration — missing; r. 10-A post was **not** received. So the summons was **not duly served**. (2) Limitation under Art. 164 runs from **knowledge** — the application is within 30 days of learning of the decree if filed promptly. (3) The second proviso (knowledge of date) does not apply — nothing shows he knew. (4) Result: decree to be **set aside** under O. IX r. 13 on costs; suit restored. (5) Alternatively, appeal under s. 96(2); if the plaintiff gave a false address deliberately, s. 12(2) also lies.
:::

## 16.5 Restoration of a suit dismissed for default — r. 9

> "9. (1) Where a suit is wholly or partly dismissed under rule 8, the plaintiff shall be precluded from bringing a fresh suit in respect of the same cause of action. But he may apply for an order to set the dismissal aside, and if he satisfies the Court that there was sufficient cause for his non-appearance when the suit was called on for hearing, the Court shall make an order setting aside the dismissal upon such terms as to costs or otherwise as it thinks fit, and shall appoint a day for proceeding with the suit. (2) No order shall be made under this rule unless notice of the application has been served on the opposite party. (3) The provisions of section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1908, shall apply to applications under sub-rule (1)."

- **Limitation:** **Article 163, Limitation Act** — **30 days** from the date of dismissal; s. 5 applies.
- An order **rejecting** restoration is appealable (**O. XLIII r. 1(c)**).

:::trap Dismissal for default in a later hearing
If the court dismisses the suit on a date when evidence was due and the plaintiff failed to produce it **despite time granted**, the dismissal may be on the **merits** under **O. XVII r. 3** — which is a **decree** (appealable), not a dismissal for default under O. IX. Identify the correct rule before suggesting a remedy (see Chapter 19).
:::

:::recap Chapter 16 in seven lines
- r. 2 / r. 3 dismissal → fresh suit or restoration (r. 4); r. 5 → three months to seek fresh summons, else dismissal; fresh suit allowed.
- r. 6: plaintiff present, defendant absent — served → ex parte (decree may be passed without evidence); not served → re-summon; late → postpone.
