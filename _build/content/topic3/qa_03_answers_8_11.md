
# Answer 8 · "Consideration" and "object" — the difference, and when they are lawful (LHC ASJ 2020)

:::glance LHC ADDITIONAL DISTRICT AND SESSIONS JUDGES EXAMINATION 2020 · CIVIL LAW-I · Q. 1 · 20 MARKS
Discuss and explain in light of the relevant statutory provisions, the difference between "consideration" and "objects" with reference to an agreement if any, and in what circumstances can they be considered lawful?
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](c8_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Two parts: (i) **define and distinguish** consideration (s. 2(d)) and object (purpose — s. 23), showing that they may coincide or differ; (ii) explain **s. 23** — when they are lawful and when **unlawful** — with the **six heads**, the **statutory illustrations**, cases, and the effect (void; s. 24 partial illegality; ss. 57–58 severability). "In what circumstances can they be considered lawful" invites you to state s. 23's **negative** formula: lawful **unless** one of the heads applies.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction

Section 10 requires that a contract be made "for a **lawful consideration** and with a **lawful object**". The Act treats both under one section — s. 23 — but they are **different ideas**.

### 2. Consideration — meaning

"When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a **consideration** for the promise" (s. 2(d)). It is the **price** or **quid pro quo** of a promise — **what is given** in exchange.

### 3. Object — meaning

The Act does not define "object". It means the **purpose** or **design** of the agreement — **what the parties intend to achieve** by it. In most contracts the object is the **performance** itself (e.g., transfer of a house for money), but sometimes the parties share an **ulterior purpose** (e.g., money lent so that the borrower may bribe an official).

### 4. Difference between consideration and object

{w:24,38,38}
|Point|Consideration|Object|
|Meaning|The price of the promise — what each party gives|The purpose or design of the agreement|
|Definition|s. 2(d)|Not defined; understood from s. 23|
|Question it answers|"What do I get for my promise?"|"Why are we making this agreement?"|
|Example|Rs. 500,000 paid as a loan|To use the money for bribery|
|Can it be lawful while the other is not?|Yes — lawful money for an unlawful purpose|Yes — unlawful consideration for a lawful purpose (e.g., paying a bribe to obtain a lawful licence)|
|Effect if unlawful|Agreement void (s. 23)|Agreement void (s. 23)|

**Illustration (a) to s. 23** shows the two together: "A agrees to sell his house to B for 10,000 rupees. Here B's promise to pay the sum of 10,000 rupees is the consideration for A's promise to sell the house, and A's promise to sell the house is the consideration for B's promise to pay the 10,000 rupees. These are lawful considerations." The **object** — sale of a house — is also lawful.

### 5. When are they lawful? — s. 23

> "The consideration or object of an agreement is lawful, **unless**— it is forbidden by law; or is of such a nature that, if permitted, it would defeat the provisions of any law; or is fraudulent; or involves or implies injury to the person or property of another; or the Court regards it as immoral, or opposed to public policy. In each of these cases, the consideration or object of an agreement is said to be unlawful. Every agreement of which the object or consideration is unlawful is **void**."

So consideration and object are **lawful** in every case **except** the six listed. The six heads follow.

### 6. Head 1 — forbidden by law

Prohibited by a statute or rule having the force of law, expressly or by implication. *Illustration (f):* A promises to obtain for B a post in the **public service** for Rs. 1,000 — void. Examples: smuggling, sale of narcotics, bribery.

### 7. Head 2 — defeating the provisions of any law

Lawful in form but evading a statute. *Illustration (i):* an estate sold for arrears of revenue; the law forbids the defaulter to buy; B buys under an understanding to reconvey to A — void, as it defeats the law.

### 8. Head 3 — fraudulent

*Illustration (e):* A, B and C agree to divide gains acquired by **fraud** — void. *Illustration (g):* an agent, without his principal's knowledge, takes money to obtain a lease of the principal's land for B — void (fraud on the principal).

### 9. Head 4 — injury to the person or property of another

"Injury" means a **wrongful** act — e.g., an agreement to publish a libel, to trespass, or a bond requiring the debtor to work without wages (bonded labour, now an offence under the **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1992**).

### 10. Head 5 — immoral

*Illustration (j):* A, B's **mukhtar**, promises to use his influence with B in favour of C for Rs. 1,000 — void. *Illustration (k):* A agrees to let her daughter to hire for **concubinage** — void, though perhaps not punishable. *Pearce v. Brooks* ((1866) LR 1 Ex 213): hire of a carriage to a prostitute for her trade — unenforceable.

### 11. Head 6 — opposed to public policy

Public policy is "a very unruly horse" (*Richardson v. Mellish*, (1824) 2 Bing 229), so courts apply only **established heads** (*Gherulal Parakh v. Mahadeodas Maiya*, AIR 1959 SC 781): trading with an enemy; **stifling prosecution** (*illustration (h)*: dropping a robbery prosecution in return for restoring the value of stolen goods — void); interfering with the course of justice; sale of public offices; marriage brokerage; and unconscionable champerty (*Ram Coomar Coondoo v. Chunder Canto Mookerjee*, (1876) ILR 2 Cal 233, PC — fair litigation funding is not void in itself).

### 12. Partial unlawfulness — s. 24 and severability

"If any part of a **single consideration** for one or more objects, or any one or any part of any one of **several considerations** for a single object, is unlawful, the agreement is void" (s. 24). *Illustration:* a salary of Rs. 10,000 for superintending a **legal** indigo business and an **illegal** traffic — wholly void, as the consideration cannot be apportioned. But where the lawful and unlawful parts are **severable**, the lawful part stands (s. 57 — reciprocal promises, legal and illegal; s. 58 — alternative promise, the legal branch enforced).

### 13. Consequences of unlawfulness

1. The agreement is **void** (s. 23).
2. **Ex turpi causa non oritur actio** — no action on an illegal agreement (*Holman v. Johnson*, (1775) 1 Cowp 341).
3. **In pari delicto potior est conditio defendentis** — money paid is generally not recoverable, except where the parties are **not equally at fault** or the plaintiff **withdraws before** the illegal purpose is carried out.
4. **Collateral transactions** to an illegal agreement are also tainted.

### 14. Illustration

Waqas lends Zain Rs. 2 million, both knowing that Zain will use it to pay a bribe to secure a CDA plot allotment. The **consideration** (the loan) is lawful in itself, but the **object** (bribery) is **forbidden by law** and **opposed to public policy**. The agreement is **void** (s. 23); Waqas cannot sue to recover the loan (*in pari delicto*). Had Waqas lent the money for Zain's business without knowing of the bribery, the loan would be valid.

### 15. Conclusion

Consideration is **what is given** for a promise; object is **the purpose** of the agreement. Both must be lawful. Under s. 23 they are lawful **unless** forbidden by law, defeating any law, fraudulent, injurious, immoral or opposed to public policy — in which case the agreement is **void**, and under s. 24 even partial unlawfulness of a single, indivisible consideration taints the whole.

## Why this answer scores

- It **defines both terms separately** and gives a **difference table**, which is the core of the question.
- It quotes **s. 23** in full and organises the answer around its **six heads**, each with an **illustration** from the Act.
- **s. 24** and **ss. 57–58** show depth on partial illegality.
- The final example shows **lawful consideration with an unlawful object** — the distinction the examiner wants to see applied.

# Answer 9 · Short notes on five concepts (LHC 2022)

:::glance LAHORE HIGH COURT 2022 · CIVIL LAW-I · Q. 1 · 20 MARKS (4 marks each)
Define and write short notes and principles laid down the following by the apex courts of Pakistan in various judgments: (i) Difference between "agreement and contract"; (ii) difference between "void" and "voidable contract"; (iii) effect of "promise" and "reciprocal promises"; (iv) concept of "express" and "implied" authority in an arrangement of agency; (v) mistake of fact and its effect on the contract.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](c9_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Five notes of **about one page each**. For each: **definition from the Act**, the **key principle**, **one authority**, and an **example**. Keep each note to the same structure so that none is short-changed. Note (iv) is from the law of **agency** (Topic 4) — answer it briefly from ss. 186–188.

## The model answer

### 1. Agreement and contract

#### i) Definitions

**Agreement:** "Every promise and every set of promises, forming the consideration for each other, is an agreement" (s. 2(e)). **Contract:** "An agreement **enforceable by law** is a contract" (s. 2(h)).

#### ii) Differences

{w:26,37,37}
|Point|Agreement|Contract|
|Scope|Wider — includes social, moral and void agreements|Narrower — only enforceable agreements|
|Elements|Promise + consideration|Agreement + s. 10 essentials|
|Legal obligation|Not necessarily|Always|
|Example|Promise to dine with a friend; wager|Sale of a car for a price|

#### iii) Principle

"Every contract is an agreement, but every agreement is not a contract." An agreement without intention to create legal relations is not a contract (*Balfour v. Balfour*, [1919] 2 KB 571).

### 2. Void and voidable contracts

#### i) Definitions

"An agreement not enforceable by law is said to be **void**" (s. 2(g)). "An agreement which is enforceable by law at the option of one or more of the parties thereto, but not at the option of the other or others, is a **voidable** contract" (s. 2(i)). A contract **becomes void** when it ceases to be enforceable (s. 2(j)).

#### ii) Differences

{w:26,37,37}
|Point|Void|Voidable|
|Validity|No legal effect from the start|Valid until avoided by the party entitled|
|Cause|Missing essential: incapacity, no consideration, unlawful object, s. 20 mistake, ss. 26–30|Defective consent: coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation (ss. 19, 19A); s. 55|
|Third parties|Acquire no rights|Bona fide purchaser before avoidance protected|
|Restitution|s. 65|s. 64|

#### iii) Principle

A minor's agreement is **void ab initio** (*Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose*, (1903) ILR 30 Cal 539, PC). A contract obtained by fraud is **voidable**, and a rogue's buyer who buys in good faith **before avoidance** gets good title (*Phillips v. Brooks*, [1919] 2 KB 243).

### 3. Promise and reciprocal promises

#### i) Definitions

"A proposal, when accepted, becomes a **promise**" (s. 2(b)). "Promises which form the consideration or part of the consideration for each other are called **reciprocal promises**" (s. 2(f)).

#### ii) Effect of reciprocal promises

1. **Simultaneous** reciprocal promises — "no promisor need perform his promise unless the promisee is **ready and willing** to perform his reciprocal promise" (s. 51; e.g., delivery against payment).
2. **Order of performance** — as fixed, or as the **nature of the transaction** requires (s. 52; a house must be built before the price is paid).
3. **Prevention** — if one party prevents the other, the contract becomes **voidable** by the party prevented, with compensation (s. 53).
4. **Default in the first promise** — the defaulter cannot claim the reciprocal performance and must compensate (s. 54).
5. **Legal and illegal** reciprocal promises — the legal set is a contract, the illegal set void (s. 57).

#### iii) Principle

In suits for specific performance, a plaintiff must show that he has **always been ready and willing** to perform his part; Pakistani courts may require the buyer to **deposit the balance** price, and failure is fatal (*Hamood Mehmood v. Mst. Shabana Ishaque*, 2017 SCMR 2022).

### 4. Express and implied authority of an agent

#### i) Law

"The authority of an agent may be **expressed or implied**" (s. 186). "An authority is said to be express when it is given by **words spoken or written**. An authority is said to be implied when it is to be **inferred from the circumstances** of the case; and things spoken or written, or the ordinary course of dealing, may be accounted circumstances of the case" (s. 187).

#### ii) Extent

"An agent having an authority to do an act has authority to do **every lawful thing which is necessary** in order to do such act" (s. 188). An agent for carrying on a business has authority to do everything necessary for, or usually done in, that business.

#### iii) Example and principle

*Illustration to s. 187:* A owns a shop in **Quetta**, lives in **Karachi**, and visits the shop occasionally; the shop is managed by B, who is in the habit of ordering goods from C in A's name and paying for them out of A's funds with A's knowledge. B has an **implied authority** to order goods from C in A's name. The principal is bound by acts within the agent's actual (express or implied) authority, and by acts within his **ostensible** authority on which third parties rely (s. 237). (Full treatment: Topic 4.)

### 5. Mistake of fact and its effect

#### i) Law

"Where **both** the parties to an agreement are under a mistake as to a matter of fact **essential** to the agreement, the agreement is **void**" (s. 20). An erroneous opinion as to **value** is not a mistake of fact (Explanation). A contract is **not voidable merely** because one party was under a mistake of fact (s. 22).

#### ii) Kinds and effects

1. **Bilateral, essential** — void: subject matter already perished (illus. (a) cargo bound for Karachi lost; (b) horse dead) — *Couturier v. Hastie* ((1856) 5 HLC 673).
2. **Quality** — not operative unless it makes the thing **essentially different** (*Bell v. Lever Brothers*, [1932] AC 161).
3. **Unilateral** — no relief (s. 22; *Smith v. Hughes*, (1871) LR 6 QB 597), except **identity** where vital (*Cundy v. Lindsay*, (1878) 3 App Cas 459) and *non est factum*.
4. **Mistake of Pakistani law** — no relief; foreign law is treated as fact (s. 21).
5. Money paid under a mistake is **recoverable** (s. 72).

#### iii) Principle

The law voids an agreement only where the mistake destroys the **very basis** of consent; otherwise the security of contracts prevails.

## Why this answer scores

- Five notes, **equal weight**, each with definition, principle, authority and example — easy for the examiner to mark.
- **Tables** for the two "difference" notes save words and show clarity.
- The agency note is answered **briefly but accurately** (ss. 186–188, 237) with a pointer to Topic 4.
- The question asks for "principles laid down by the apex courts" — each note cites a **leading judgment**, including a Pakistani Supreme Court case.

# Answer 10 · Intention of the parties and the relative importance of contractual terms (KPHC 2026)

:::glance KP HIGH COURT 2026 · CIVIL LAW-I · PART III · Q. 7 · 20 MARKS
The intentions of the contracting parties are the main factors in deciding on the relative importance of contractual terms. Discuss whether this is, in fact, the approach currently taken by the courts.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](c10_map.png)

## Decoding the question

This is a **critical essay**. The statement is **partly true**. You must (i) explain **why** terms differ in importance — **conditions, warranties and innominate terms**; (ii) show where courts rely on **intention** (construction of the contract: SGA s. 12(4); s. 55 Contract Act; *Poussard*; *Bettini*); (iii) show where they look at **other factors** — the **consequences of the breach** (*Hongkong Fir*), **commercial certainty** (*The Mihalis Angelos*; *Bunge v. Tradax*), **statute** (SGA ss. 14–17), and the courts' refusal to be bound by the **labels** the parties used (*Schuler v. Wickman*); and (iv) conclude with a **balanced view**.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — why the importance of a term matters

Every contract has terms of unequal weight. When a term is broken, the innocent party may wish to **terminate** the contract (and claim damages) or may be limited to **damages** while still performing. The classification of the term broken decides which. The statement says that the parties' **intention** decides that classification. The courts' actual approach is more **mixed**.

### 2. The classes of terms

1. **Condition** — "a stipulation **essential to the main purpose** of the contract, the breach of which gives rise to a right to treat the contract as repudiated" (Sale of Goods Act 1930, s. 12(2)).
2. **Warranty** — "a stipulation **collateral to the main purpose** of the contract, the breach of which gives rise to a claim for damages but not to a right to reject the goods and treat the contract as repudiated" (s. 12(3)).
3. **Innominate (intermediate) term** — a term whose breach may be trivial or serious; the remedy depends on the **gravity of the consequences** (*Hongkong Fir*, below).

### 3. The intention-based (traditional) approach

#### i) Statute

"Whether a stipulation in a contract of sale is a condition or a warranty depends in each case on the **construction of the contract**. A stipulation may be a condition, though called a warranty in the contract" (SGA s. 12(4)). Construction means finding the **intention** of the parties, judged **objectively** from the contract as a whole.

#### ii) The Contract Act

Under s. 55, failure to perform at the fixed time makes the contract **voidable** only "**if the intention of the parties was that time should be of the essence** of the contract"; otherwise the promisee gets **compensation** only. Here the Act expressly makes **intention** the test.

#### iii) Classic cases

1. *Poussard v. Spiers and Pond* ((1876) 1 QBD 410): a leading singer's absence from the **opening night** and first week went to the **root** of the engagement — **condition**; the producers could terminate.
2. *Bettini v. Gye* ((1876) 1 QBD 183): a singer's late arrival for **rehearsals** — a subsidiary term (**warranty**); damages only.

In both cases the court asked what importance **the parties** must be taken to have attached to the term, judged at the time of contracting.

### 4. Express classification — labels are not conclusive

Parties may **expressly** state that a term is a "condition" or that "time is of the essence". Courts give great weight to this, but **not conclusive** weight.

*L. Schuler AG v. Wickman Machine Tool Sales Ltd.* ([1974] AC 235): a clause calling weekly visits to customers a "**condition**" did not entitle Schuler to terminate for a **single** missed visit. The House of Lords held that the more unreasonable the result, the more clearly the parties must show they intended it. The court looked behind the word to the **true intention** in the context of the whole contract.

Similarly, under s. 55, a clause saying "time is of the essence" in a sale of land may be **displaced** by other terms (e.g., provision for late payment with interest) or by **conduct** (*Chand Rani v. Kamal Rani*, (1993) 1 SCC 519). In sales of **immovable property**, the courts **presume** that time is not of the essence, even where a date is fixed.

### 5. The consequences-based approach — innominate terms

*Hongkong Fir Shipping Co. v. Kawasaki Kisen Kaisha* ([1962] 2 QB 26): the term "**seaworthy**" could be broken in many ways — from a missing nail to a sinking hull. Diplock LJ held that for such terms the court looks **at the event** — whether the breach deprived the innocent party of "**substantially the whole benefit**" intended. Twenty weeks' delay in a 24-month charter did not; the charterers had **no right** to terminate.

This approach does **not** depend on what the parties intended when contracting; it depends on **what happened** after breach. It reduces the risk of termination for trivial breaches on a **falling market** (a motive seen in cases like *Arcos v. Ronaasen* [1933] AC 470, where buyers rejected timber for a tiny deviation in thickness).

### 6. The return of certainty — commercial contracts

Courts later stressed **certainty** in **mercantile** contracts:

1. *The Mihalis Angelos* ([1971] 1 QB 164): an "**expected ready to load**" clause is a **condition**; commercial parties need to know at once whether they can terminate.
2. *Bunge Corp. v. Tradax Export* ([1981] 1 WLR 711, HL): **time clauses** in mercantile contracts are generally **conditions**, so that the innocent party need not wait to see how serious the consequences are.

Here, the classification rests on **commercial policy** and the **type of contract** as much as on the actual intention of the parties.

### 7. Classification by statute and precedent

1. The **Sale of Goods Act** implies certain **conditions** — title, description, quality and fitness, sample (ss. 14–17) — whatever the parties thought.
2. Some terms are classified by **binding precedent** (e.g., time of shipment in c.i.f. contracts).
3. **s. 13 SGA**: a buyer may **waive** a condition or treat its breach as a breach of warranty; after **acceptance** of goods, a breach of condition **must** be treated as a breach of warranty — the law, not intention, decides.

### 8. The Contract Act's own approach in Pakistan

The Contract Act does not use the words "condition" and "warranty" but reaches the same results through:

1. **s. 39** — refusal or disablement to perform "**in its entirety**" allows termination; a partial breach gives compensation;
2. **s. 55** — **intention** that time be of the essence;
3. **s. 73** — compensation for every breach.

Pakistani courts therefore start from the **intention** of the parties (objectively construed), and look at the **gravity** of the breach and the nature of the contract (commercial vs land) in deciding whether termination is justified.

### 9. Evaluation

{w:30,70}
|Factor|Role in the courts' approach|
|**Intention (construction)**|Starting point — SGA s. 12(4); s. 55; *Poussard*; *Bettini*|
|**Labels used by the parties**|Strong evidence but not conclusive — *Schuler*|
|**Consequences of breach**|Decisive for innominate terms — *Hongkong Fir*|
|**Commercial certainty**|Time clauses in mercantile contracts are conditions — *Mihalis Angelos*; *Bunge v. Tradax*|
|**Statute and precedent**|Implied conditions (SGA ss. 14–17); s. 13 SGA; presumptions on time in land sales|

### 10. Conclusion

The statement is **only partly accurate**. **Intention**, objectively ascertained by construing the contract, remains the **starting point** — the SGA and s. 55 of the Contract Act say so expressly. But it is **not the only, or always the main**, factor. Courts decline to follow labels that produce unreasonable results (*Schuler*); for innominate terms they look at the **consequences** of the breach (*Hongkong Fir*); and in commercial contracts they classify terms with an eye to **certainty** (*Bunge*). The current approach is therefore a **combination** of intention, consequences and commercial policy.

## Why this answer scores

- It **takes a position** — "partly true" — and proves it, which is what "discuss whether" requires.
- It moves **historically**: classic intention-based cases → innominate terms → return to certainty.
- It anchors the essay in the **statutes in the syllabus** (SGA ss. 12–13; Contract Act ss. 39, 55, 73).
- The **evaluation table** shows the examiner the whole argument in one view.

# Answer 11 · Breach of a supply contract — damages, remoteness, mitigation and the penalty clause (practice problem)

:::glance PRACTICE PROBLEM (EXAMINATION STYLE) · ss. 73–75 · 20 MARKS
Danish Textiles, Faisalabad, contracted with Khurram Engineering to supply and install a new dyeing machine for Rs. 15 million by 1 March 2026. Danish told Khurram at the time of contracting that it needed the machine to fulfil an **export order** from a Turkish buyer worth Rs. 30 million, on which it expected a profit of Rs. 4 million. The contract provided: "In case of delay, the supplier shall pay Rs. 500,000 per week of delay as **liquidated damages**." Danish paid Rs. 3 million as advance. The machine was installed on 1 May 2026 — **eight weeks late**. Danish lost the Turkish order. During the delay it also lost ordinary local work worth Rs. 600,000 in profit. Danish could have hired a similar machine for Rs. 200,000 a month, but did not. Danish claims Rs. 4,000,000 (the export profit) plus Rs. 4,000,000 under the clause. Advise.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](c11_map.png)

## Decoding the question

The problem tests **s. 73** (the two limbs of *Hadley v. Baxendale*, communicated special circumstances, illustrations), the **Explanation** to s. 73 (**mitigation** — the hire option), and **s. 74** (the "liquidated damages" clause — under Pakistani law a **ceiling**, not an automatic sum; *Fateh Chand*). The claim combines the two, so you must also deal with **double recovery**. End with a **figure** the court is likely to award.

## The model answer

### 1. Issues

1. Is Khurram in **breach**, and was time of the essence?
2. Which losses are recoverable under **s. 73** — the export profit and the local profit?
3. What is the effect of Danish's failure to **mitigate**?
4. What is the effect of the **Rs. 500,000 per week** clause under **s. 74**?
5. Can Danish recover **both** the clause amount and the s. 73 damages?

### 2. Breach

Khurram promised installation **by 1 March 2026** and installed on **1 May** — eight weeks late. Danish **accepted** late performance. Under s. 55, if time was of the essence, the contract was voidable; but by accepting late performance Danish cannot claim compensation **unless it gave notice** of its intention to do so at the time of acceptance (s. 55, para 3). Assuming Danish reserved its rights (as a prudent party would, and as the claim suggests), it may claim compensation. Without such notice, the claim would **fail** — a point the answer must flag.

### 3. The law — s. 73

> "…compensation for any loss or damage caused to him thereby, which **naturally arose in the usual course of things** from such breach, or which the parties **knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result** from the breach of it. Such compensation is not to be given for any **remote and indirect** loss … Explanation.— In estimating the loss or damage …, the **means which existed of remedying the inconvenience** caused by the non-performance of the contract must be taken into account."

This is the rule in *Hadley v. Baxendale* ((1854) 9 Exch 341): (i) losses arising **naturally**; (ii) losses in the **contemplation of both parties** because of **special circumstances communicated** at the time of contracting.

### 4. The export profit — Rs. 4 million (second limb)

Danish **told** Khurram, **at the time of contracting**, that the machine was needed for a specific **export order** with an expected profit of Rs. 4 million. That loss was therefore within the parties' contemplation (s. 73, second limb). Compare:

1. **Illustration (j)**: where the seller is told the purpose of a purchase (to perform a contract with B), he is liable for the **lost profit** on that contract.
2. **Illustration (l)**: a builder **informed** of the owner's lease to C is liable for lost rent and compensation paid to C.
3. Contrast **illustration (k)** and *Victoria Laundry v. Newman Industries* ([1949] 2 KB 528), where uncommunicated, exceptionally lucrative contracts were **not** recoverable.

Prima facie, the export profit is recoverable — **subject to mitigation**.

### 5. The local profit — Rs. 600,000 (first limb)

Loss of **ordinary profits** from a profit-earning machine during delay arises **naturally** in the usual course of things (*Victoria Laundry* — ordinary laundry profits recovered; illus. (i) — **average profit** of the mill during the delay). This loss is recoverable under the first limb — again subject to mitigation.

### 6. Mitigation — the hire option

Danish could have **hired** a similar machine for **Rs. 200,000 a month** — about **Rs. 400,000** for the eight weeks — but did not. Under the **Explanation** to s. 73, the means of remedying the inconvenience "must be taken into account" (*British Westinghouse Electric v. Underground Electric Railways*, [1912] AC 673). Had Danish hired the machine:

1. it could probably have **fulfilled the export order** and earned the Rs. 4 million profit; and
2. it could have continued its **local work**.

Danish therefore cannot recover losses it **reasonably could have avoided**. Its recoverable loss is limited to what mitigation would have cost — the **hire charges** (about Rs. 400,000) plus any extra expenses reasonably incurred — unless Danish shows that hiring was **not reasonably possible** (e.g., no machine was in fact available in time, or installation of a hired machine would itself have taken too long). The burden of proving failure to mitigate is on the **defendant**, Khurram.

### 7. The "liquidated damages" clause — s. 74

> "When a contract has been broken, if a **sum is named** in the contract as the amount to be paid in case of such breach … the party complaining of the breach is entitled, **whether or not actual damage or loss is proved** to have been caused thereby, to receive … **reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount so named**."

1. Under s. 74, the distinction between **penalty** and **liquidated damages** does not matter; the named sum is a **ceiling** — here **Rs. 4,000,000** (8 weeks × Rs. 500,000).
2. The court awards only **reasonable compensation** within that ceiling (*Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Dass*, AIR 1963 SC 1405).
3. The words "whether or not actual damage or loss is proved" relieve the claimant from proving the **exact** amount of loss, but the court must still be satisfied that the sum awarded is **reasonable**, having regard to the loss that resulted and the duty to mitigate (*Maula Bux v. Union of India*, AIR 1970 SC 1955 — where loss can be proved, it should be).

### 8. No double recovery

The s. 74 clause and the s. 73 claim compensate the **same loss** — the loss caused by **delay**. Danish cannot claim **both** Rs. 4 million under the clause **and** Rs. 4 million as export profit. Where the contract names a sum for the breach, **s. 74 governs** and the compensation cannot exceed the sum named (Rs. 4 million) for that breach.

### 9. The advance of Rs. 3 million

The contract was **performed** (late), and the advance is part of the **price** of Rs. 15 million. Danish must pay the **balance** (Rs. 12 million) and may **set off** the compensation awarded (O. VIII r. 6 CPC; legal set-off of an ascertained sum, or equitable set-off arising from the same transaction).

### 10. Likely result

|Head|Likely outcome|
|Export profit Rs. 4,000,000|In principle recoverable (communicated — second limb), but **reduced** for failure to mitigate|
|Local profit Rs. 600,000|In principle recoverable (first limb), but avoidable by hiring|
|Mitigation|Recovery limited to the cost of hire (about Rs. 400,000) plus reasonable expenses, unless hiring was impracticable|
|s. 74 clause|Ceiling of Rs. 4,000,000; the court awards **reasonable compensation** within it|

A court is likely to award **reasonable compensation** in the region of the **cost of mitigation** plus reasonable incidental losses — far less than the Rs. 8 million claimed. If Danish proves that no substitute machine was available, the award could approach the **Rs. 4 million ceiling**, but cannot exceed it.

### 11. Conclusion

Khurram is liable for the **eight-week delay** (subject to Danish's reservation of rights under s. 55). The **export profit** was within the parties' contemplation and the **local profit** arose naturally (s. 73; *Hadley v. Baxendale*; *Victoria Laundry*). But Danish's failure to **hire a substitute** limits recovery to what reasonable mitigation would have cost (Explanation to s. 73). The weekly sum in the contract is only a **ceiling** under s. 74 (*Fateh Chand*), and the same loss cannot be recovered twice. Danish should receive **reasonable compensation not exceeding Rs. 4 million**, most likely close to the cost of mitigation.
