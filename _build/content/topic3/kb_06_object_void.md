
# Chapter 6 · Lawful consideration and lawful object (ss. 23, 24)

## 6.1 Consideration and object — two different things

- **Consideration** is what each party **gives** or promises in exchange — the **price** of the promise (s. 2(d)).
- **Object** is the **purpose** or design of the agreement — **what the parties want to achieve** by it.

Usually the two coincide, but not always. Example: A lends B Rs. 500,000 (consideration — the loan), but both know B will use it to bribe an official (object). The **consideration** (money) is lawful in itself, but the **object** is unlawful, so the agreement is void. (This is the LHC ASJ 2020 question.)

## 6.2 When are they unlawful — s. 23

> "23. The consideration or object of an agreement is lawful, unless— it is **forbidden by law**; or is of such a nature that, if permitted, it would **defeat the provisions of any law**; or is **fraudulent**; or involves or implies **injury to the person or property** of another; or the Court regards it as **immoral**, or **opposed to public policy**.
> In each of these cases, the consideration or object of an agreement is said to be unlawful. Every agreement of which the object or consideration is unlawful is **void**."

![Section 23 — the six heads of unlawfulness](s23_heads.png)

### Head 1 — Forbidden by law

Anything **prohibited** by a statute, rule or regulation having the force of law — whether **expressly** or **by necessary implication** (e.g., where a statute imposes a penalty on doing an act without a licence, and its purpose is to protect the public). *Illustration (f):* A promises to obtain for B an **employment in the public service**, and B promises to pay Rs. 1,000 — void (sale of public office; also an offence of bribery).

- A contract to sell **narcotics**, to smuggle goods, or to sell a **liquor** permit — forbidden.
- An agreement to pay a **bribe** for a government contract.
- **Riba** — see Chapter 12 for how interest stipulations are viewed in Pakistan today.

Not every statutory requirement makes a contract void: if a statute merely imposes a **penalty for revenue purposes** (e.g., non-registration of a trader for tax), the contract itself may remain valid. The test is the **object of the statute** — whether it intended to prohibit the contract.

### Head 2 — Would defeat the provisions of any law

An act lawful on its face, but which, if allowed, would **circumvent** a statute. *Illustration (i):* an estate sold for arrears of revenue; the law forbids the defaulter to buy it; B buys it on an understanding to reconvey to A — void, as it defeats the law. Similar: a **benami** purchase to defeat creditors; an agreement to evade **stamp duty** or **taxes**.

### Head 3 — Fraudulent

*Illustration (e):* A, B and C agree to divide gains acquired by **fraud** — void. *Illustration (g):* an agent agrees, without his principal's knowledge, to obtain for B a lease of the principal's land for money — void (fraud by concealment on the principal).

### Head 4 — Injury to person or property of another

"Injury" means a **wrongful** act — criminal or tortious. Examples: an agreement to publish a **defamatory** statement; to infringe copyright; to commit trespass. A loan bond under which the debtor would work for the creditor **without wages** until repayment (bonded labour) is void as injurious to the person — and now an offence under the **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1992**.

### Head 5 — Immoral

"Immoral" in s. 23 is understood as **sexual immorality** and similar matters the court regards as contrary to accepted moral standards — not every moral failing. *Illustration (k):* A agrees to let her daughter to hire to B for **concubinage** — void, though the letting may not be punishable. *Illustration (j):* A, who is B's **mukhtar**, promises to use his influence with B in favour of C, for Rs. 1,000 — void as immoral. A landlord who knowingly lets premises for prostitution cannot recover rent (*Pearce v. Brooks* (1866) LR 1 Ex 213 — hire of a carriage to a prostitute for her trade).

### Head 6 — Opposed to public policy

"Public policy" means the **interest of the public** — the principle that no one may lawfully do what tends to injure the public or is against the public good.

:::maxim Public policy is "a very unruly horse"
"…and when once you get astride it you never know where it will carry you" — Burrough J in *Richardson v. Mellish* (1824) 2 Bing 229. Courts therefore apply the doctrine **cautiously**, within **established heads**, and should not invent new heads lightly (*Gherulal Parakh v. Mahadeodas Maiya*, AIR 1959 SC 781).
:::

**Established heads of public policy:**

1. **Trading with an enemy** in wartime.
2. **Stifling prosecution** — *illustration (h)*: A promises to drop a **robbery** prosecution if B restores the value of the things taken — void. (A compromise of a **compoundable** offence under s. 345, CrPC, with the court's permission where required, is lawful. Also, where a civil dispute is settled and the criminal complaint is withdrawn, the settlement is valid **unless** the withdrawal was **part of the consideration**: *Kamini Kumar Basu v. Birendra Nath Basu*, AIR 1930 PC 100.)
3. **Interference with the course of justice** — bribing a witness or judge; an agreement to give false evidence.
4. **Maintenance and champerty** — see below.
5. **Sale of public offices and titles**; agreements to procure government employment (*illustration (f)*).
6. **Marriage brokerage** — paying a person to procure a marriage is contrary to public policy (in some cases).
7. Agreements in **restraint of marriage**, **trade**, or **legal proceedings** (ss. 26–28 — made expressly void).
8. **Creating monopolies** and agreements affecting the freedom of trade (now also the **Competition Act 2010**).
9. Agreements tending to create **interest against duty** — e.g., a public servant or agent agreeing to take a secret profit.
10. Agreements to **defraud the revenue** or creditors.

### Maintenance and champerty

- **Maintenance:** helping another to bring or defend a suit in which the helper has no interest.
- **Champerty:** maintenance in return for a **share** of the property or proceeds recovered.

:::case Ram Coomar Coondoo v. Chunder Canto Mookerjee ((1876) ILR 2 Cal 233, Privy Council)
__Held.__ The English law of maintenance and champerty does **not** apply as such in the subcontinent. A **fair** agreement to supply funds for litigation in return for a share of the result is **not** in itself void. It is void only if it is **extortionate** or **unconscionable**, or made for an **improper object** (e.g., gambling in litigation, injuring others, or oppressing the defendant).
:::

**Advocates.** Under the professional conduct rules (Pakistan Legal Practitioners and Bar Councils Act 1973 and the **Canons of Professional Conduct and Etiquette**), an advocate may not stipulate for a **share in the subject matter** of litigation (a contingency fee by share) — that is professional misconduct even if not void under s. 23.

## 6.3 The effect of illegality

:::maxim Ex turpi causa non oritur actio — no action arises from a base cause
A court will not help a person who founds his claim on an illegal or immoral act. Lord Mansfield in *Holman v. Johnson* (1775) 1 Cowp 341: "No court will lend its aid to a man who founds his cause of action upon an immoral or an illegal act." This rule is **not** for the benefit of the defendant; it is a rule of **public policy** — the court protects its own integrity.
:::

:::maxim In pari delicto potior est conditio defendentis — where both are equally at fault, the defendant's position is stronger
Money paid or property delivered under an illegal agreement cannot generally be recovered. The loss lies where it falls.
:::

**Exceptions** — recovery may be allowed:

1. Where the parties are **not in pari delicto** — e.g., one party was a victim of **fraud, coercion or undue influence**, or the law was meant to **protect** that class of persons (*Kiriri Cotton Co. v. Dewani* [1960] AC 192, PC — a tenant who paid an illegal premium could recover it, because the rent law aimed to protect tenants).
2. **Locus poenitentiae** — a party who **repents** and withdraws **before** the illegal purpose is carried out may recover what he paid (*Taylor v. Bowers* (1876) 1 QBD 291).
3. Where the claim can be proved **without relying on the illegality** — e.g., a claim based on independent title.

### Collateral transactions

When the main agreement is **illegal** (forbidden by law, immoral, etc.), agreements **collateral** to it — knowingly made to help carry it out — are **also void**. But when an agreement is merely **void** (not illegal — e.g., a wager in India), collateral agreements are valid. In **Pakistan**, ss. 30A–30C expressly make agreements collateral to **wagers** void (Chapter 7).

|Punishment|Parties not punished|Parties may be punished (e.g., PPC)|

## 6.4 Partial illegality — s. 24 and severability

> "24. If **any part of a single consideration** for one or more objects, or any one or any part of any one of several considerations for a single object, is unlawful, the agreement is **void**."

*Illustration:* A promises to superintend, for B, a **legal manufacture of indigo** and an **illegal traffic** in other articles, for a salary of Rs. 10,000 a year — void, because the single consideration (salary) is for objects partly unlawful and **cannot be apportioned**.

**Severability.** Where the lawful part **can be separated** from the unlawful part, the lawful part stands. This is what **s. 57** (reciprocal promises: legal and illegal acts) and **s. 58** (alternative promise, one branch illegal) provide:

- **s. 57:** where persons promise to do certain things which are **legal**, and also, under specified circumstances, certain things which are **illegal**, the first set is a contract and the second a void agreement. *Illustration:* A agrees to sell B a house for Rs. 10,000, but if B uses it as a gambling house, he shall pay Rs. 50,000 — the first is valid, the second void.
- **s. 58:** in an alternative promise, one branch legal and the other illegal, only the **legal branch** can be enforced. *Illustration:* A and B agree that A shall pay B Rs. 1,000, for which B shall afterwards deliver either rice or smuggled opium — valid for rice, void for opium.

:::recap Chapter 6 in six lines
- Consideration = price; object = purpose. Both must be lawful (s. 23).
- Six heads: forbidden by law; defeats any law; fraudulent; injury to person or property; immoral; opposed to public policy.
- Public policy — "unruly horse"; established heads only (*Gherulal Parakh*); stifling prosecution, trading with enemy, sale of offices, interference with justice; champerty not void unless unconscionable (*Ram Coomar Coondoo*).
- *Ex turpi causa*; *in pari delicto* — no recovery, except not in pari delicto (*Kiriri Cotton*), locus poenitentiae, independent title.
- Illegal agreements taint collateral transactions; merely void ones do not (except wagers in Pakistan, s. 30A).
- s. 24: single consideration partly unlawful — wholly void; severable parts saved (ss. 57, 58).
:::

# Chapter 7 · Agreements expressly declared void (ss. 26 to 30C)

The Act lists agreements it **expressly** declares void. Together with s. 25 (no consideration), s. 24 (partly unlawful), s. 20 (bilateral mistake) and s. 56 (impossibility), they form the list in s. 10 ("not hereby expressly declared to be void").

![Agreements expressly declared void](void_agreements.png)

## 7.1 Restraint of marriage — s. 26

> "26. Every agreement in restraint of the marriage of any person, **other than a minor**, is void."

**Rationale.** Marriage is a fundamental personal freedom and a social institution; nobody should be paid or bound to give it up. Examples: A promises B Rs. 1 million if B never marries — void. A promise not to marry **anyone other than** a particular person has also been held void. An agreement **in restraint of the marriage of a minor** is **not** covered — indeed restraining child marriage is in line with the **Child Marriage Restraint Act 1929** and the **ICT Child Marriage Restraint Act 2025** (fixing 18 years for both).

Note that **conditions in a nikahnama** (e.g., a stipulation that the husband will not take a second wife without the first wife's consent, or the delegation of the right of divorce — *talaq-e-tafweez*) are not "restraint of marriage" agreements under s. 26; they are regulated by Muslim personal law and the **Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961**.

## 7.2 Restraint of trade — s. 27

> "27. Every agreement by which any one is **restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business** of any kind, is **to that extent void**.
> Exception 1.— One who **sells the good-will** of a business may agree with the buyer to refrain from carrying on a similar business, **within specified local limits**, so long as the buyer, or any person deriving title to the good-will from him, carries on a like business therein: Provided that such limits appear to the Court **reasonable**, regard being had to the nature of the business."

**Rationale.** Everyone must be free to earn a living, and the public benefits from competition. The Act (drafted in 1872) took a **stricter** line than English law: **any** restraint, whether **total or partial**, reasonable or not, is void unless it falls within the **statutory exception**.

:::case Madhub Chunder v. Rajcoomar Doss ((1874) 14 Beng LR 76)
__Facts.__ The defendant agreed to close his shop in a particular locality of Calcutta in return for the plaintiff paying his debts.

__Held.__ The agreement was **void** under s. 27. Unlike English law, the Act does not distinguish between **partial** and **total** restraint; a restraint of **any** kind, even limited to a locality, is void unless within the exception.
:::

:::case Nordenfelt v. Maxim Nordenfelt Guns and Ammunition Co. ([1894] AC 535, House of Lords)
__Held.__ (English law) A restraint of trade is **prima facie void**, but can be justified if it is **reasonable** — in the interests of the **parties** and the **public**. A worldwide 25-year covenant by the seller of an armaments business was reasonable given the worldwide nature of the business. (Useful for contrast; our Act allows only the statutory exception.)
:::

### The goodwill exception — conditions

1. The promisor must have **sold the goodwill** of a business;
2. the restraint is on carrying on a **similar business**;
3. it applies within **specified local limits**;
4. it lasts so long as the **buyer** (or his successor in title to the goodwill) carries on a **like business** there; and
5. the limits must be **reasonable** in the court's opinion, considering the nature of the business.

**Partnership:** restraints on partners (during partnership, on outgoing partners, and on dissolution) are allowed by **ss. 11, 36, 54 and 55 of the Partnership Act 1932** — formerly the second and third exceptions to s. 27, now removed from the Contract Act.

### Employment covenants

{w:30,70}
|Situation|Position|
|Restraint **during** employment (not to work for others while in service)|**Valid** — it is not a restraint of trade but a term to ensure faithful service (*Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. Century Spinning*, AIR 1967 SC 1098 — a negative covenant during the term of a fixed-period contract was enforced by injunction).|
|Restraint **after** employment ends (not to join a competitor or start a similar business)|**Void** under s. 27, however reasonable (*Superintendence Co. of India v. Krishan Murgai*, (1981) 2 SCC 246).|
|**Confidentiality** / non-disclosure of trade secrets|**Valid** — it protects property in confidential information, and does not restrain the trade itself.|
|Non-solicitation of clients during service|Valid; after service — doubtful, generally treated as restraint if it prevents trade.|

**Injunction to enforce negative covenant:** s. 57, **Specific Relief Act** (Pakistan; Indian s. 42) allows an injunction to enforce a **negative** agreement attached to an affirmative one, even if the affirmative part cannot be specifically enforced (*Lumley v. Wagner* (1852) — the opera singer). This applies to valid negative covenants (e.g., exclusive service during the term), not to covenants void under s. 27.

### Trade combinations and exclusive dealing

Agreements that **regulate** business rather than restrain it — e.g., **sole selling agency**, **exclusive dealing** (A agrees to buy all his requirements from B; B agrees to supply only A in a territory) — are generally **not** void under s. 27, because they promote rather than restrict trade, unless they are unreasonably restrictive. Anti-competitive agreements are now regulated by the **Competition Act 2010** (s. 4, prohibited agreements, enforced by the **Competition Commission of Pakistan**).

## 7.3 Restraint of legal proceedings — s. 28

> "28. Every agreement, by which any party thereto is **restricted absolutely** from enforcing his rights under or in respect of any contract, by the usual legal proceedings in the ordinary tribunals, or which **limits the time** within which he may thus enforce his rights, is **void to that extent**.
> Exception 1.— This section shall not render illegal a contract by which two or more persons agree that any **dispute which may arise** between them … shall be referred to **arbitration** … [and] a suit may be brought for its specific performance; and if a suit, other than for such specific performance or for the recovery of the amount so awarded, is brought … in respect of any subject which they have so agreed to refer, the existence of such contract shall be a **bar to the suit**.
> Exception 2.— Nor shall this section render illegal any contract **in writing**, by which two or more persons agree to refer to arbitration any **question between them which has already arisen**, or affect any provision of any law in force for the time being as to references to arbitration."

### Two kinds of void clause

1. **Absolute restriction** from suing in ordinary courts — e.g., "Neither party shall have any remedy in any court". Void.
2. **Shortening limitation** — e.g., "Any suit must be filed within 3 months of the breach" (where the Limitation Act gives 3 years). Void to that extent.

**Distinction — extinction of rights.** A clause which provides that the **rights themselves** shall be **forfeited** or **extinguished** if a claim is not made within a time (common in **insurance** policies: "the claim is forfeited unless made within 12 months") has been held **not** to fall within the Pakistani-form s. 28, because it does not limit the time to sue — it ends the right itself. (India amended its s. 28 in 1997 to make such clauses void; **Pakistan has not**.)

### What is allowed

- **Arbitration agreements** for future disputes (Exception 1) and present disputes (Exception 2, in writing). Arbitration in Pakistan is governed by the **Arbitration Act 1940** (stay of suit under s. 34), and foreign awards by the **Recognition and Enforcement (Arbitration Agreements and Foreign Arbitral Awards) Act 2011**.
- **Exclusive jurisdiction clauses** — where **two or more courts** have jurisdiction, parties may agree to sue in **one** of them. That is not an absolute restriction (*ABC Laminart v. A.P. Agencies*, (1989) 2 SCC 163). But parties **cannot confer** jurisdiction on a court that has **none** under s. 20, CPC (Topic 1).
- **Ouster of courts by statute** (e.g., special tribunals) is not affected by s. 28 — s. 28 deals with **agreements**.

## 7.4 Uncertainty — s. 29

> "29. Agreements, the meaning of which is **not certain, or capable of being made certain**, are void."

*Illustrations:* (a) "a hundred tons of oil", with nothing to show what kind — void; (b) oil of a specified commercial description — valid; (c) A deals only in **coconut oil** — his trade makes the meaning certain; (d) "all the grain in my granary at **Rahimyarkhan**" — certain; (e) price "to be fixed by C" — capable of being made certain; (f) "my white horse for Rs. 500 **or** Rs. 1,000" — void.

:::maxim Id certum est quod certum reddi potest — that is certain which can be made certain
If the terms can be ascertained by a **method** agreed by the parties (a third person's valuation, market price, trade custom, previous dealings), the agreement is not void for uncertainty.
:::

An **agreement to agree** in future on essential terms is void for uncertainty (e.g., "at a price to be agreed later" with no mechanism) — but the courts try to **uphold** commercial agreements where the parties obviously intended to be bound (e.g., by implying a "reasonable price" under s. 9, Sale of Goods Act).

## 7.5 Wagering agreements — s. 30

> "30. Agreements by way of **wager are void**; and no suit shall be brought for recovering anything alleged to be **won on any wager**, or **entrusted to any person to abide the result** of any game or other uncertain event on which any wager is made.
> Exception in favour of certain prizes for horse-racing.— This section shall not be deemed to render unlawful a subscription, or contribution, or agreement to subscribe or contribute, made or entered into for or toward any plate, prize or sum of money, of the value or amount of **five hundred rupees or upwards**, to be awarded to the winner or winners of any **horse race**.
> Section 294A of the Pakistan Penal Code not affected.— Nothing in this section shall be deemed to legalize any transaction connected with horse-racing, to which the provisions of section 294A of the Pakistan Penal Code apply."

### Meaning of "wager"

The Act does not define it. The classic definition is that of **Hawkins J** in *Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.* ([1892] 2 QB 484, at first instance):

> "A wagering contract is one by which two persons, professing to hold **opposite views** touching the issue of a **future uncertain event**, mutually agree that, dependent upon the determination of that event, one shall **win from the other**, and that other shall pay or hand over to him, a sum of money or other stake; **neither of the contracting parties having any other interest** in that contract than the sum or stake he will so win or lose."

### Essentials of a wager

1. A promise to pay money or money's worth on the **happening or non-happening** of an **uncertain event** (future, or past/present but unknown to the parties);
2. **Mutual chances** of gain and loss — each party must stand to **win or lose**;
3. **Neither party controls** the event;
4. **No other interest** in the event than the stake — no **insurable interest**;
5. **Two parties** (or two sides).

### What is not a wager

{w:28,72}
|Transaction|Why it is not a wager|
|**Insurance** (life, fire, marine)|The insured has an **insurable interest** in the subject; it is a contract of **indemnity** (or, for life insurance, a contingent contract). Without insurable interest, a "policy" would be a wager.|
|**Commercial transactions / futures** with intention of **actual delivery**|Valid sale contracts. But if the real intention is only to **pay differences** in price, without delivery, it is a wager.|
|**Competitions of skill** — crossword, quiz, athletic contests|Success depends on **skill**, not chance. (Competitions depending substantially on **chance** may be **lotteries** — an offence under s. 294A PPC unless authorised.)|
|**Prize bonds**, government-authorised schemes|Lawful under the governing law.|
|**Horse-race prize** of Rs. 500 or more (s. 30 Exception)|Subscription towards a prize to the **winner** is saved, but **betting** on races is not legalised (s. 294A PPC).|
|**Chit funds / committees** (rotating savings)|Ordinarily a lawful arrangement of mutual savings, not a wager.|

### Pakistan's additions — ss. 30A, 30B, 30C (Ordinance XLVII of 1960)

> "30A. All agreements **knowingly** made to **further or assist** the entering into, effecting or carrying out, or to **secure or guarantee** the performance, of any agreement void under section 30, are **void**."

> "30B. No suit or other proceeding shall lie for the recovery of— (a) any sum of money paid or payable in respect of any agreement void under section 30A, or (b) any **commission, brokerage, fee or reward** in respect of knowingly effecting or carrying out, or aiding in effecting or carrying out, of any such agreement …, or (c) any sum of money knowingly paid or payable on account of any person by way of commission, brokerage, fee, reward or other claim in respect of any such agreement."

> "30C. No **guardian, executor, administrator, heir or personal representative** of any minor or deceased person … shall be entitled to or allowed any **credit** in his account for or in respect of any payment made by him on behalf of such minor or deceased person in respect of any such agreement, or any such commission, brokerage, fee, reward or claim as is referred to in sections 30A and 30B."

:::ict Gherulal Parakh is not the law in Pakistan on collateral agreements
In **Gherulal Parakh v. Mahadeodas Maiya** (AIR 1959 SC 781), the Indian Supreme Court held that a wager is **void but not illegal**, so a **collateral** agreement (e.g., a partnership to carry on wagering business, or a loan to pay a wager debt) is **valid**. Pakistan reversed this result by statute **in 1960**: under **s. 30A** collateral agreements knowingly made to further wagers are **void**; under **s. 30B** brokers and agents cannot sue for commission; and under **s. 30C** guardians and executors get no credit for such payments. In an IHC answer, cite Gherulal **only for contrast**.
:::

:::eg Applying ss. 30 and 30A
Bilal and Danish bet Rs. 100,000 on the result of a cricket match; Ehsan lends Bilal Rs. 100,000 knowing it is to pay Danish if Bilal loses. Bilal loses and pays.
1. The bet is void; Danish cannot sue if Bilal had refused to pay (s. 30).
2. Ehsan's loan, knowingly made to carry out the wager, is **void** (s. 30A); he cannot recover it (s. 30B(a)).
3. If Farhan, a "bookie", arranged the bet for commission, he cannot sue for it (s. 30B(b)).
4. Betting may also be an offence (Gambling laws; s. 294A PPC for lotteries).
:::

# Chapter 8 · Contingent contracts (ss. 31 to 36)

## 8.1 Meaning — s. 31

> "31. A 'contingent contract' is a contract to do or not to do something, **if some event, collateral to such contract, does or does not happen**."

*Illustration:* A contracts to pay B Rs. 10,000 if B's house is burnt. This is a contingent contract.

### Essentials

1. Performance depends on the **happening or non-happening** of an event;
2. the event is **uncertain**;
3. the event is **collateral** — not part of the consideration or the promise itself; and
4. the event is **not merely the will** (mere discretion) of the promisor.

**Examples:** contracts of **insurance**, **indemnity** and **guarantee** (Topic 4); an agreement to sell land "**subject to the permission** of the Capital Development Authority"; a promise to pay a bonus "if the project is completed by 30 June".

:::trap Contingent contract vs wager
Both depend on an uncertain event, but a wager has **reciprocal** chances of gain and loss and no interest other than the stake; a contingent contract need not be reciprocal and is valid. A contract of insurance is the classic contingent contract.
:::

## 8.2 Rules for enforcement

(c) A to pay B when B **marries C**; C dies unmarried to B — void.|
|33|Contingent on an event **not happening** — can be enforced when the happening of that event becomes **impossible**, and not before.|A to pay B if a certain ship **does not return**; the ship **sinks** — enforceable.|
|34|If the event is the **future conduct of a living person**, it is deemed impossible when that person does anything that makes it impossible that he should so act within any definite time, or otherwise than under further contingencies.|A to pay B if **B marries C**; C marries D — B's marriage to C is now deemed impossible, though D may die and C may marry B later.|
|35 (1)|Contingent on a specified event **happening within a fixed time** — **void** if the event has not happened by the end of that time, or becomes impossible before it.|(a) A to pay if a ship **returns within a year** — enforceable if it returns; void if burnt within the year.|
|35 (2)|Contingent on a specified event **not happening within a fixed time** — enforceable when the time expires without it, or earlier if it becomes certain it will not happen.|(b) A to pay if a ship **does not return** within a year — enforceable if it does not return, or is burnt within the year.|
|36|Contingent on an **impossible event** — **void**, whether or not the parties knew it was impossible.|(a) If two straight lines should enclose a space — void. (b) If B will marry A's daughter C, who was **already dead** — void.|

:::eg A Pakistani setting
Zahid agrees to sell his plot in Bahria Enclave to Kashif for Rs. 12 million, "subject to the NOC of the Society within 90 days". This is a contingent contract (s. 31). If the NOC is granted within 90 days, the contract becomes enforceable; if the 90 days pass without the NOC, it becomes **void** (s. 35, para 1), and Zahid must refund the advance (s. 65). If the Society refuses the NOC **before** 90 days, the event has become **impossible** and the contract becomes void at once. But if Zahid himself **prevents** the NOC by not applying, he cannot take advantage of his own wrong (s. 53 — prevention by the other party; and the maxim *nullus commodum capere potest de injuria sua propria*).
:::

:::recap Chapter 8 in four lines
- Contingent contract: performance depends on an uncertain, collateral event (s. 31).
- Event happening — wait for it; impossible = void (s. 32). Event not happening — enforceable when it becomes impossible (s. 33). Living person's conduct (s. 34).
- Within fixed time: happen-within — void at expiry; not-happen-within — enforceable at expiry (s. 35).
- Impossible event — void even if parties unaware (s. 36).
:::
