
# Chapter 4 · Capacity to contract — minors and persons of unsound mind (ss. 11, 12, 68)

## 4.1 Who is competent — s. 11

> "11. Every person is competent to contract who is of the **age of majority** according to the law to which he is subject, and who is of **sound mind**, and is **not disqualified** from contracting by any law to which he is subject."

So three classes of persons are **not competent**:

1. **minors**;
2. persons of **unsound mind** (at the time of contracting — s. 12); and
3. persons **disqualified by law**.

**Rationale.** The law of contract assumes that each party can understand his own interest and bargain for it. A child or a person who cannot understand the transaction is easily exploited, so the law protects him by refusing to hold him to his promises — even at the cost of the other party.

### Age of majority in Pakistan

Under the **Majority Act 1875**, s. 3, a person attains majority on completing **18 years**. The exception: where a **guardian of the person or property** has been appointed by a court (under the Guardians and Wards Act 1890) or the property is under a **Court of Wards**, majority comes at **21 years**. The Majority Act does **not** govern marriage, dower, divorce, adoption and religion (s. 2) — those follow personal law.

## 4.2 Nature of a minor's agreement — void ab initio

The Act says that a minor is "not competent"; it does not say what happens to his agreement. The Privy Council supplied the answer.

:::case Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose ((1903) ILR 30 Cal 539, Privy Council)
__Facts.__ Dharmodas, a **minor**, mortgaged his house to a money-lender, Brahmo Dutt, to secure a loan of Rs. 20,000, of which about Rs. 8,000 was actually advanced. The lender's attorney **knew** that Dharmodas was a minor. Dharmodas (through his mother as next friend) sued to have the mortgage declared void and cancelled. The lender (later his widow, Mohori Bibee) asked for **repayment** of the money advanced.

__Held.__
1. A minor's agreement is **void ab initio** (absolutely void), not merely voidable. Sections 10 and 11 make competence an essential of a contract; an agreement by an incompetent person is therefore no contract at all.
2. The lender could **not** recover the money under **s. 64** (restoration on rescission of a voidable contract) or **s. 65** (agreement discovered to be void), because those sections assume a contract between **competent** parties.
3. Relief under **s. 41, Specific Relief Act** (compensation on cancellation) is **discretionary**; it was refused because the lender **knew** of the minority.

__Ratio decidendi.__ An agreement with a minor is void from the beginning; it cannot be enforced against him, and a person who deals with a minor does so at his own risk.
:::

:::maxim Minor's protection — "the law protects the minor even against himself"
The policy is to shield the minor from his own immaturity. Therefore the protection cannot be taken away by the minor's own act — not even by his lie about his age.
:::

## 4.3 The rules on minors — one by one

### Rule 1 — No ratification on majority

Because the agreement is void, there is **nothing to ratify**. A person who, on attaining majority, "ratifies" a loan taken during minority is not bound by the old agreement. If he makes a **fresh promise** for a **fresh consideration**, that new contract binds him. (The past advance is not good consideration for the new promise, because it was not given at the desire of a competent promisor.)

### Rule 2 — No estoppel against a minor

Even if the minor **falsely represented** himself to be a major, he is **not estopped** from pleading minority. Estoppel (Art. 114, Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984) is a rule of evidence and cannot create a contract that the law of contract says cannot exist (*Sadiq Ali Khan v. Jai Kishori*, AIR 1928 PC 152).

### Rule 3 — Restitution: the minor may be made to restore benefits (equity)

If the minor obtained **property or money** by fraud, the court may, on equitable principles, require him to **restore** what he received if it is still **traceable** in his hands — but not to **repay** as if under the contract.

:::case Khan Gul v. Lakha Singh (AIR 1928 Lahore 609, Full Bench)
__Facts.__ A minor, falsely representing himself to be a major, agreed to sell land to the plaintiff and received **Rs. 17,500** as price, but refused to deliver possession. The buyer sued for possession or, in the alternative, refund of the price.

__Held.__ (Sir Shadi Lal C.J.) The contract was void and could not be enforced. But the court could order the minor to **refund** the money he had obtained by fraud. Restitution rests on **equity**, which prevents a minor from using his minority as a **sword** to keep the fruits of his fraud, while the law allows it as a **shield** against liability. The court relied on s. 41 of the Specific Relief Act and the principle behind it.

__Ratio.__ Restitution (giving back what was obtained) is different from enforcing the contract. It may be ordered against a minor who obtained a benefit by fraud.
:::

:::trap Lahore and Allahabad took different views
The Allahabad Full Bench in *Ajudhia Prasad v. Chandan Lal* (AIR 1937 All 610) held that restitution can be ordered under s. 41 SRA only where the **minor is the plaintiff** seeking cancellation — not where he is the **defendant**. The Lahore view (*Khan Gul*) is broader. Since Lahore precedents form part of Pakistan's legal heritage, state the Lahore view and mention the contrary Allahabad view. The English rule is narrower still: *R. Leslie Ltd. v. Sheill* [1914] 3 KB 607 — no refund of **money** (it cannot be traced), but traceable **goods** may be recovered.
:::

### Rule 4 — No specific performance either way

A minor's agreement cannot be specifically enforced **against** him (it is void); nor, by the rule of **mutuality**, **by** him, because the other party could not have enforced it against the minor (*Mir Sarwarjan v. Fakhruddin Mahomed Chowdhury*, (1912) ILR 39 Cal 232, PC — a guardian's agreement to **buy** land for a minor could not be specifically enforced).

### Rule 5 — A minor can be a promisee or beneficiary

Nothing prevents a minor from **receiving** a benefit. A minor may be a **payee** or **endorsee** of a negotiable instrument (s. 26, Negotiable Instruments Act — he binds all parties except himself), a **mortgagee**, or a transferee. He can **enforce** a contract under which he has **fully performed** his part, or which imposes no obligation on him (e.g., a mortgage in his favour where he has advanced money: *Raghava Chariar v. Srinivasa*, (1917) ILR 40 Mad 308, Full Bench).

### Rule 6 — A minor can be an agent

"Any person may become an agent, but no person who is not of the age of majority and of sound mind can become an agent, so as to be responsible to his principal" (s. 184). So the **principal** is bound by acts of a minor agent; the minor is not liable to the principal. (Topic 4.)

### Rule 7 — Partnership

A minor cannot be a partner, but **with the consent of all the partners** he may be **admitted to the benefits** of partnership (s. 30, Partnership Act 1932); within six months of majority he must elect to become a partner or not.

### Rule 8 — Guardian's contracts

A contract made by a **guardian** on behalf of the minor, **within his authority** and **for the minor's benefit**, binds the minor's estate and can be enforced by or against the minor (*Subrahmanyam v. Subba Rao*, AIR 1948 PC 95). The Guardians and Wards Act 1890, s. 29, restricts a court-appointed guardian from selling or mortgaging the ward's immovable property without the court's **permission**.

### Rule 9 — Necessaries — s. 68

> "68. If a person, incapable of entering into a contract, or any one whom he is legally bound to support, is supplied by another person with **necessaries suited to his condition in life**, the person who has furnished such supplies is entitled to be **reimbursed from the property** of such incapable person."

*Illustration (a):* A supplies B, a lunatic, with necessaries suitable to his condition in life. A is entitled to be reimbursed from B's property. *Illustration (b):* A supplies the wife and children of B, a lunatic, with necessaries suitable to their condition in life. A is entitled to be reimbursed from B's property.

Points to note:

1. The liability is **quasi-contractual** — not a contract (it is in Chapter V, "certain relations resembling those created by contract").
2. Only the minor's **property** is liable; there is **no personal liability**.
3. "Necessaries" means not only bare necessities (food, clothing, shelter) but goods and services **suited to his condition in life** — including education, medical care, legal services to protect his property, and the cost of a necessary marriage. Luxuries are excluded. Whether something is a necessary is a question of **fact**, judged by the minor's status **and** his **actual needs at the time** (if he already had an adequate supply, further supply is not "necessary": *Nash v. Inman* [1908] 2 KB 1 — eleven fancy waistcoats for a Cambridge undergraduate).
4. The supplier must prove both that the goods were necessaries **and** that the minor was not already sufficiently supplied.

### Rule 10 — Minor's liability in tort

A minor is liable for his **torts**, but a plaintiff cannot convert a contract claim into a tort claim to get round minority (*R. Leslie v. Sheill* — fraudulently borrowing money is not actionable as deceit to recover the loan).

### Rule 11 — Surety for a minor

If a minor's loan is guaranteed, the **surety** may be held liable as a **principal debtor**, because the guarantee was given precisely to cover the risk of the minor's incapacity (the point is debated; see Topic 4, s. 128).

## 4.4 Persons of unsound mind — s. 12

> "12. A person is said to be of sound mind for the purpose of making a contract if, at the time when he makes it, he is **capable of understanding** it and of **forming a rational judgment** as to its effect upon his interests.
> A person who is usually of unsound mind, but occasionally of sound mind, may make a contract when he is of sound mind.
> A person who is usually of sound mind, but occasionally of unsound mind, may not make a contract when he is of unsound mind."

*Illustrations:* (a) a patient in a lunatic asylum may contract during **lucid intervals**; (b) a sane man **delirious from fever** or **so drunk** that he cannot understand the terms cannot contract while the delirium or drunkenness lasts.

### The two-part test

1. **Understanding** — can he understand the nature of the transaction?
2. **Rational judgment** — can he judge its effect on **his own interests**?

The relevant time is **the time of making the contract**. The **burden of proof**: a person is presumed sane; whoever alleges unsoundness must prove it. But if a person is shown to be **usually of unsound mind**, the burden shifts to the person who says the contract was made in a **lucid interval**.

### Effect

The Act treats a person of unsound mind like a minor: his agreement is **void** (ss. 10–12, following *Mohori Bibee*), with liability for **necessaries** from his property (s. 68). Other cases of incapacity:

- **Illness, old age, brain injury** — the question is always the s. 12 test **at the time**. Weakness short of incapacity may still matter under **undue influence** (s. 16(2)(b): "mental capacity temporarily or permanently affected by reason of age, illness, or mental or bodily distress").
- **Intoxication** — illustration (b) to s. 12.
- **Mental Health Ordinance 2001** — a court may appoint a manager or guardian for the property of a mentally disordered person; contracts relating to such property must then be made through that manager.

## 4.5 Persons disqualified by law

The Act refers to persons "disqualified from contracting by any law to which he is subject". Examples:

1. **Alien enemies** — cannot sue in Pakistani courts without the Federal Government's permission (s. 83, CPC); contracts with alien enemies during war are void or suspended on grounds of public policy.
2. **Foreign States, rulers and diplomats** — cannot be sued without the consent of the Federal Government (s. 86, CPC; State Immunity Ordinance 1981), though they can contract.
3. **Insolvents** — an undischarged insolvent cannot deal with property vested in the official receiver or assignee.
4. **Companies and statutory bodies** — can contract only within their **objects and powers**; a contract **ultra vires** a statutory corporation is void.
5. **Public servants** — may be prohibited by service rules from certain contracts (e.g., purchasing property at auctions they conduct).

:::recap Chapter 4 in seven lines
- Competent = major (18; 21 if court guardian) + sound mind + not disqualified (s. 11; Majority Act s. 3).
- Minor's agreement is **void ab initio**; no refund under ss. 64–65 (*Mohori Bibee*).
- No ratification; no estoppel (*Sadiq Ali Khan*); restitution of fraud benefits in equity (*Khan Gul*, Lahore FB; contrast *Ajudhia Prasad*; *Leslie v. Sheill*).
- No specific performance either way (*Mir Sarwarjan*); minor can be promisee / beneficiary / agent / admitted to partnership benefits.
- Necessaries — reimbursement from property only (s. 68; *Nash v. Inman*).
- Unsound mind: capable of understanding + rational judgment **at the time** (s. 12); lucid intervals; drunkenness.
- Disqualified: alien enemies (s. 83 CPC), foreign states (s. 86 CPC), insolvents, ultra vires bodies.
:::

# Chapter 5 · Free consent (ss. 13 to 22)

## 5.1 Consent and free consent

> "13. Two or more persons are said to consent when they **agree upon the same thing in the same sense**."

This is *consensus ad idem* — a meeting of minds. If there is **no consent at all** (the parties are at cross-purposes about the very thing), the agreement is **void**.

:::case Raffles v. Wichelhaus ((1864) 2 H & C 906)
__Facts.__ Cotton was sold "to arrive ex *Peerless* from Bombay". There were two ships called *Peerless*, sailing in October and December. The buyer meant one, the seller the other.

__Held.__ There was **no consensus ad idem**, so no contract.
:::

> "14. Consent is said to be **free** when it is not caused by— (1) coercion, as defined in section 15, or (2) undue influence, as defined in section 16, or (3) fraud, as defined in section 17, or (4) misrepresentation, as defined in section 18, or (5) mistake, subject to the provisions of sections 20, 21 and 22.
> Consent is said to be so caused when it **would not have been given but for** the existence of such coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake."

The last paragraph lays down a **"but for" causation** test: the defect must have **caused** the consent. If a person would have entered the contract anyway, he cannot avoid it.

## 5.2 Coercion — s. 15

> "15. 'Coercion' is the **committing, or threatening to commit, any act forbidden by the Pakistan Penal Code** (XLV of 1860) or the **unlawful detaining, or threatening to detain, any property**, to the prejudice of any person whatever, with the intention of causing any person to enter into an agreement.
> Explanation.— It is immaterial whether the Pakistan Penal Code is or is not in force in the place where the coercion is employed."

*Illustration:* A, on board an English ship on the high seas, causes B to enter into an agreement by an act amounting to criminal intimidation under the PPC. A later sues B at **Karachi**. A has employed coercion, though the act is not an offence by English law.

### Ingredients

1. Committing or threatening an act **forbidden by the PPC**, **or** unlawfully **detaining** or threatening to detain **property**;
2. to the prejudice of **any person whatever** — the threat may be directed at a third person (e.g., the victim's son), and may be made by **anyone**, not necessarily a party;
3. with the **intention** of causing **any person** to enter into an agreement.

:::case Chikham Amiraju v. Chikham Seshamma ((1917) ILR 41 Mad 33)
__Facts.__ A Hindu husband threatened to **commit suicide** unless his wife and son executed a release in favour of his brother regarding certain property. They executed it.

__Held.__ (Majority) The threat of suicide was **coercion**. Attempt to commit suicide was punishable under the Penal Code (s. 309), so a threat to commit it is a threat to commit an act "forbidden" by the Code. The release was voidable.

__Note for Pakistan.__ Attempted suicide (s. 325 PPC) was **decriminalised** by the Criminal Laws (Amendment) Act 2022, so the same facts today would more naturally fall under **undue influence** or general pressure. Mention this as a point of analysis.
:::

:::case Ranganayakamma v. Alwar Setti ((1889) ILR 13 Mad 214)
__Facts.__ A 13-year-old widow was prevented by relatives of her late husband from removing his body for cremation until she **adopted** a boy of their choosing.

__Held.__ The adoption was not binding; her consent was obtained by **coercion** (obstructing a corpse's removal for funeral rites is an offence under the Penal Code — s. 297).
:::

### Coercion vs duress (English law)

{w:24,38,38}
|Point|Coercion (Pakistani Act)|Duress (English common law)|
|Acts covered|Any act forbidden by the PPC + unlawful detention of property|Originally violence or threats to the person; now also economic duress|
|Against whom|"Any person whatever" — a stranger too|Originally the contracting party or close family|
|By whom|Any person, even a stranger to the contract|Party or with his knowledge|
|Penal Code|Immaterial whether in force where used|—|

**Economic duress.** English law now recognises **economic duress** — illegitimate commercial pressure, such as a threat to break a contract unless more money is paid (*Pao On v. Lau Yiu Long* [1980] AC 614; *The Atlantic Baron* [1979] QB 705). Under our Act, such pressure is not "coercion" unless it involves a PPC offence or unlawful detention of property, but it may amount to **undue influence** (where there is domination) or be relevant to **s. 72** (money paid under coercion).

**s. 72.** "A person to whom money has been paid, or anything delivered, by mistake or under coercion, must repay or return it." (Chapter 11.)

## 5.3 Undue influence — ss. 16 and 19A

> "16. (1) A contract is said to be induced by 'undue influence' where the relations subsisting between the parties are such that one of the parties is **in a position to dominate the will** of the other and **uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage** over the other.
> (2) In particular and without prejudice to the generality of the foregoing principle, a person is deemed to be in a position to dominate the will of another— (a) where he holds a **real or apparent authority** over the other or where he stands in a **fiduciary relation** to the other; or (b) where he makes a contract with a person whose **mental capacity is temporarily or permanently affected** by reason of **age, illness, or mental or bodily distress**.
> (3) Where a person who is in a position to dominate the will of another, enters into a contract with him, and the transaction appears, on the face of it or on the evidence adduced, to be **unconscionable**, the **burden of proving** that such contract was not induced by undue influence shall lie upon the person in a position to dominate the will of the other.
> Nothing in this sub-section shall affect the provisions of section 111 of the Evidence Act, 1872 [now **Article 127, Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984**]."

*Illustrations:* (a) a father, by misusing parental influence, obtains from his son just come of age a bond for more than is due — undue influence; (b) a medical attendant induces an old, sick patient to pay an unreasonable fee — undue influence; (c) a village money-lender's fresh loan on unconscionable terms — burden on the lender; (d) a bank charging a high rate during money-market stringency — **ordinary business**, not undue influence.

### The three questions — Raghunath Prasad

:::case Raghunath Prasad v. Sarju Prasad ((1923) 51 IA 101, Privy Council)
__Facts.__ A Hindu, involved in litigation with his father and in urgent need of money, borrowed Rs. 10,000 at **24 % compound interest**. The lender later sued for over Rs. 1,00,000.

__Held.__ The court must ask in order:
1. Were the relations between the parties such that one was **in a position to dominate** the will of the other?
2. If yes, has he **used that position** to obtain an **unfair advantage**?
3. If the transaction is **unconscionable**, the **burden shifts** to the dominant party to prove that it was **not** induced by undue influence.

Mere urgent need for money and a high rate of interest do **not** by themselves show that the lender was in a position to dominate the borrower's will. Undue influence was not proved; but the court **reduced compound interest** to simple interest under its equitable powers.
:::

### Relationships of domination (presumed)

- **Real authority** — father and son, guardian and ward, employer and employee (in some cases), religious or spiritual adviser and disciple (*Mannu Singh v. Umadat Pande*, (1890) ILR 12 All 523 — a guru induced a disciple to gift all his property).
- **Apparent authority** — a police officer and an accused, a revenue officer and a cultivator.
- **Fiduciary relation** — trustee and beneficiary, solicitor (advocate) and client, doctor and patient, spiritual adviser and devotee.
- **Mental distress** — age, illness, bodily or mental distress (s. 16(2)(b)).

### Pardanashin and illiterate women

:::case Farid-un-Nisa v. Mukhtar Ahmad ((1925) 52 IA 342, Privy Council)
__Held.__ Where a **pardanashin** woman executes a deed, the person relying on it must prove **affirmatively** that the deed was **explained** to her and **understood** by her, that it was her **free and intelligent act**, and ideally that she had **independent advice**. The burden is on the person who seeks to benefit from the deed.
:::

Pakistani courts apply this protective rule broadly to **illiterate, old or dependent women** and to transactions by which a woman gives up her **inheritance** (e.g., *Mst. Janat Bibi v. Sikandar Ali*, PLD 1990 SC 642: where a pardanashin or illiterate woman is said to have transferred property, the beneficiary must prove the transaction was explained to her, understood, and made with independent advice). Article 127 of the Qanun-e-Shahadat places the burden of proving **good faith** on the person who stood in a position of **active confidence**.

### Guarantees given by a wife or parent for a family member's debts

:::case Lloyds Bank Ltd. v. Bundy ([1975] QB 326)
__Facts.__ An elderly farmer, who had banked with Lloyds for years and relied on its manager, mortgaged his only asset — his farmhouse — to guarantee his son's failing company's debts, on the manager's advice. He received no independent advice.

__Held.__ The transaction was set aside. A relationship of **confidence** existed; the bank had a conflict of interest and should have ensured that the father got **independent advice**.
:::

:::case Royal Bank of Scotland v. Etridge (No. 2) ([2001] UKHL 44)
__Held.__ When a wife (or other person in a non-commercial relationship) stands surety for her husband's debts, the bank is "**put on inquiry**". To protect itself, it must take reasonable steps to ensure that the surety had the transaction **explained** by an **independent** legal adviser. If it does not, it is affected by any undue influence or misrepresentation by the husband. (Earlier: *Barclays Bank v. O'Brien* [1994] 1 AC 180.)
:::

### Effect — s. 19A

> "19A. When consent to an agreement is caused by undue influence, the agreement is a contract **voidable** at the option of the party whose consent was so caused. Any such contract may be set aside either **absolutely** or, if the party who was entitled to avoid it has received any **benefit** thereunder, upon such **terms and conditions** as to the Court may seem just."

*Illustration (b) to s. 19A:* a money-lender advances Rs. 100 and by undue influence takes a bond for Rs. 200 at 6 % per month. The court may set aside the bond, ordering repayment of **Rs. 100 with such interest as is just**.

## 5.4 Fraud — s. 17

> "17. 'Fraud' means and includes any of the following acts committed by a **party** to a contract, or with his **connivance**, or by his **agent**, with **intent to deceive** another party thereto or his agent, or to induce him to enter into the contract:— (1) the **suggestion, as a fact, of that which is not true**, by one who does not believe it to be true; (2) the **active concealment** of a fact by one having knowledge or belief of the fact; (3) a **promise made without any intention of performing it**; (4) any other act **fitted to deceive**; (5) any such act or omission as the law specially declares to be fraudulent.
> Explanation.— **Mere silence** as to facts likely to affect the willingness of a person to enter into a contract is **not fraud**, unless the circumstances of the case are such that, regard being had to them, it is the **duty** of the person keeping silence to speak, or unless his silence is, in itself, **equivalent to speech**."

### Ingredients

1. A **representation** (or concealment) of **fact** — not opinion, puffery or a statement of law;
2. **false**, and made **knowingly**, or **without belief** in its truth, or **recklessly** careless whether true or false;
3. by a **party** (or with his connivance or by his agent);
4. with **intent to deceive** or induce;
5. the other party **acted on it** (was actually deceived and induced — s. 19 Explanation); and
6. (for damages) **suffered loss**.

:::case Derry v. Peek ((1889) 14 App Cas 337, House of Lords)
__Facts.__ A company's prospectus said it had the right to use steam power for its trams. In fact, Board of Trade consent was needed and was later refused. The directors honestly believed consent would be a formality.

__Held.__ Fraud is proved when a false representation is made **(i) knowingly, (ii) without belief in its truth, or (iii) recklessly**, careless whether it be true or false. An **honest belief**, however unreasonable, is not fraud. The directors were not liable in deceit.

__Lesson.__ Honest but careless statements are **misrepresentation** (s. 18), not fraud (s. 17).
:::

### Silence — when it is fraud

*Illustrations to s. 17:*

- (a) A sells by auction a horse he knows to be unsound, saying nothing — **not fraud** (*caveat emptor*).
- (b) The buyer is A's daughter just come of age — the **relationship** creates a duty to speak.
- (c) B says: "If you do not deny it, I shall assume the horse is sound." A says nothing — silence is **equivalent to speech**.
- (d) Traders; A has private information of a price change — A is **not bound** to inform B.

**Duty to speak arises:**

1. in contracts **uberrimae fidei** (of the utmost good faith) — **insurance**, family settlements, partnership, company prospectuses;
2. in **fiduciary** relationships;
3. where a statement **true when made becomes false** before the contract (*With v. O'Flanagan* [1936] Ch 575 — the value of a medical practice fell before sale);
4. where a **half-truth** misleads (telling part of the truth so that the whole is false);
5. where a statute requires disclosure — e.g., **s. 55(1)(a), Transfer of Property Act**: the seller of immovable property must disclose **material defects** in the property or title of which he is aware and the buyer is not, and which the buyer could not discover with ordinary care.

:::maxim Caveat emptor — let the buyer beware
In general, a party need not volunteer facts; each party must look after his own interests. The Act's Explanation to s. 17 and s. 16 of the Sale of Goods Act reflect this rule — subject to the exceptions above.
:::

:::ict Sale of goods in Pakistan — the seller must disclose defects
For **sales of goods**, Pakistan has cut down the silence rule by statute. **Section 16A of the Sale of Goods Act** (inserted by Act XVIII of 1994) provides that, "notwithstanding anything contained in section 16, and save where the parties have entered into an agreement to the contrary, the seller shall be under an **obligation to inform the buyer of any defect** in the goods sold at the time of the contract, except in a case where the defect is obviously known to the buyer." So a Pakistani seller of goods who keeps quiet about a defect breaches a statutory duty, even where the general rule in s. 17 would treat mere silence as no fraud. (Full treatment: Topic 4, Chapter 7.)
:::

### Promise without intention to perform

A promise made **without intending to perform it** is fraud (s. 17(3)) — e.g., buying goods on credit with no intention to pay.

### Remedies for fraud

1. **Rescind** the contract (s. 19) — voidable at the option of the deceived party;
2. **affirm** it and insist on being put in the position he would have been in if the representation were true (s. 19, second para; illustration (c) — estate represented free from mortgage: buyer may insist on the mortgage being redeemed);
3. **damages** — fraud is also the **tort of deceit**;
4. restoration on rescission (s. 64).

## 5.5 Misrepresentation — s. 18

> "18. 'Misrepresentation' means and includes— (1) the **positive assertion**, in a manner not warranted by the information of the person making it, of that which is not true, **though he believes it to be true**; (2) any **breach of duty** which, **without an intent to deceive**, gains an advantage to the person committing it, or any one claiming under him, by misleading another to his prejudice …; (3) causing, however **innocently**, a party to an agreement to make a **mistake as to the substance** of the thing which is the subject of the agreement."

{w:24,38,38}
|Point|Fraud (s. 17)|Misrepresentation (s. 18)|
|Belief|Maker knows it is false, or is reckless|Maker honestly believes it to be true|
|Intent|Intent to deceive|No intent to deceive|
|Remedy|Rescind or affirm; **damages** in tort of deceit|Rescind or affirm; **no damages** for innocent misrepresentation (generally)|
|Silence|Can be fraud if duty to speak|Ordinarily not, unless breach of duty under s. 18(2)|
|s. 19 Exception|Does not protect **active** fraud; protects only **fraudulent silence**|Not voidable if truth discoverable with ordinary diligence|

### When the contract is not voidable — s. 19 Exception and Explanation

> "Exception.— If such consent was caused by **misrepresentation** or by **silence, fraudulent within the meaning of section 17**, the contract, nevertheless, is not voidable, if the party whose consent was so caused had the **means of discovering the truth with ordinary diligence**.
> Explanation.— A fraud or misrepresentation which **did not cause the consent** … does not render a contract voidable."

*Illustration (b) to s. 19:* B examined the accounts showing only 400 maunds (not 500) — he was not misled, so the contract is **not voidable**.

:::trap The Exception does not cover active fraud
The s. 19 Exception applies only to **misrepresentation** and **fraudulent silence**. A person who tells a **deliberate lie** cannot say, "You could have found out." (Compare the English rule in *Redgrave v. Hurd* (1881) 20 Ch D 1: it is no defence that the misled party could have discovered the truth.)
:::

## 5.6 Mistake — ss. 20, 21, 22

### Bilateral mistake of fact — s. 20

> "20. Where **both** the parties to an agreement are under a **mistake as to a matter of fact essential to the agreement**, the agreement is **void**.
> Explanation.— An erroneous opinion as to the **value** of the thing which forms the subject-matter of the agreement is not to be deemed a mistake as to a matter of fact."

*Illustrations:* (a) cargo supposed to be on its way from England to **Karachi** had already been lost — void; (b) a horse already dead — void; (c) an estate for B's life sold when B was already dead — void.

Kinds of operative bilateral mistake:

1. **As to the existence** of the subject-matter (*res extincta*) — *Couturier v. Hastie* (1856) 5 HLC 673: a cargo of corn had already been sold by the ship's master because it was fermenting; contract void. (Also s. 7, Sale of Goods Act.)
2. **As to title** (*res sua*) — buying what already belongs to the buyer (*Cooper v. Phibbs* (1867) LR 2 HL 149).
3. **As to identity** of the subject-matter (*Raffles v. Wichelhaus* — really no consensus).
4. **As to quantity, price or a fundamental quality** that makes the thing **essentially different** from what was supposed.

:::case Bell v. Lever Brothers Ltd. ([1932] AC 161, House of Lords)
__Facts.__ Lever Brothers paid large compensation to two directors to terminate their service contracts, not knowing (nor did the directors have it in mind) that the directors had committed breaches that would have allowed dismissal without compensation.

__Held.__ The agreement was **not void**. A mistake as to **quality** makes a contract void only if it makes the thing **essentially and radically different** from what it was believed to be. Here Lever got what it bargained for — termination of the contracts.
:::

### Mistake of law — s. 21

> "21. A contract is **not voidable** because it was caused by a mistake as to **any law in force in Pakistan**; but a mistake as to a law **not in force in Pakistan** has the same effect as a mistake of fact."

*Illustration:* A and B contract on the erroneous belief that a debt is barred by the Pakistan law of limitation — the contract is not voidable.

:::maxim Ignorantia juris non excusat — ignorance of the law is no excuse
Everyone is presumed to know the law of his own country. Foreign law, however, is a matter of **fact** to be proved, so a mistake about it is treated like a mistake of fact.
:::

:::maxim Ignorantia facti excusat — ignorance of fact excuses
A genuine mistake of fact may excuse — which is why s. 20 treats bilateral mistake of fact as making the agreement void.
:::

### Unilateral mistake — s. 22

> "22. A contract is **not voidable** merely because it was caused by **one of the parties** to it being under a mistake as to a matter of fact."

**Rationale.** If one party's private mistake could undo a contract, no contract would be safe. The other party is entitled to rely on outward appearances (the **objective** theory of contract).

:::case Smith v. Hughes ((1871) LR 6 QB 597)
__Facts.__ A buyer bought oats after seeing a sample, believing them to be **old** oats; they were **new** oats. The seller said nothing about their age.

__Held.__ The buyer's mistake as to **quality** did not affect the contract, as long as the seller had not represented them as old oats and the contract did not term them "old". Silence about the buyer's mistake as to quality is not fraud (*caveat emptor*). (In a Pakistani sale of goods, compare the seller's statutory duty to disclose **defects** under SGA s. 16A — a mistake about the age of oats is not a defect, but a hidden flaw would be.)
:::

**Exceptions — when unilateral mistake matters:**

1. **Mistake as to identity of the other party**, where identity is **vital** to the contract — the contract is **void**.

:::case Cundy v. Lindsay ((1878) 3 App Cas 459)
__Facts.__ A rogue named Blenkarn, writing from 37 Wood Street, signed his letters to look like "Blenkiron & Co.", a respectable firm at 123 Wood Street. Lindsay sent goods to him. He sold them to Cundy, an innocent buyer.

__Held.__ Lindsay intended to contract **only with Blenkiron & Co.**; there was **no contract** with Blenkarn (void). Blenkarn got no title, so Cundy got none and had to return the goods.
:::

:::case Phillips v. Brooks Ltd. ([1919] 2 KB 243)
__Facts.__ A rogue in a jeweller's **shop** said he was "Sir George Bullough" and was allowed to take a ring on credit. He pledged it with Brooks.

__Held.__ In a **face-to-face** dealing, the seller is presumed to intend to deal with **the person in front of him**, whoever he is. The contract was **voidable** for fraud, not void; Brooks, who took the ring in good faith **before** avoidance, got a good title. (Followed in *Lewis v. Averay* [1972] 1 QB 198; *Ingram v. Little* [1961] 1 QB 31 went the other way on its facts; *Shogun Finance v. Hudson* [2003] UKHL 62 confirmed the written-contract / face-to-face distinction.)
:::

2. **Mistake as to the nature of the document signed** — ***non est factum*** ("it is not my deed").

:::case Foster v. Mackinnon ((1869) LR 4 CP 704)
__Facts.__ An old man with poor eyesight was told the paper he signed was a **guarantee**; it was in fact a **bill of exchange**.

__Held.__ He was not liable, even to an innocent endorsee: his mind did not go with his signature, as he was deceived about the **very nature** of the document, and he was not negligent.
:::

:::case Saunders v. Anglia Building Society (Gallie v. Lee) ([1971] AC 1004, House of Lords)
__Facts.__ An elderly widow signed, without reading (her glasses were broken), a document which she was told was a gift of her house to her nephew. It was in fact a sale to the nephew's business partner, who mortgaged the house.

__Held.__ The plea of *non est factum* **failed**. It is available only where (i) the document signed is **radically or fundamentally different** from what the signer believed, and (ii) the signer was **not careless** in signing. Here the document had the same object — to transfer her house so that money could be raised — and she had been careless.
:::

3. **Where the other party knew of the mistake** about the **terms** (not mere quality) — the contract may not bind (e.g., *Hartog v. Colin & Shields* [1939] 3 All ER 566 — an obvious price-per-pound vs price-per-piece error that the buyer must have noticed).

:::trap Mistake is not the same as fraud
A mistake **caused by** the other party's false statement is dealt with under **fraud or misrepresentation** (ss. 17–19) — voidable. Mistake under ss. 20–22 is a mistake **not induced** by the other party. Misrepresentation s. 18(3) covers innocently **causing** a mistake as to substance.
:::

:::recap Chapter 5 in nine lines
- Consent = same thing in same sense (s. 13; *Raffles v. Wichelhaus*). Free consent = not caused by the five defects ("but for" test, s. 14).
- Coercion: PPC act or unlawful detention of property, by anyone, against anyone (s. 15; *Chikham Amiraju*; *Ranganayakamma*). Voidable (s. 19); refund (s. 72).
- Undue influence: domination + unfair advantage; burden shifts if unconscionable (s. 16; *Raghunath Prasad*); pardanashin rule (*Farid-un-Nisa*; Art. 127 QSO); guarantees (*Bundy*, *Etridge*). Voidable / set aside on terms (s. 19A).
- Fraud: knowingly false, active concealment, promise without intent, any act fitted to deceive (s. 17; *Derry v. Peek*). Silence not fraud unless duty to speak or equivalent to speech.
- Misrepresentation: honest but unwarranted assertion; breach of duty; innocently causing mistake as to substance (s. 18).
- Remedies: rescind or affirm (s. 19); Exception — not for misrepresentation / fraudulent silence if truth discoverable with ordinary diligence; consent must be caused (Explanation).
- Bilateral mistake of essential fact — void (s. 20; *Couturier*; *Bell v. Lever* on quality). Value opinion is not mistake.
- Mistake of Pakistani law — no relief; foreign law = fact (s. 21).
- Unilateral mistake — not voidable (s. 22; *Smith v. Hughes*), except identity (*Cundy* vs *Phillips*) and *non est factum* (*Foster v. Mackinnon*; *Saunders*).
:::
