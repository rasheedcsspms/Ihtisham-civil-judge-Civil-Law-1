# Chapter 1 · What a contract is — the essentials (ss. 2 and 10)

## 1.1 From proposal to contract — the ladder of s. 2

Section 2 builds the idea of a contract step by step:

> "(a) When one person signifies to another his willingness to do or to abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the assent of that other to such act or abstinence, he is said to make a **proposal**: (b) When the person to whom the proposal is made signifies his assent thereto, the proposal is said to be **accepted**. A proposal, when accepted, becomes a **promise**: (c) The person making the proposal is called the '**promisor**', and the person accepting the proposal is called the '**promisee**': (d) When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a **consideration** for the promise: (e) Every promise and every set of promises, forming the consideration for each other, is an **agreement**: (f) Promises which form the consideration or part of the consideration for each other are called **reciprocal promises**: (g) An agreement not enforceable by law is said to be **void**: (h) An agreement enforceable by law is a **contract**: (i) An agreement which is enforceable by law at the option of one or more of the parties thereto, but not at the option of the other or others, is a **voidable contract**: (j) A contract which ceases to be enforceable by law **becomes void** when it ceases to be enforceable."

So: **proposal + acceptance = promise**; **promise + consideration = agreement**; **agreement + enforceability = contract**.

![From proposal to contract — the ladder of section 2](contract_ladder.png)

:::maxim Every contract is an agreement, but every agreement is not a contract
An agreement becomes a contract only when the law will enforce it. Social and domestic arrangements, agreements without consideration, agreements with unlawful objects, and agreements declared void are agreements — but not contracts.
:::

## 1.2 What agreements are contracts — s. 10

> "10. All agreements are contracts if they are made by the **free consent** of parties **competent to contract**, for a **lawful consideration** and with a **lawful object**, and are **not hereby expressly declared to be void**.
> Nothing herein contained shall affect any law in force in Pakistan, and not hereby expressly repealed, by which any contract is required to be made **in writing** or in the presence of **witnesses**, or any law relating to the **registration** of documents."

### The essentials of a valid contract

1. **Offer and acceptance** — there must be an agreement (s. 2(a)–(e)).
2. **Intention to create legal relations** — implied in the words "enforceable by law".
3. **Lawful consideration** (ss. 2(d), 23, 25).
4. **Capacity** of parties (ss. 11–12).
5. **Free consent** (ss. 13–22).
6. **Lawful object** (ss. 23–24).
7. **Not expressly declared void** (ss. 24–30, 56).
8. **Certainty** of meaning (s. 29).
9. **Possibility of performance** (s. 56).
10. **Formalities** — writing, attestation or registration where another law requires them (s. 10, second para).

### Intention to create legal relations

The Act does not say this in words, but it is implied by "enforceable by law". In **commercial** agreements the intention is presumed; in **social and domestic** arrangements the presumption is the other way.

:::case Balfour v. Balfour ([1919] 2 KB 571)
__Facts.__ A husband, going abroad for work, promised to pay his wife £30 a month. Later they separated and he stopped paying. She sued.

__Held.__ The promise was not enforceable. Agreements between husband and wife in the ordinary course of domestic life are not usually intended to have legal consequences; "the small courts of this world" — family homes — are not to be governed by contract law.

__Ratio.__ Where the parties did not intend legal consequences, there is no contract even if there is offer, acceptance and consideration. The intention is judged objectively from the nature of the relationship and the circumstances. (Contrast *Merritt v. Merritt* [1970] 1 WLR 1211, where spouses who had **separated** made a written agreement about the house — held binding.)
:::

### Must a contract be in writing?

**No — as a general rule.** A contract may be **oral**, **written** or **implied from conduct** (s. 9: "In so far as the proposal or acceptance of any promise is made in words, the promise is said to be express. In so far as such proposal or acceptance is made otherwise than in words, the promise is said to be implied."). But s. 10 preserves other laws that require writing or registration. For example:

- **s. 25(1) and (3), Contract Act** — a promise without consideration out of natural love and affection must be **written and registered**; a promise to pay a time-barred debt must be **written and signed**;
- **Transfer of Property Act 1882** — sale of immovable property of Rs. 100 or more, mortgage, lease of immovable property from year to year must be by **registered instrument** (ss. 54, 59, 107);
- **Registration Act 1908, s. 17** — compulsory registration of specified documents;
- **Negotiable Instruments Act 1881** — bills, notes and cheques are in writing by definition;
- **Arbitration Act 1940** — an arbitration agreement must be **written**;
- **Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984, Art. 17** — instruments relating to **financial obligations** or future obligations "shall be attested by two men, or one man and two women", and other matters as the law requires (Art. 17(2)(a)) — an important Pakistani rule of **evidence and form** for loan documents.

:::eg Oral contract
Naeem orally agrees to paint Hira's house in F-8 for Rs. 150,000; he paints it, and she refuses to pay. The contract is valid though oral; Naeem must prove it by evidence. (If Hira's promise were an agreement to sell her house, writing and registration would be needed for the **transfer**, though an oral agreement to sell can be specifically enforced if proved.)
:::

## 1.3 Kinds of contracts and agreements

|**Contract that becomes void** (s. 2(j))|Valid when made, but later ceases to be enforceable — e.g., frustration (s. 56), contingent event becomes impossible (s. 32).|

- Proposal + acceptance = promise; promise + consideration = agreement; enforceable agreement = contract (s. 2).
- s. 10: free consent + competent parties + lawful consideration + lawful object + not declared void.
- Intention to create legal relations — presumed in commerce, not in domestic matters (*Balfour v. Balfour*).
- Writing not generally required (s. 9: express / implied), but other laws may require writing, attestation or registration (s. 10 para 2; Art. 17 QSO; TPA; Registration Act).
- Void vs voidable vs illegal vs unenforceable — know the differences.
- E-contracts recognised (Electronic Transactions Ordinance 2002).

# Chapter 2 · Proposal and acceptance (ss. 2(a)–(b), 3 to 9)

## 2.1 Proposal (offer)

A proposal is a definite expression of **willingness** to be bound on certain terms, made "with a view to obtaining the assent" of the other (s. 2(a)). It may be:

- **specific** — to a particular person; or
- **general** — to the world at large; anyone who performs the conditions accepts it (s. 8).

:::case Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. ([1893] 1 QB 256)
__Facts.__ The company advertised that it would pay £100 to anyone who used its smoke ball three times daily for two weeks and still caught influenza, and deposited £1,000 in a bank to show sincerity. Mrs Carlill used it as directed and caught influenza.

__Held.__ The advertisement was a **general offer** to the world, not a mere puff (the deposit showed serious intent). It was **accepted by performing the conditions**, and **notice of acceptance** was impliedly dispensed with. She was entitled to £100.

__Ratio.__ A general offer can be accepted by anyone who performs its conditions; performance is acceptance (s. 8 of our Act says the same).
:::

### Knowledge of the offer is essential

:::case Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt ((1913) 11 ALJ 489)
__Facts.__ The defendant's nephew absconded. The plaintiff, his servant, was sent to search for him. After he left, the defendant announced a reward for finding the boy. The plaintiff found him without knowing of the reward, and later claimed it.

__Held.__ No contract: a person cannot accept an offer of which he has **no knowledge**. Acceptance means assent to the proposal, which is impossible without knowing it.
:::

### Cross offers

Identical offers made by two parties to each other in ignorance of each other's offer do **not** create a contract, because neither is an **acceptance** of the other.

## 2.2 Invitation to treat — not an offer

An **invitation to treat** (or "invitation to offer") is a statement inviting others to make offers. Responding to it makes an **offer**, which the inviter may accept or reject. Common examples:

1. **Goods displayed** in a shop with price tags.
2. **Advertisements** of goods for sale, catalogues and price lists.
3. **Auction advertisements**; at the auction, the **bid** is the offer and the fall of the hammer the acceptance.
4. **Tender notices** — inviting bids is an invitation; each **bid (tender)** is an offer; the authority may accept the best or none.
5. **Prospectus** inviting applications for shares.
6. **Quotation of price** in reply to an inquiry.

:::case Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v. Boots Cash Chemists ([1953] 1 QB 401)
__Held.__ In a self-service shop, the display of goods with prices is an **invitation to treat**. The customer makes the **offer** at the cash desk, and the shop **accepts** it there (where a registered pharmacist supervised the sale). Otherwise a customer who picked up an item could not change his mind, and the shop could not refuse a sale.
:::

:::case Harvey v. Facey ([1893] AC 552, Privy Council)
__Facts.__ H telegraphed: "Will you sell us Bumper Hall Pen? Telegraph lowest cash price." F replied: "Lowest price for Bumper Hall Pen £900." H replied: "We agree to buy … for £900."

__Held.__ No contract. F's reply only answered the question about **price**; it was **not an offer** to sell. H's last telegram was an offer, which F never accepted.
:::

:::case Spencer v. Harding ((1870) LR 5 CP 561)
__Held.__ A circular inviting **tenders** for the purchase of stock was not an offer to sell to the highest bidder; it was an invitation to make offers. No contract arose merely because the plaintiff's tender was the highest. (Contrast an express promise to accept the **highest** or **lowest** tender, which may itself be an offer.)
:::

:::eg Tenders and bids in Pakistan (LHC 2023 question)
The Capital Development Authority advertises: "Sealed bids are invited for the construction of a community centre in I-10." This is an **invitation to treat**. Each contractor's **bid** is an **offer**. The CDA may accept any bid or reject all (subject to the **Public Procurement Rules 2004** under the PPRA Ordinance 2002, which require transparency and reasons, and allow rejection of all bids before acceptance). Once the CDA communicates **acceptance** (a letter of acceptance), a contract is formed. A bidder may **withdraw** his bid before acceptance (s. 5), unless he has given a separate binding promise (e.g., a bid security forfeited under the tender conditions).
:::

## 2.3 Acceptance — s. 7, s. 8

> "7. In order to convert a proposal into a promise, the acceptance must— (1) be **absolute and unqualified**; (2) be expressed in some **usual and reasonable manner**, unless the proposal prescribes the manner in which it is to be accepted. If the proposal prescribes a manner in which it is to be accepted, and the acceptance is not made in such manner, the proposer may, within a reasonable time after the acceptance is communicated to him, insist that his proposal shall be accepted in the prescribed manner, and not otherwise; but if he fails to do so, he accepts the acceptance."

> "8. Performance of the conditions of a proposal, or the acceptance of any consideration for a reciprocal promise which may be offered with a proposal, is an acceptance of the proposal."

### Rules of acceptance

1. **Absolute and unqualified** — a conditional or varied acceptance is a **counter-offer**, which **destroys** the original offer.
2. **Communicated** to the proposer (s. 3) — mental acceptance is not enough; **silence is not acceptance** unless the parties have agreed otherwise or conduct shows assent.
3. **By the offeree** (or his authorised agent) — a third person cannot accept.
4. In the **prescribed or a usual and reasonable manner** (s. 7(2)).
5. **While the offer is alive** — before lapse or revocation (s. 6).
6. With **knowledge** of the offer (*Lalman Shukla*).

:::case Hyde v. Wrench ((1840) 3 Beav 334)
__Facts.__ W offered to sell his farm for £1,000. H replied offering £950. W refused. H then said he would pay £1,000. W refused.

__Held.__ H's offer of £950 was a **counter-offer** which **rejected and killed** the original offer. H could not later revive it by "accepting" £1,000. (A mere **inquiry** — "Would you accept payment in two instalments?" — is not a counter-offer.)
:::

:::case Felthouse v. Bindley ((1862) 11 CBNS 869)
__Facts.__ An uncle wrote to his nephew: "If I hear no more about him, I consider the horse mine at £30 15s." The nephew did not reply, though he intended to sell.

__Held.__ No contract. The offeror **cannot impose silence** on the offeree as acceptance. Acceptance must be communicated.
:::

## 2.4 Communication — s. 4 (when complete)

> "4. The communication of a proposal is complete when it comes to the knowledge of the person to whom it is made.
> The communication of an acceptance is complete,— as against the proposer, when it is put in a course of transmission to him, so as to be out of the power of the acceptor; as against the acceptor, when it comes to the knowledge of the proposer.
> The communication of a revocation is complete,— as against the person who makes it, when it is put into a course of transmission to the person to whom it is made, so as to be out of the power of the person who makes it; as against the person to whom it is made, when it comes to his knowledge."

{w:26,37,37}
|Communication of|Complete as against the sender|Complete as against the receiver|
|Proposal|—|When it comes to the **knowledge** of the offeree|
|Acceptance|(Proposer is bound) when **put in course of transmission** — e.g., posted|(Acceptor is bound) when it comes to the **knowledge of the proposer**|
|Revocation|When **put in course of transmission**|When it comes to the **knowledge** of the other|

So, under the **postal rule**, the **proposer is bound** as soon as the letter of acceptance is **posted**; the **acceptor is bound** only when it **reaches** the proposer. In between, the acceptor can still revoke his acceptance by a faster means (s. 5).

### Instantaneous communication — telephone, e-mail, chat

:::case Entores Ltd. v. Miles Far East Corporation ([1955] 2 QB 327)
__Held.__ For **instantaneous** communications (telephone, telex), the postal rule does not apply. The contract is complete only when the acceptance is **received** by the offeror, and is made at the place where the acceptance is **received**.
:::

:::case Bhagwandas Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas (AIR 1966 SC 543)
__Held.__ In a contract made over the **telephone**, the contract is complete where the acceptance is **heard** by the proposer, because under s. 4 the communication of acceptance is complete as against the proposer when it comes to his knowledge in instantaneous communication. The court at that place has jurisdiction (cause of action — s. 20 CPC).
:::

**E-mail and messages:** courts generally treat them like instantaneous communications where delivery is immediate, so the acceptance is effective when it reaches the offeror's system / is capable of being read. The **Electronic Transactions Ordinance 2002** recognises electronic communications and contains rules on their attribution and time of dispatch and receipt. In an exam problem, state the s. 4 rule, note the *Entores* / *Bhagwandas* line for instantaneous media, and reach a reasoned conclusion.

## 2.5 Revocation — ss. 5 and 6

> "5. A proposal may be revoked at any time before the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer, but not afterwards. An acceptance may be revoked at any time before the communication of the acceptance is complete as against the acceptor, but not afterwards."

*Illustration (s. 5):* A proposes by post to sell his house to B; B accepts by a letter sent by post. A may revoke his proposal at any time **before or at the moment** when B **posts** his acceptance, but not afterwards. B may revoke his acceptance at any time **before or at the moment** when his letter **reaches A**, but not afterwards.

> "6. A proposal is revoked— (1) by the communication of notice of revocation by the proposer to the other party; (2) by the lapse of the time prescribed in such proposal for its acceptance, or, if no time is so prescribed, by the lapse of a reasonable time, without communication of the acceptance; (3) by the failure of the acceptor to fulfil a condition precedent to acceptance; or (4) by the death or insanity of the proposer, if the fact of his death or insanity comes to the knowledge of the acceptor before acceptance."

Also: an offer lapses on **rejection** or **counter-offer** (*Hyde v. Wrench*). A promise to keep an offer open is not binding **without consideration** (an "option" for which consideration is paid is binding).

:::eg The LHC 2017 car problem — the method
Farooq e-mails: "I will give you Rs. 1,500,000 for the car." (**offer**). Javaid replies: "Interesting suggestion. Would you go to Rs. 1,800,000?" — this is a **counter-offer** (or at least a new proposal) which **rejects** Farooq's offer (*Hyde v. Wrench*). Farooq replies: "Yes. Consider it sold at Rs. 1,800,000. No need to reply. I will collect it later in the week." This is an **acceptance** of Javaid's counter-proposal — absolute and unqualified, communicated by e-mail. Once it reached Javaid, a **contract was formed** (*Entores*; s. 4). Javaid's later sale to another person was a **breach**; when that deal fell through, Javaid could still demand performance — but whether Farooq is bound depends on whether Javaid had **repudiated** (anticipatory breach accepted by Farooq) — see the full model answer in the Q&A volume.
:::

:::recap Chapter 2 in eight lines
- Proposal = willingness to do / abstain, seeking assent (s. 2(a)); specific or general (*Carlill*); knowledge required (*Lalman Shukla*); cross offers make no contract.
- Invitation to treat: displays (*Boots*), price quotations (*Harvey v. Facey*), tenders (*Spencer v. Harding*), auction notices, prospectus.
- Acceptance: absolute and unqualified; counter-offer kills offer (*Hyde v. Wrench*); silence is not acceptance (*Felthouse*); prescribed / reasonable manner (s. 7); performance = acceptance (s. 8).
- s. 4: proposal complete on knowledge; acceptance binds proposer on posting, acceptor on receipt; revocation likewise.
- Instantaneous media: complete where acceptance is received / heard (*Entores*; *Bhagwandas Kedia*); e-contracts recognised (ETO 2002).
- s. 5: revoke proposal before acceptance is posted; revoke acceptance before it reaches proposer.
- s. 6: notice; lapse of time; failure of condition precedent; death / insanity known before acceptance.
- Tenders in Pakistan: bid = offer; letter of acceptance = acceptance; procurement rules regulate rejection.
:::

# Chapter 3 · Consideration and privity (ss. 2(d), 25)

## 3.1 Meaning

> "2(d) When, **at the desire of the promisor**, the **promisee or any other person** has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise."

In simple words, consideration is **the price of the promise** — "something in return" (*quid pro quo*). Without it, an agreement is generally **void** (s. 25).

:::maxim Ex nudo pacto non oritur actio — no action arises from a bare promise
A promise without consideration is a "nude pact" (gratuitous promise) and cannot be sued upon, except in the cases listed in s. 25.
:::

:::maxim Quid pro quo — something for something
Each party gives something in exchange for what he receives. This is the core idea of consideration.
:::

## 3.2 Essentials of consideration

1. **At the desire of the promisor** — an act done at the desire of a third party, or voluntarily, is not consideration.
2. **By the promisee or any other person** — in Pakistan (unlike England), consideration may move from a **third person**.
3. **Past, present or future** — "has done … does … promises to do".
4. **Real, not illusory** — must have some value in law (it need not be **adequate** — s. 25 Expl. 2).
5. **Not something the promisor is already legally bound to do** — performing an existing **legal duty** (e.g., a public servant's official duty) is generally not consideration.
6. **Lawful** (s. 23).

:::case Durga Prasad v. Baldeo ((1880) ILR 3 All 221)
__Facts.__ At the **Collector's** desire, the plaintiff built shops in a bazaar. The defendant, who occupied a shop, later promised to pay the plaintiff a commission on articles sold, in consideration of the plaintiff's expenditure.

__Held.__ The promise was **void** for want of consideration, because the construction was done at the desire of the **Collector**, not of the **promisor** (the defendant).
:::

:::case Chinnaya v. Ramayya ((1882) ILR 4 Mad 137)
__Facts.__ An old woman gifted her estate to her daughter on condition that the daughter would pay an annuity to the old woman's brother (the plaintiff). The daughter promised the brother to pay it, but did not.

__Held.__ The brother could sue. Consideration **moved from a third person** (the mother), which is enough under s. 2(d) ("the promisee **or any other person**"); and the promise was made directly to the brother.
:::

### Past consideration — Pakistan vs England

Under s. 2(d) ("has done"), **past consideration** is good if the past act was done **at the desire of the promisor**. In English law past consideration is generally "no consideration". In addition, s. 25(2) makes enforceable a promise to compensate a person who **voluntarily** did something for the promisor.

### Adequacy

"An agreement to which the consent of the promisor is freely given is not void merely because the consideration is inadequate; but the inadequacy of the consideration may be taken into account by the Court in determining the question whether the consent of the promisor was freely given" (s. 25 Expl. 2). *Illustrations (f) and (g):* a horse worth Rs. 1,000 sold for Rs. 10 — valid if consent was free; inadequacy is evidence on the question of free consent.

## 3.3 Privity of contract

**Privity of contract** means that only a **party** to a contract can sue on it; a stranger to the contract cannot, even if the contract was made for his benefit.

:::case Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co. v. Selfridge & Co. ([1915] AC 847)
__Held.__ Only a person who is a party to a contract can sue on it; and in English law consideration must move from the promisee. Dunlop, not being a party to the resale agreement between the dealer and Selfridge, could not enforce Selfridge's promise not to sell below list price. (Earlier: *Tweddle v. Atkinson*, (1861) 1 B & S 393.)
:::

### Privity in Pakistan — distinguish two rules

- **Privity of consideration** — not required: consideration may move from a third person (s. 2(d); *Chinnaya*).
- **Privity of contract** — generally followed: a **stranger to the contract** cannot sue (the Privy Council so held in *Jamna Das v. Ram Autar*, (1911) 39 IA 7, and Pakistani courts follow it).

### Exceptions — when a stranger can sue

1. **Trust or charge** — a beneficiary under a trust or a charge created by the contract (e.g., a provision for maintenance of a widow charged on property).
2. **Family settlements** — members for whose benefit a family arrangement is made.
3. **Acknowledgment / estoppel** — where the promisor acknowledges the third party's right (e.g., A pays B money for C; B acknowledges to C that he holds it for C).
4. **Covenants running with land** — a purchaser of land with notice of a covenant is bound by it.
5. **Agency** — the principal can sue on a contract made by his agent.
6. **Assignment** — an assignee of contractual rights (e.g., of a debt) can sue.

## 3.4 Agreements without consideration — s. 25 and its exceptions

> "25. An agreement made without consideration is void, unless— (1) it is expressed **in writing and registered** under the law for the time being in force for the registration of documents, and is made on account of **natural love and affection** between parties standing in a **near relation** to each other; or unless (2) it is a promise to **compensate**, wholly or in part, a person who has already **voluntarily done something** for the promisor, or something which the promisor was legally compellable to do; or unless (3) it is a promise, made **in writing and signed** by the person to be charged therewith, or by his agent generally or specially authorized in that behalf, to pay wholly or in part a **debt** of which the creditor might have enforced payment but for the **law for the limitation** of suits. In any of these cases, such an agreement is a contract.
> Explanation 1.— Nothing in this section shall affect the validity, as between the donor and donee, of any **gift actually made**.
> Explanation 2.— An agreement to which the consent of the promisor is freely given is not void merely because the consideration is **inadequate** …"

![Section 25 — the rule and its exceptions](s25_exceptions.png)

### Exception 1 — natural love and affection (s. 25(1))

Conditions: (a) **in writing**; (b) **registered**; (c) made on account of **natural love and affection**; (d) between parties in **near relation**. *Illustration (b):* A, for natural love and affection, promises to give his son B Rs. 1,000, puts it in writing and registers it — a contract. (Whether "near relation" and "love and affection" exist is a question of fact; a registered promise made in the context of a family quarrel may show no love and affection.)

### Exception 2 — compensation for past voluntary service (s. 25(2))

Conditions: (a) the promisee did something **voluntarily** (not at the promisor's request — otherwise it would be ordinary past consideration); (b) **for the promisor**, or something the promisor was **legally compellable** to do; (c) the promisor was **in existence and capable** of contracting when the act was done; (d) the promise is to compensate **wholly or in part**. *Illustrations (c) and (d):* A finds B's purse and gives it to him; B promises A Rs. 50 — a contract. A supports B's infant son; B promises to pay A's expenses — a contract.

### Exception 3 — time-barred debt (s. 25(3))

Conditions: (a) a **debt** which the creditor could have enforced **but for limitation**; (b) a **promise to pay** it wholly or in part; (c) **in writing** and **signed** by the debtor or his authorised agent. *Illustration (e):* A owes B Rs. 1,000, time-barred; A signs a written promise to pay Rs. 500 — a contract for Rs. 500.

:::trap s. 25(3) vs s. 19 Limitation Act
- **s. 19, Limitation Act:** an **acknowledgment** in writing signed **before** the limitation period expires gives a **fresh period** of limitation for the **existing** debt.
- **s. 25(3), Contract Act:** a **promise** to pay a debt **already time-barred** creates a **new contract**; the suit is on the new promise.
An acknowledgment after expiry does not revive limitation under s. 19 — but if it contains an express **promise to pay** in writing and signed, it may be enforceable under s. 25(3).
:::

### Other exceptions

- **Gift actually made** (Expl. 1) — a completed gift needs no consideration; only a **promise** to give is void.
- **Agency** — "No consideration is necessary to create an agency" (s. 185).
- **Bailment** — gratuitous bailment is valid (ss. 148, 160).
- **Guarantee** — anything done or promise made for the benefit of the principal debtor is sufficient consideration to the surety (s. 127).
- **Remission of performance** — s. 63 allows a promisee to accept less, or any satisfaction, without fresh consideration (see Chapter 10).

### Charitable subscriptions

:::case Kedarnath Bhattacharji v. Gorie Mahomed ((1886) ILR 14 Cal 64)
__Facts.__ The defendant subscribed Rs. 100 for building a town hall. On the strength of such subscriptions, the plaintiff (a commissioner) entered into a contract with a builder.

__Held.__ The promise was **enforceable**: on the faith of it, the plaintiff incurred a **liability** at the promisor's (implied) desire; that was consideration. (Contrast *Abdul Aziz v. Masum Ali*, (1914) ILR 36 All 268: a promise to subscribe to rebuild a mosque was **not** enforceable, because nothing had been done on the faith of it — no consideration.)
:::

:::recap Chapter 3 in seven lines
- Consideration: at the **desire of the promisor**; from the **promisee or any other person**; past, present or future; real; not an existing legal duty; lawful (s. 2(d)).
- *Durga Prasad* (desire of promisor); *Chinnaya* (third-party consideration).
- Inadequacy does not void a contract but bears on free consent (s. 25 Expl. 2).
- Privity of contract: only parties sue (*Dunlop*; *Jamna Das*); exceptions — trust / charge, family settlement, acknowledgment / estoppel, covenant with land, agency, assignment.
- **s. 25 exceptions:** (1) written + registered + natural love and affection + near relation; (2) compensation for past voluntary service; (3) written, signed promise to pay a time-barred debt; plus completed gifts.
- Other no-consideration rules: agency (s. 185), gratuitous bailment, surety (s. 127), remission (s. 63).
- Charitable subscriptions enforceable only if the promisee acted on them (*Kedarnath* vs *Abdul Aziz*).
:::
