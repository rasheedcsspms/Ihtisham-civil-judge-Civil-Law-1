
# Chapter 9 · Performance of contracts (ss. 37 to 61)

## 9.1 The duty to perform — s. 37

> "37. The parties to a contract must either **perform, or offer to perform**, their respective promises, unless such performance is **dispensed with or excused** under the provisions of this Act, or of any other law.
> Promises **bind the representatives** of the promisors in case of the death of such promisors before performance, unless a contrary intention appears from the contract."

*Illustrations:* (a) A promises to deliver goods to B on a certain day for Rs. 1,000 and dies before that day — A's **representatives** must deliver, and B must pay them. (b) A promises to **paint a picture** for B and dies — the contract cannot be enforced either way, because it depended on A's **personal skill**.

:::maxim Pacta sunt servanda — agreements must be kept
The whole law of contract rests on this idea: a promise freely made must be performed. Performance is the normal and natural way a contract ends.
:::

## 9.2 Offer to perform (tender) — s. 38

If the promisor makes a proper **offer of performance** and the promisee refuses it, the promisor is **not responsible** for non-performance and does **not lose his rights** under the contract.

A valid tender must be:

1. **unconditional**;
2. made at a **proper time and place**, so that the promisee has a reasonable opportunity to see that the promisor is **able and willing** there and then to do the **whole** of what he promised;
3. if it is an offer to **deliver goods**, the promisee must have a reasonable opportunity to **inspect** them; and
4. an offer to **one** of several joint promisees is as good as an offer to all.

*Illustration:* A contracts to deliver 100 bales of cotton of a particular quality at B's warehouse on 1 March. A must bring the cotton to the warehouse that day, so that B can satisfy himself as to the quality and quantity.

**Tender of money:** must be of the **exact amount** due, in **legal tender** (unless the creditor accepts a cheque, etc.). A tender of money does **not** discharge the debt — it only stops further interest and costs; the debtor remains liable to pay.

## 9.3 Refusal to perform — s. 39 and anticipatory breach

> "39. When a party to a contract has **refused to perform, or disabled himself from performing**, his promise **in its entirety**, the promisee may **put an end to the contract**, unless he has signified, by words or conduct, his **acquiescence** in its continuance."

*Illustrations:* (a) a singer, engaged for two nights a week for two months, wilfully absents herself on the sixth night — the manager may end the contract; (b) if, with the manager's assent, she sings on the seventh night, he has **acquiesced** and cannot end the contract, but may claim compensation for the sixth night.

### Anticipatory breach

**Anticipatory breach** happens when, **before** the time for performance, a party **refuses** to perform or **disables** himself from performing (e.g., sells to another the specific thing he promised to sell). The innocent party has a choice:

1. **Accept the repudiation** — treat the contract as ended and sue for damages **at once**, without waiting for the date of performance; or
2. **Keep the contract alive** — wait for the due date and hold the other to performance. But then the contract remains alive for **both** sides, and the innocent party takes the **risk** of later events (e.g., frustration) that may excuse the other party.

:::case Hochster v. De la Tour ((1853) 2 E & B 678)
__Facts.__ In April, De la Tour engaged Hochster as a courier for a tour starting on 1 June. In May he wrote that he no longer needed him. Hochster sued **before** 1 June.

__Held.__ Hochster could sue **immediately**; he need not wait until 1 June. A renunciation before the due date is an **anticipatory breach** which the innocent party may accept as ending the contract.
:::

:::case Avery v. Bowden ((1855) 5 E & B 714)
__Facts.__ A charterer told the shipowner's captain at Odessa that he had no cargo for him and he should go away; the captain stayed on, hoping for cargo. Then the **Crimean War** broke out, making loading illegal.

__Held.__ Since the shipowner had **not accepted** the repudiation, the contract continued and was then **frustrated** by the war. The charterer was discharged and not liable.
:::

**Measure of damages** for anticipatory breach: by reference to the market price at the **date fixed for performance** (if the contract is kept alive) or as at the date of acceptance of repudiation, taking into account the **duty to mitigate** (s. 73 Explanation; *illustration (c) to s. 73*: buyer says he will not accept the rice — damages measured at the time when he so informs the seller).

## 9.4 Who must perform — ss. 40 to 45

|40|If the parties intended **personal performance** (skill, taste, confidence — painting, singing, professional services), the **promisor himself** must perform. Otherwise, he or his representatives may employ a **competent person**. *Illus. (a)* payment of money may be made through another; *(b)* painting must be personal.|
|41|If the promisee **accepts performance from a third person**, he cannot afterwards enforce it against the promisor.|

|45|**Joint promisees** — the right to claim performance rests with all jointly during their lives; after one's death, with his representative and the survivors jointly.|

## 9.5 Time and place — ss. 46 to 50

{w:10,90}
|s.|Rule|
|46|No application needed and no time specified — perform within a **reasonable time** (a question of fact in each case).|
|47|Time specified, no application needed — perform at any time during the **usual hours of business** on that day at the proper place. *Illus.*: goods brought to B's warehouse **after closing hours** — not performed.|
|48|Time specified, but the promisor has not undertaken to perform without application — the **promisee must apply** at a proper place and within usual business hours.|
|49|No application needed and no place fixed — the **promisor must apply** to the promisee to appoint a reasonable place, and perform there. *Illus.*: 1,000 maunds of jute.|
|50|Performance may be made in any **manner** or at any **time** the promisee **prescribes or sanctions**. *Illus. (a)* transfer to A's bank account at A's request is good payment even if the bank later fails; *(d)* sending a note by post at the creditor's request — debt discharged on posting.|

**Debtor must seek the creditor.** Where no place of payment is fixed, the general rule (from English law and accepted in the subcontinent) is that the **debtor must find the creditor** and pay him. This also affects **jurisdiction** (s. 20(c), CPC — a part of the cause of action arises where the money is payable).

## 9.6 Reciprocal promises — ss. 51 to 58

**Reciprocal promises** are promises that form the consideration for each other (s. 2(f)). They may be:

1. **Mutual and independent** — each party must perform irrespective of the other (e.g., A to pay on 1 June; B to deliver on 1 July).
2. **Conditional / dependent** — one need not be performed until the other is (s. 52; s. 54).
3. **Mutual and concurrent** — to be performed **simultaneously** (s. 51).

{w:10,90}
|s.|Rule|
|51|**Simultaneous** reciprocal promises — no promisor need perform unless the promisee is **ready and willing** to perform his own. *Illus.*: delivery against payment — neither need act unless the other is ready.|
|52|**Order of performance** — as fixed expressly, or as the **nature of the transaction** requires. *Illus. (a)*: a house must be built before the price is paid; *(b)*: security must be given before stock-in-trade is delivered.|
|53|If one party **prevents** the other from performing, the contract becomes **voidable** at the option of the party prevented, and he gets **compensation**.|
|54|If the promise to be performed **first** is not performed, its promisor **cannot claim** the reciprocal performance and must **compensate** the other. *Illus. (a)*: A hires B's ship but provides no cargo — A must compensate B.|
|55|Failure to perform at the **fixed time** — voidable **if time is of the essence**; otherwise only compensation (see 9.7).|
|56|Impossibility — Chapter 10.|
|57|Legal and illegal reciprocal promises — the legal set is a contract; the illegal set void.|
|58|Alternative promise with one illegal branch — only the legal branch enforceable.|

**Readiness and willingness** is a recurring idea: in a suit for **specific performance**, the plaintiff must aver and prove that he has **always been ready and willing** to perform his part (s. 24(b), Specific Relief Act 1877; Form 47–48, Appendix A, CPC). Pakistani courts insist on this strictly, and in a suit for specific performance of an agreement to sell, the buyer may be directed to **deposit the balance sale price** in court; failure to do so can defeat the suit (*Hamood Mehmood v. Mst. Shabana Ishaque*, 2017 SCMR 2022).

## 9.7 Time as of the essence — s. 55

> "55. When a party to a contract promises to do a certain thing at or before a specified time … and fails to do any such thing at or before the specified time, the contract, or so much of it as has not been performed, becomes **voidable** at the option of the promisee, **if the intention of the parties was that time should be of the essence** of the contract.
> If it was not the intention of the parties that time should be of the essence of the contract, the contract does not become voidable by the failure to do such thing at or before the specified time; but the promisee is entitled to **compensation** …
> If … the promisee **accepts performance** at any time other than that agreed, the promisee cannot claim compensation for any loss occasioned by the non-performance of the promise at the time agreed, **unless**, at the time of such acceptance he gives **notice** to the promisor of his intention to do so."

### When is time of the essence?

1. **Express stipulation** — e.g., "time shall be of the essence" **and** the conduct of the parties is consistent with it.
2. **Nature of the contract** — **commercial** contracts (sale of goods with fluctuating prices, shipping, carriage) — time is usually of the essence for delivery (though not for **payment**: s. 11, Sale of Goods Act).
3. **Surrounding circumstances** — the purpose known to both (e.g., a wedding dress to be delivered before the wedding).

**Sale of immovable property.** The presumption is that time is **not** of the essence, even if a date is fixed, unless the contract or the circumstances clearly show otherwise (*Chand Rani v. Kamal Rani*, (1993) 1 SCC 519; *Gomathinayagam Pillai v. Palaniswami Nadar*, AIR 1967 SC 868). Time can be made essential later by a **notice** fixing a **reasonable** time. Pakistani courts apply the same principle, while looking closely at whether the buyer was genuinely **ready and willing** (9.6).

:::eg Time in a Pakistani property deal
Saima agrees to sell her house in G-11 to Tariq for Rs. 40 million; Rs. 4 million earnest money paid; balance by 31 March; "time is the essence". Tariq has not paid by 31 March and asks for two weeks. Saima keeps silent and accepts the balance on 15 April, without any notice reserving compensation. The contract was voidable on 31 March (s. 55, para 1), but by **accepting late performance** without notice she has **waived** her right to compensation for the delay (s. 55, para 3).
:::

## 9.8 Appropriation of payments — ss. 59 to 61

When a debtor owes **several distinct debts** to one creditor and pays a sum not enough for all, to which debt does it go?

{w:10,32,58}
|s.|Who appropriates|Rule|
|59|**Debtor** — expressly or by circumstances|The payment, **if accepted**, must be applied as the debtor indicated. *Illus. (a)*: Rs. 1,000 paid on the date a Rs. 1,000 note falls due — applied to the note.|
|60|**Creditor** — where the debtor has not indicated|The creditor may apply it **at his discretion** to **any lawful debt actually due**, **even a time-barred one**.|
|61|**Law** — where neither appropriates|Applied to debts **in order of time** (oldest first), whether or not time-barred; debts of equal standing — **proportionately**.|

**Clayton's Case** (*Devaynes v. Noble* (1816) 1 Mer 572): in a **running account** (e.g., a bank current account), the **first payment in** is presumed to discharge the **first item out** — "first in, first out". Section 61 reflects the same idea.

**Note:** appropriation to a time-barred debt does **not** revive limitation for the balance (a creditor can appropriate, but cannot sue for the rest unless s. 19 or s. 25(3) applies). Also, payment towards **interest** before **principal** is the usual commercial rule where both are due, unless otherwise agreed.

:::recap Chapter 9 in eight lines
- Perform or offer to perform; representatives bound unless personal (s. 37); *pacta sunt servanda*.
- Valid tender — unconditional, proper time / place, able and willing, inspection (s. 38).
- Refusal / disablement — promisee may end the contract unless he acquiesced (s. 39); anticipatory breach (*Hochster*); keeping alive carries risk (*Avery v. Bowden*).
- Personal performance (s. 40); joint promisors — joint and several, contribution, release of one (ss. 42–44); joint promisees (s. 45).
- Time and place (ss. 46–50); performance as promisee sanctions (s. 50); debtor must seek creditor.
- Reciprocal promises: simultaneous (s. 51), order (s. 52), prevention (s. 53), first promise default (s. 54); readiness and willingness for specific performance.
- Time of essence → voidable; otherwise compensation; late acceptance without notice → no compensation (s. 55); in land sales time is not essence by presumption.
- Appropriation: debtor → creditor (even barred debt) → law (oldest first, else proportionate) (ss. 59–61; Clayton's Case).
:::

# Chapter 10 · Discharge — agreement, frustration and other modes (ss. 56, 62–67)

## 10.1 The ways a contract ends

![Modes of discharge of a contract](discharge_modes.png)

1. **By performance** — actual performance or a valid tender (ss. 37–38).
2. **By agreement** — novation, rescission, alteration (s. 62); remission, extension, accord and satisfaction (s. 63); waiver; merger.
3. **By impossibility / frustration** (s. 56).
4. **By lapse of time** — the **Limitation Act 1908** bars the **remedy** (the right survives — s. 25(3), s. 60).
5. **By operation of law** — death in personal contracts (s. 37 illus. (b)); **insolvency**; **merger** of a lower right into a higher (e.g., a simple contract debt into a decree or a registered mortgage); **unauthorised material alteration** of a written contract.
6. **By breach** — actual or anticipatory (s. 39) — which entitles the innocent party to treat the contract as discharged.
7. **By the promisee's neglect** to afford reasonable facilities (s. 67).

## 10.2 Discharge by agreement — s. 62

> "62. If the parties to a contract agree to **substitute a new contract** for it, or to **rescind or alter** it, the original contract need not be performed."

- **Novation** — a **new contract** replaces the old, either between the **same parties** on new terms (*illus. (b)*: a mortgage for Rs. 5,000 in place of a debt of Rs. 10,000) or with a **new party** (*illus. (a)*: A, B and C agree that C becomes B's debtor in place of A). Novation needs the **consent of all parties**; *illus. (c)*: B tells A to credit C with Rs. 1,000, but C does not assent — no novation.
- **Rescission** — the parties cancel the contract by mutual agreement (without a new contract taking its place).
- **Alteration** — a change in one or more terms by agreement of all parties.

**Requirements.** The new agreement must be **valid** and made **before** the breach of the old contract (once a breach has occurred, the parties may still settle, but this is then an accord and satisfaction of the claim for damages). If the new contract is **void** or unenforceable, the old one revives (it was never discharged).

## 10.3 Remission and accord and satisfaction — s. 63

> "63. Every promisee may **dispense with or remit**, wholly or in part, the performance of the promise made to him, or may **extend the time** for such performance, or may **accept instead of it any satisfaction** which he thinks fit."

*Illustrations:* (a) B forbids A to paint the picture — A is no longer bound; (b) A owes Rs. 5,000; B accepts **Rs. 2,000** in satisfaction of the whole debt, paid at the time and place the Rs. 5,000 was payable — the **whole debt is discharged**; (c) C pays B Rs. 1,000 in satisfaction of A's debt of Rs. 5,000 — whole claim discharged; (d) an unascertained sum settled by Rs. 2,000 — discharged; (e) a **composition** with creditors of eight annas in the rupee — discharge.

:::trap Section 63 differs from the English rule in Pinnel's Case and Foakes v. Beer
In England, payment of a **smaller sum** cannot discharge a **larger debt** already due, because there is no **consideration** for the creditor's promise to forgo the balance (*Pinnel's Case* (1602); *Foakes v. Beer* (1884) 9 App Cas 605) — unless something new is added (a different place, earlier time, a chattel), or **promissory estoppel** applies (*Central London Property Trust v. High Trees House* [1947] KB 130). Under **s. 63**, **no consideration is needed**: the promisee may simply accept less in full satisfaction. But the acceptance must be **voluntary** — a receipt obtained by **pressure** (e.g., "accept this or get nothing" when the creditor is in distress) may be set aside, and an endorsement "received in full and final settlement" may be challenged if signed under coercion or protest.
:::

**Waiver** — the intentional relinquishment of a known right; it may be express or implied from conduct (e.g., accepting late performance — s. 55). **Promissory estoppel** — a party who has promised not to enforce his strict rights, intending the other to act on it, and the other has acted on it, cannot go back on his promise where it would be inequitable. It is recognised in Pakistan, though it cannot be used against **statute** or to compel a public authority to act **unlawfully**.

## 10.4 Rescission of voidable contracts — ss. 64 and 66

> "64. When a person at whose option a contract is voidable **rescinds** it, the other party thereto need not perform any promise therein contained in which he is promisor. The party rescinding a voidable contract shall, if he have received any **benefit** thereunder from another party to such contract, **restore** such benefit, so far as may be, to the person from whom it was received."

> "66. The rescission of a voidable contract may be **communicated or revoked** in the same manner, and subject to the same rules, as apply to the communication or revocation of a proposal."

**Loss of the right to rescind:** (1) **affirmation** after knowing the facts; (2) **delay** (laches); (3) **third-party rights** acquired in good faith for value before rescission (*Phillips v. Brooks*); (4) where **restitution** is impossible (the parties cannot be restored to their original position). The **Specific Relief Act** (ss. 35–38) gives a court remedy of rescission.

## 10.5 Frustration and impossibility — s. 56

> "56. An agreement to do an act **impossible in itself** is void.
> A contract to do an act which, **after the contract is made**, becomes **impossible**, or, by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, **unlawful**, becomes **void** when the act becomes impossible or unlawful.
> Where one person has promised to do something which he **knew**, or, with reasonable diligence, **might have known**, and which the promisee did not know to be impossible or unlawful, such promisor must make **compensation** to such promisee for any loss which such promisee sustains through the non-performance of the promise."

*Illustrations:* (a) discover treasure by magic — void (initial impossibility); (b) A and B contract to marry; A goes **mad** — void; (c) A, **already married** and forbidden by his personal law to practise polygamy, contracts to marry B — A must **compensate** B; (d) A contracts to take in cargo at a foreign port; A's Government **declares war** on that country — void when war is declared; (e) an actor is too **ill** on several occasions — the contract to act on those occasions becomes void.

### Three paragraphs, three rules

1. **Initial impossibility** (para 1) — impossible **in itself** when made — **void** ab initio.
2. **Supervening impossibility or illegality** (para 2) — **frustration** — the contract **becomes void** when the event happens.
3. **Known impossibility** (para 3) — the promisor who knew (or should have known) must **compensate** the ignorant promisee.

### The doctrine of frustration

:::case Taylor v. Caldwell ((1863) 3 B & S 826)
__Facts.__ A music hall was hired for four concerts. Before the first concert, the hall was **destroyed by fire**, without fault of either party.

__Held.__ Both parties were **excused**. Where performance depends on the continued **existence** of a particular thing, a condition is **implied** that the parties will be excused if the thing perishes without fault.
:::

:::case Krell v. Henry ([1903] 2 KB 740)
__Facts.__ Henry hired Krell's flat in Pall Mall for two days to watch King Edward VII's **coronation procession**. The procession was **cancelled** because the King fell ill.

__Held.__ The contract was **frustrated**. Its **foundation** — the procession — had disappeared, though the flat could still physically be used. (Contrast *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co. v. Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683: hire of a boat to see a **naval review** and cruise round the fleet — **not** frustrated when the review was cancelled, because the cruise round the fleet could still take place; the review was not the sole foundation.)
:::

:::case Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur & Co. (AIR 1954 SC 44)
__Facts.__ A company sold plots of land and undertook to build roads and drains. During the Second World War part of the land was **requisitioned** by the Government. The company claimed the contract was frustrated.

__Held.__
1. In the subcontinent, frustration is governed by **s. 56**, which is a **positive rule of law**; the court need not rely on the English theory of an "implied term".
2. "**Impossible**" in s. 56 is not limited to **physical** or literal impossibility; it includes **impracticability** — where the performance becomes **useless from the point of view of the object** and purpose the parties had in view.
3. On the facts, the requisition was **temporary**, the contract had no time limit for the work, and it did **not** go to the root. **Not frustrated.**
:::

:::case Davis Contractors Ltd. v. Fareham UDC ([1956] AC 696, House of Lords)
__Facts.__ Contractors agreed to build 78 houses in 8 months for a fixed price. Due to labour shortages, the work took 22 months and cost much more.

__Held.__ **Not frustrated.** Frustration occurs only when, without default of either party, the obligation has become incapable of being performed because the circumstances would make it **radically different** from what was undertaken: "It was not this that I promised to do." Mere **hardship**, **inconvenience** or **increased cost** is not enough. (Similarly, *Tsakiroglou & Co. v. Noblee Thorl* [1962] AC 93: closure of the **Suez Canal**, forcing a longer route round the Cape, did **not** frustrate a sale of groundnuts.)
:::

### Grounds of frustration

1. **Destruction** of the subject-matter (*Taylor v. Caldwell*).
2. **Death or incapacity** in contracts of **personal service** (illus. (b), (e)).
3. **Change of law** or **government action** making performance unlawful or impossible — requisition, prohibition of export or import, ban on a business.
4. **Outbreak of war** (illus. (d)).
5. **Non-occurrence of an event** which was the **foundation** of the contract (*Krell v. Henry*).
6. **Radical change of circumstances** that makes performance **useless** for the agreed object (*Satyabrata Ghose*).

### Not frustration

1. **Difficulty**, **hardship** or **increased cost** of performance (*Davis Contractors*; *Tsakiroglou*).
2. **Commercial impossibility** — the deal has become unprofitable.
3. **Strikes, lockouts, civil disturbances** that only delay performance (unless they go to the root).
4. **Failure of one object** where there are several objects (*Herne Bay*).
5. **Self-induced frustration** — where the impossibility results from the party's **own act or choice** (*Maritime National Fish Ltd. v. Ocean Trawlers Ltd.* [1935] AC 524 — the company chose to allocate its limited fishing licences to other boats).
6. Events which the parties **foresaw** and **provided for** in the contract (e.g., a **force majeure** clause) — such cases are governed by the **contract** (and s. 32 as a contingent contract), not s. 56.

### Effect of frustration

1. The contract becomes **void** from the time of the frustrating event — both parties are **discharged** from further performance (s. 56, para 2).
2. Any party who has received an **advantage** must **restore** it or make compensation (s. 65; *illus. (d) to s. 65*: a singer too ill to sing must refund the advance, but need not compensate for lost profits).
3. Para 3 compensation where the promisor knew of the impossibility.

:::eg COVID-19 and lockdowns
A wedding hall in Islamabad booked for 15 April 2020 could not hold the event because of the government's lockdown order banning gatherings. Performance became **unlawful** by an event neither party could prevent — the contract became void (s. 56, para 2); the hall must refund the advance (s. 65), less any agreed and reasonable costs if the contract so provided. If the contract had a **force majeure** clause dealing with "epidemic or government order", that clause would govern (the contract would be applied under s. 32 as a contingent contract).
:::

## 10.6 Restitution after a void agreement — s. 65

> "65. When an agreement is **discovered to be void**, or when a contract **becomes void**, any person who has received any **advantage** under such agreement or contract is bound to **restore** it, or to make **compensation** for it, to the person from whom he received it."

*Illustrations:* (a) A pays B Rs. 1,000 for B's promise to marry C, A's daughter; C is **dead** at the time — the agreement is void; B must **repay** Rs. 1,000; (b) A was to deliver 250 maunds of rice by 1 May but delivered 130; B **retains** them — B must pay for 130; (c) a singer who sang five nights before the manager rescinded — the manager must pay for those five nights; (d) a singer too ill — must refund the advance.

**When s. 65 does not apply:**

1. To an agreement **void because of incapacity** of a party (*Mohori Bibee* — minor).
2. Where the parties **knew from the start** that the agreement was **illegal** — the *in pari delicto* rule prevails (the agreement was not "discovered" to be void).

## 10.7 Promisee's neglect to give facilities — s. 67

> "67. If any promisee **neglects or refuses to afford** the promisor **reasonable facilities** for the performance of his promise, the promisor is **excused** by such neglect or refusal as to any non-performance caused thereby."

*Illustration:* A contracts to repair B's house; B refuses to point out the places needing repair — A is excused.

- Discharge by performance, agreement, impossibility, lapse of time, operation of law, breach, promisee's neglect.

- ss. 64, 66: rescinding party restores benefits; rescission communicated like a proposal; right lost by affirmation, delay, third-party rights, impossibility of restitution.
- s. 56: initial impossibility void; supervening impossibility / illegality — frustration (*Taylor v. Caldwell*; *Krell v. Henry*; *Satyabrata Ghose* — impracticability, s. 56 exhaustive); known impossibility — compensation.
- Not frustration: hardship, cost (*Davis Contractors*; *Tsakiroglou*), self-induced (*Maritime National Fish*), foreseen events (force majeure clause, s. 32).
- s. 65: restore advantages when an agreement is discovered void or a contract becomes void — not for minors or knowingly illegal agreements.
- s. 67: promisee's refusal of facilities excuses the promisor.
:::
