
# Answer 1 · "All agreements are contracts" — essentials, competence and writing (LHC 2022 · SHC 2022)

:::glance LAHORE HIGH COURT 2022 · CIVIL LAW-I · Q. 2 — with SINDH HIGH COURT 2022 · Q. 2 · 20 MARKS
LHC: "All agreements are contracts". Discuss all aspects and their essentials as defined in the Contract Act (IX of 1872).
SHC: What agreements are contracts? Who are persons competent to contract? Are all contracts required to be in writing?
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](c1_map.png)

## Decoding the question

The quotation is **incomplete on purpose**. Section 10 does not say that all agreements are contracts; it says all agreements are contracts **if** five conditions are met. The examiner wants you to (i) **correct** the statement, (ii) build the **definitions** of s. 2 step by step, (iii) explain **each essential** with a section and a case, (iv) deal with **competence** (s. 11) and (v) answer the **writing** question from the second paragraph of s. 10. A strong answer ends with the maxim "every contract is an agreement, but every agreement is not a contract".

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — the statement is only half true

The proposition "all agreements are contracts" is **not** correct as it stands. Section 10 of the Contract Act 1872 provides:

> "All agreements are contracts **if** they are made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration and with a lawful object, and are not hereby expressly declared to be void."

So an agreement becomes a contract only when it satisfies the conditions of s. 10. The correct statement is: **"All contracts are agreements, but all agreements are not contracts."**

### 2. The building blocks — section 2

1. **Proposal** — "when one person signifies to another his willingness to do or to abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the assent of that other" (s. 2(a)).
2. **Promise** — "a proposal, when accepted, becomes a promise" (s. 2(b)).
3. **Consideration** — what the promisee or any other person does, abstains from or promises at the **desire of the promisor** (s. 2(d)).
4. **Agreement** — "every promise and every set of promises, forming the consideration for each other" (s. 2(e)).
5. **Contract** — "an agreement **enforceable by law**" (s. 2(h)).

Hence: **proposal + acceptance = promise; promise + consideration = agreement; agreement + enforceability = contract.**

### 3. Essential 1 — offer and acceptance (agreement)

There must be a lawful offer and an **absolute and unqualified** acceptance, communicated in a usual and reasonable manner (ss. 3–9). A counter-offer is not acceptance (*Hyde v. Wrench*, (1840) 3 Beav 334); a general offer is accepted by performing its conditions (s. 8; *Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.*, [1893] 1 QB 256).

### 4. Essential 2 — intention to create legal relations

The phrase "**enforceable by law**" implies that the parties must intend legal consequences. In social and domestic arrangements the presumption is against such an intention (*Balfour v. Balfour*, [1919] 2 KB 571 — a husband's promise of a monthly allowance to his wife was not enforceable); in commercial dealings it is presumed.

### 5. Essential 3 — free consent (ss. 13–22)

Consent means agreeing "upon the same thing in the same sense" (s. 13). It is **free** when not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake (s. 14). Consent caused by the first four makes the contract **voidable** (ss. 19, 19A); a bilateral mistake of essential fact makes the agreement **void** (s. 20).

### 6. Essential 4 — competent parties (ss. 11–12)

#### i) Who is competent

"Every person is competent to contract who is of the **age of majority** according to the law to which he is subject, and who is of **sound mind**, and is **not disqualified** from contracting by any law to which he is subject" (s. 11).

#### ii) Minors

Majority is attained at **18 years** (21 where a court has appointed a guardian) — Majority Act 1875, s. 3. A minor's agreement is **void ab initio** (*Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose*, (1903) ILR 30 Cal 539, PC).

#### iii) Unsound mind

A person must be "capable of understanding" the contract and "of forming a rational judgment as to its effect upon his interests" **at the time** (s. 12). A lunatic may contract in a **lucid interval**; a sane man **delirious or drunk** cannot (illustrations).

#### iv) Disqualified persons

Alien enemies (s. 83 CPC), foreign States (s. 86 CPC), insolvents as to their vested property, and bodies acting **ultra vires**.

### 7. Essential 5 — lawful consideration

Consideration must be given at the **desire of the promisor** (*Durga Prasad v. Baldeo*, (1880) ILR 3 All 221); it may move from the promisee **or any other person** (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*, (1882) ILR 4 Mad 137); it need not be **adequate** (s. 25 Expl. 2). An agreement without consideration is **void** (s. 25) except in the three cases listed there.

### 8. Essential 6 — lawful object (ss. 23–24)

The consideration and object are unlawful if **forbidden by law**, would **defeat any law**, are **fraudulent**, involve **injury** to person or property, or are **immoral** or **opposed to public policy** (s. 23). *Illustration (f)*: paying for a public-service job — void.

### 9. Essential 7 — not expressly declared void

The Act declares certain agreements void: partly unlawful consideration (s. 24); no consideration (s. 25); restraint of marriage (s. 26), trade (s. 27) or legal proceedings (s. 28); uncertainty (s. 29); **wagers** (s. 30) and, in Pakistan, agreements **collateral** to wagers (s. 30A); agreements contingent on impossible events (s. 36); impossible acts (s. 56).

### 10. Other essentials

1. **Certainty** — "agreements, the meaning of which is not certain, or capable of being made certain, are void" (s. 29).
2. **Possibility of performance** — an agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void (s. 56; illustration (a): discovering treasure by magic).

### 11. Must contracts be in writing?

#### i) General rule — no

A contract may be **oral**, **written**, or **implied by conduct**: "In so far as the proposal or acceptance … is made in words, the promise is said to be express. In so far as such proposal or acceptance is made otherwise than in words, the promise is said to be implied" (s. 9). An oral contract is fully valid; the only difficulty is **proof**.

#### ii) Exceptions — where another law requires form

The second paragraph of s. 10 saves "any law in force in Pakistan … by which any contract is required to be made **in writing** or in the presence of **witnesses**, or any law relating to the **registration** of documents". Examples:

1. **s. 25(1)** — a promise out of natural love and affection must be **written and registered**; **s. 25(3)** — a promise to pay a time-barred debt must be **written and signed**.
2. **Transfer of Property Act 1882** — sale of immovable property worth Rs. 100 or more, mortgage, lease from year to year — **registered instrument** (ss. 54, 59, 107); **Registration Act 1908, s. 17**.
3. **Negotiable Instruments Act 1881** — promissory notes, bills and cheques are written instruments.
4. **Arbitration Act 1940** — an arbitration agreement must be **written** (s. 2(a)).
5. **Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984, Art. 17(2)(a)** — instruments relating to **financial or future obligations** "shall be attested by two men, or one man and two women".

### 12. Illustration

Rehan orally agrees to buy 500 bags of cement from Usman at Rs. 1,300 per bag, delivery in a week. Both are adults of sound mind; the price is consideration; the object is lawful; nothing declares it void. It is a **valid contract** though oral. But if Rehan agreed to buy Usman's plot in Islamabad, the **sale itself** would need a registered deed (s. 54 TPA), and a written agreement to sell would need attestation under Art. 17 QSO to be proved as a document.

### 13. Conclusion

An agreement is a contract only when it has the elements of s. 10 — **free consent, competent parties, lawful consideration, lawful object, and no express declaration of voidness** — together with certainty, possibility and intention to create legal relations. Writing is not generally required, but s. 10 itself preserves laws that demand writing, attestation or registration. Hence the true rule is: **every contract is an agreement, but every agreement is not a contract.**

## Why this answer scores

- It **corrects the quotation** in the first line, showing the examiner that you saw the trap.
- The **s. 2 ladder** shows the structure of the Act; each essential gets a **section and a case**.
- It answers all three SHC sub-questions (what agreements are contracts, competence, writing) under clear headings.
- The **Pakistani form rules** — Art. 17 QSO, TPA, Registration Act — lift the answer above textbook level.

# Answer 2 · Offer, acceptance, invitation to treat, revocation — are bids offers? (LHC 2023)

:::glance LAHORE HIGH COURT 2023 · CIVIL LAW-I · Q. 4 · 20 MARKS
Explain the expressions 'Offer', 'Acceptance', 'Invitation to treat', and 'Revocation of Proposal(s) and Acceptance(s)'. Whether the bid(s) invited from potential bidders through advertisement constitute an offer or simply an invitation to make an offer. Elucidate with illustrations.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](c2_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Four definitions, then **one application**: bids invited by advertisement. The examiner wants (i) s. 2(a) offer and its kinds, (ii) acceptance and its rules (ss. 2(b), 7, 8), (iii) invitation to treat with the leading cases, (iv) **revocation** under ss. 4, 5 and 6, with the s. 5 illustration, and (v) the clear answer: an advertisement inviting bids is an **invitation to treat**; each **bid is an offer**; the **letter of acceptance** concludes the contract. Pakistani procurement rules add depth.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction

A contract begins with a **proposal** and its **acceptance**. Not every statement that looks like an offer is one: many are only **invitations to treat**. The distinction decides **when** the contract is made and **who** is bound.

### 2. Offer (proposal) — s. 2(a)

#### i) Definition

"When one person **signifies** to another his **willingness** to do or to abstain from doing anything, **with a view to obtaining the assent** of that other to such act or abstinence, he is said to make a proposal" (s. 2(a)).

#### ii) Essentials

1. It must be **communicated** — "the communication of a proposal is complete when it comes to the knowledge of the person to whom it is made" (s. 4). A person who acts in ignorance of the offer cannot accept it (*Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt*, (1913) 11 ALJ 489).
2. Its terms must be **certain** (s. 29).
3. It must show an intention to be **bound** upon acceptance.
4. It may be **express or implied** (s. 9), **specific** or **general**.

#### iii) General offer

An offer to the world is accepted by anyone who **performs its conditions** (s. 8). In *Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.* ([1893] 1 QB 256) the advertisement promising £100, backed by a bank deposit, was a general offer accepted by Mrs Carlill's use of the smoke ball.

### 3. Acceptance — ss. 2(b), 7, 8

"When the person to whom the proposal is made **signifies his assent** thereto, the proposal is said to be accepted" (s. 2(b)). To convert a proposal into a promise the acceptance must:

1. be **absolute and unqualified** (s. 7(1)) — a counter-offer **rejects** the original offer (*Hyde v. Wrench*, (1840) 3 Beav 334);
2. be expressed in a **usual and reasonable manner**, or the manner prescribed (s. 7(2));
3. be **communicated** — silence is not acceptance (*Felthouse v. Bindley*, (1862) 11 CBNS 869);
4. be made **while the offer subsists**;
5. may be by **conduct** — performance of the conditions (s. 8).

### 4. Invitation to treat

#### i) Meaning

An invitation to treat is a statement inviting others to **make offers**; it is not itself an offer, and a reply to it is not an acceptance.

#### ii) Examples and cases

1. **Display of goods** with prices — *Pharmaceutical Society v. Boots Cash Chemists* ([1953] 1 QB 401): the customer offers at the cash desk; the shop accepts.
2. **Reply stating a price** — *Harvey v. Facey* ([1893] AC 552, PC): "Lowest price £900" was information, not an offer.
3. **Auctions** — the bid is the offer; the fall of the hammer, acceptance.
4. **Catalogues, price lists, prospectuses.**
5. **Tender notices** — *Spencer v. Harding* ((1870) LR 5 CP 561): a circular inviting tenders was not an offer to sell to the highest bidder.

#### iii) Rationale

If displays and advertisements were offers, a seller with limited stock would be bound to everyone who "accepted", and could not choose with whom to deal.

### 5. When communication is complete — s. 4

{w:24,38,38}
|Communication|Complete as against the sender|Complete as against the receiver|
|Proposal|—|When it comes to the knowledge of the offeree|
|Acceptance|Proposer bound — when put in course of transmission|Acceptor bound — when it comes to the proposer's knowledge|
|Revocation|When put in course of transmission|When it comes to the knowledge of the other|

For **instantaneous** media (telephone, e-mail), the contract is complete where and when the acceptance is **received** (*Entores v. Miles Far East*, [1955] 2 QB 327; *Bhagwandas Kedia v. Girdharilal*, AIR 1966 SC 543).

### 6. Revocation of proposal and acceptance — s. 5

"A proposal may be revoked at any time **before the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer**, but not afterwards. An acceptance may be revoked at any time **before the communication of the acceptance is complete as against the acceptor**, but not afterwards" (s. 5).

*Illustration to s. 5:* A proposes by post to sell his house to B; B posts his acceptance. A may revoke **before or at the moment** B posts the acceptance. B may revoke his acceptance **before or at the moment** his letter reaches A.

### 7. Modes of revocation of a proposal — s. 6

A proposal is revoked —

1. by **notice** of revocation communicated by the proposer;
2. by **lapse** of the time prescribed, or of a reasonable time;
3. by the acceptor's **failure to fulfil a condition precedent** to acceptance;
4. by the **death or insanity** of the proposer, if known to the acceptor before acceptance.

Also by **rejection** or **counter-offer**. A promise to keep an offer open is not binding without consideration.

### 8. Are bids invited through an advertisement offers?

#### i) The answer — an invitation to treat

An advertisement inviting bids is **not** an offer. It is an **invitation to treat** — an invitation to make offers. Each **bid** or **tender** submitted is an **offer**. The inviting authority is free to accept any bid or reject all of them. A contract arises only when the authority **communicates acceptance** of a bid (*Spencer v. Harding*).

#### ii) Exception — a promise to accept the highest or lowest bid

If the advertisement **promises** to award the contract to the highest (or lowest) bidder, that promise may itself be an **offer**, accepted by submitting the qualifying bid (*Harvela Investments v. Royal Trust Co. of Canada*, [1986] AC 207 — a promise to accept the highest sealed bid bound the vendor).

#### iii) Auctions

An advertisement of an auction is not a promise to hold it (*Harris v. Nickerson*, (1873) LR 8 QB 286). At the auction each bid is an offer, which the bidder may **withdraw before the hammer falls**.

#### iv) Revocation of a bid

As a bid is an offer, the bidder may **withdraw** it before acceptance is communicated (s. 5). But the tender documents usually require a **bid security**, which may be **forfeited** under the tender conditions if the bid is withdrawn during its validity period.

#### v) Public procurement in Pakistan

Public bodies such as the CDA must follow the **Public Procurement Regulatory Authority Ordinance 2002** and the **Public Procurement Rules 2004**. These require open advertisement, evaluation on stated criteria, and **reasons** for rejecting all bids, and they allow rejection of all bids **before acceptance**. A contract is concluded when the **acceptance** of the successful bid is communicated, usually by a **letter of acceptance**, followed by a formal agreement.

### 9. Illustration

The CDA advertises: "Sealed bids are invited for constructing a community centre in I-10." Three contractors bid; Ahmad Builders quotes the lowest. Before the CDA decides, Ahmad Builders withdraws its bid. There is no contract: the advertisement was an invitation; the bid was an offer revocable before acceptance (s. 5). The CDA may forfeit the **bid security** under the tender conditions, but it cannot sue for breach of a contract to build.

### 10. Conclusion

An **offer** is a willingness to be bound on acceptance; an **acceptance** must be absolute and communicated; an **invitation to treat** only invites offers. A proposal may be revoked until the acceptance is posted, and an acceptance until it reaches the proposer (ss. 4–6). Bids invited by advertisement are therefore **invitations to make offers**: each bid is an offer, and the contract is made only when the authority communicates acceptance.

## Why this answer scores

- Each of the four expressions gets a **definition from the Act** plus a **leading case**.
- The **s. 4 table** and the **s. 5 illustration** show precise knowledge of timing.
- The bid question is answered **directly**, with the exception (*Harvela*) and the auction rule.
- The **Pakistani procurement framework** makes the answer practical.

# Answer 3 · Farooq and Javaid — negotiations for the sale of a car by e-mail (LHC 2017)

:::glance LAHORE HIGH COURT 2017 · CIVIL LAW-I · Q. 2 · 20 MARKS
Knowing that Javaid wanted to sell his car so that he could buy a new one, Farooq sent him an e-mail message saying, "I will give you Rs. 1,500,000/- for the car." Javaid replied, "Interesting suggestion. Would you go to Rs. 1,800,000/-?" In response, Farooq sent a further message stating, "Yes. Consider it sold at Rs. 1,800,000/-. No need to reply. I will collect it later in the week." However, Farooq later learned from a mutual friend that Javaid had then accepted a higher offer for the car from someone else. Consequently, he was very surprised when it turned out that Javaid's other deal had fallen through and that Javaid was now expecting Farooq to buy the car. Farooq angrily refused to do so.
Discuss the rights, duties, and remedies, if any, of Farooq and Javaid arising out of negotiations for the sale of Javaid's car.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](c3_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Read every message and **label** it: offer, counter-offer or inquiry, acceptance. Then decide **when** the contract (if any) was formed, given that the messages are **e-mails**. Next, analyse Javaid's sale to someone else: was it a **breach** (anticipatory repudiation), and did Farooq **accept** it? Finally, decide whether Javaid can still hold Farooq to the bargain, and state the **remedies** of each side. The examiner rewards a **reasoned conclusion** where the facts are open — e.g., whether "Interesting suggestion. Would you go to…?" is a counter-offer or a mere inquiry.

## The model answer

### 1. Issues

1. Was Farooq's first e-mail an **offer**?
2. Was Javaid's reply a **counter-offer**, an **inquiry**, or an **offer** in its own right?
3. Was Farooq's "Yes. Consider it sold…" an **acceptance** — and when was the contract formed by e-mail?
4. Did Javaid's sale to another buyer amount to **anticipatory breach**, and what was its effect?
5. What are the **rights and remedies** of each party now?

### 2. The law in brief

1. **Proposal** — s. 2(a); **acceptance** must be **absolute and unqualified** (s. 7(1)); a **counter-offer** rejects the offer (*Hyde v. Wrench*).
2. **Communication** — s. 4; for instantaneous media the acceptance is complete when **received** (*Entores v. Miles Far East*; *Bhagwandas Kedia*). E-mails are recognised as valid communications by the **Electronic Transactions Ordinance 2002**.
3. **Revocation** — ss. 5 and 6.
4. **Refusal to perform** — s. 39: "When a party to a contract has refused to perform, or disabled himself from performing, his promise in its entirety, the promisee may put an end to the contract, unless he has signified, by words or conduct, his acquiescence in its continuance."
5. **Damages** — ss. 73, 75; Sale of Goods Act ss. 55–57.

### 3. Farooq's first e-mail — an offer

"I will give you Rs. 1,500,000 for the car" is a definite statement of **willingness** to buy a specific car at a fixed price, made **with a view to obtaining Javaid's assent** (s. 2(a)). It is an **offer**, communicated when Javaid read it (s. 4).

### 4. Javaid's reply — counter-offer or inquiry?

#### i) Counter-offer view

"Would you go to Rs. 1,800,000?" does not accept Rs. 1,500,000. It proposes **different terms** (a higher price). It is a **counter-offer** that **rejects and destroys** Farooq's original offer (*Hyde v. Wrench* — an offer of £950 for a farm offered at £1,000 killed the original offer).

#### ii) Inquiry view

The words "Interesting suggestion" and the question form might suggest a **request for information** (*Stevenson v. McLean* (1880) 5 QBD 346 — an inquiry whether credit terms were acceptable did not reject the offer).

#### iii) The better view

Javaid named a **specific price** for the **specific car** and invited Farooq to agree to it. Read objectively, it was a **proposal to sell at Rs. 1,800,000** — a **counter-offer** capable of acceptance. Farooq's original Rs. 1,500,000 offer lapsed (s. 6; rejection).

### 5. Farooq's second e-mail — acceptance

"Yes. Consider it sold at Rs. 1,800,000. No need to reply. I will collect it later in the week" is an **absolute and unqualified** assent to Javaid's proposal (s. 7(1)). "No need to reply" dispenses with any further communication **from Javaid**; it does not make **silence** the acceptance (the acceptance is Farooq's own message — *Felthouse v. Bindley* does not apply). The words "I will collect it later in the week" relate to **performance**, not to a new term.

### 6. When and where was the contract formed?

E-mail is generally treated as **instantaneous** communication: the acceptance is complete when it **reaches** the offeror (Javaid) and is capable of being read (*Entores*; *Bhagwandas Kedia*). Even under the **postal rule** of s. 4, Javaid (the proposer) would be bound when Farooq's e-mail was **sent** out of his power. On either view, a **binding contract of sale** for Rs. 1,800,000 was concluded **before** Javaid accepted the other buyer's offer. As a specific car was sold unconditionally in a deliverable state, **property** probably passed to Farooq at once (Sale of Goods Act s. 20), though payment and delivery were to follow.

### 7. Did Javaid revoke his proposal?

A proposal may be revoked only **before** acceptance is complete as against the proposer (s. 5). Javaid never sent a revocation; his acceptance of a higher offer from a third party, made **after** Farooq's acceptance, is **not a revocation** at all. (Even before acceptance, a revocation must be **communicated** — s. 6(1); news from a mutual friend is at most an indirect communication — *Dickinson v. Dodds* (1876) 2 Ch D 463.)

### 8. Javaid's sale to another — anticipatory breach

By agreeing to sell the same car to someone else, Javaid **disabled himself** from performing his promise to Farooq (s. 39). This is an **anticipatory breach** (*Hochster v. De la Tour*, (1853) 2 E & B 678). Farooq then had two options:

1. **Accept the repudiation** — treat the contract as at an end (s. 39) and claim compensation; or
2. **Affirm** the contract — keep it alive and demand performance when due.

### 9. Did Farooq accept the repudiation?

When Farooq learnt of the other sale, he was "very surprised" when the other deal later fell through, and he **refused** to buy. The facts suggest that Farooq, on learning of the repudiation, **treated the contract as ended** — he made other plans and did not affirm. Acceptance of a repudiation need not be formal; it may be by **conduct** that clearly shows the innocent party treats the contract as over. If so, the contract **came to an end** when Farooq accepted the repudiation, and Javaid **cannot revive it** by offering performance later. (Had Farooq **affirmed** the contract — e.g., by telling Javaid that he still expected the car — the contract would have remained alive for **both** sides, and Farooq would have been bound to pay: *Avery v. Bowden*, (1855) 5 E & B 714.)

### 10. Rights and remedies

#### i) Javaid

Javaid has **no right** to compel Farooq to buy. He was the party in breach; Farooq rightly rescinded (s. 39), and "a person who rightly rescinds a contract is entitled to compensation" (s. 75), not liable to perform.

#### ii) Farooq

1. Farooq is **discharged** from his duty to pay Rs. 1,800,000.
2. He may claim **compensation** for any loss that "naturally arose in the usual course of things" (s. 73) — e.g., the **difference** between Rs. 1,800,000 and the higher price of a similar car in the market when the breach occurred (SGA s. 57 — damages for non-delivery; s. 73 illus. (a)). If a similar car was available at the same price, damages are **nominal**.
3. He must **mitigate** his loss (Explanation to s. 73).
4. **Specific performance** is not ordinarily granted for an ordinary car, as damages are adequate (SRA s. 12, Explanation), and in any case Farooq no longer wants it.

### 11. Conclusion

A contract for the sale of the car at **Rs. 1,800,000** was formed when Farooq's e-mail accepting Javaid's counter-offer reached Javaid. Javaid's later sale to another buyer was an **anticipatory breach** under s. 39, which Farooq **accepted** by treating the contract as ended. Javaid therefore **cannot compel** Farooq to buy the car now. Farooq is discharged and may claim **compensation** under ss. 73 and 75 for any loss proved, subject to mitigation.

## Why this answer scores

- Each message is **labelled** with its legal character, and the doubtful one (counter-offer vs inquiry) is argued **both ways** before a conclusion.
- The **e-mail** timing is handled with s. 4, *Entores* and the ETO 2002.
- The decisive point — **acceptance of repudiation** vs **affirmation** (*Hochster*; *Avery v. Bowden*) — is identified, which is what the last facts were planted for.
- The answer ends with **remedies for both parties**, as the question asks.
