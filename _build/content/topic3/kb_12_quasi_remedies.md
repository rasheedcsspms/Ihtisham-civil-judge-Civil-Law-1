
# Chapter 11 · Quasi-contracts — relations resembling contract (ss. 68 to 72)

## 11.1 The idea

Chapter V of the Act is titled "**Of certain relations resembling those created by contract**". These are obligations which the **law imposes** — without any agreement — to prevent a person from being **unjustly enriched** at another's expense. They are called **quasi-contracts** (or restitution).

:::maxim Nemo debet locupletari ex aliena jactura — no one should be enriched at another's loss
"…the gist of the action is that the defendant, upon the circumstances of the case, is obliged by the ties of natural justice and equity to refund the money" — Lord Mansfield in *Moses v. Macferlan* (1760) 2 Burr 1005. This is the root of quasi-contract.
:::

**Features:**

1. There is **no agreement**; the obligation is imposed by **law**.
2. The right is usually to **money** (or restoration of a thing).
3. The right is **in personam** — against a particular person.
4. The person liable is treated **as if** he had contracted: compensation for failure to discharge such an obligation is the **same as for breach of contract** (s. 73, para 3).

## 11.2 The five cases

![Quasi-contracts — sections 68 to 72](quasi_contracts.png)

### Case 1 — Necessaries supplied to an incapable person (s. 68)

Covered in Chapter 4: reimbursement **from the property** of a minor or person of unsound mind (or of their dependants), for **necessaries suited to their condition in life**.

### Case 2 — Payment by an interested person (s. 69)

> "69. A person who is **interested in the payment** of money which **another is bound by law to pay**, and who therefore pays it, is entitled to be **reimbursed** by the other."

*Illustration:* B holds land in **Sind** on a lease from A, the zamindar. A's land revenue is in arrears and the land is advertised for sale, which would **annul B's lease**. B pays the revenue to save his lease. A must make good the amount to B.

**Ingredients:** (1) the payer is **interested** in the payment (to protect his own interest — not a mere volunteer); (2) the other person is **bound by law** to pay; (3) the payer is **not** himself bound to pay; (4) he pays to protect his interest.

### Case 3 — Benefit of a non-gratuitous act (s. 70)

> "70. Where a person **lawfully** does anything for another person, or delivers anything to him, **not intending to do so gratuitously**, and such other person **enjoys the benefit** thereof, the latter is bound to make compensation to the former in respect of, or to restore, the thing so done or delivered."

*Illustrations:* (a) a tradesman leaves goods at B's house **by mistake**; B treats them as his own — he must pay; (b) A saves B's property from fire — no compensation if A intended to act **gratuitously**.

**Ingredients:**

1. The act is done **lawfully**;
2. it is done **not intending to act gratuitously**;
3. the other person **enjoys the benefit** — he had the **option** to accept or reject, and accepted it.

:::case State of West Bengal v. B.K. Mondal & Sons (AIR 1962 SC 779)
__Facts.__ A contractor built a kitchen, sheds and other structures for the Government at the request of an officer. The contract did not comply with s. 175(3) of the Government of India Act 1935 (contracts with Government must be in a prescribed form), so it was **not a valid contract**. The Government used the structures but refused to pay.

__Held.__ The Government was liable under **s. 70**. The work was done **lawfully**, **not gratuitously**, and the Government **accepted and enjoyed** the benefit. Section 70 does not enforce the void contract; it imposes a separate obligation to pay for the benefit received.
:::

This principle is often used in Pakistan for **public works**: when a contractor does additional work at the request of officials, and the department uses it, s. 70 allows a claim for **reasonable value** (**quantum meruit**), even if the formal contract or approval was lacking — subject to the requirement that the act was **lawful**.

### Case 4 — Finder of goods (s. 71)

> "71. A person who **finds goods** belonging to another and takes them into his custody, is subject to the **same responsibility as a bailee**."

So the finder must take **reasonable care** (s. 151), must not use them for himself, and must **return** them to the owner when found. In turn, he has rights: to **retain** the goods until compensated for his trouble and expenses (s. 168), to sue for a **reward** offered (s. 168), and in some cases to **sell** them (s. 169 — perishable goods, or lawful charges reaching two-thirds of value). (Bailment is in Topic 4.)

### Case 5 — Money paid or thing delivered by mistake or under coercion (s. 72)

> "72. A person to whom money has been paid or anything delivered **by mistake or under coercion**, must **repay or return** it."

*Illustrations:* (a) A and B jointly owe C Rs. 100; A pays; B, unaware, pays again — C must repay B; (b) a railway refuses to deliver goods except on payment of an **illegal charge**; the consignee pays to get his goods — he can recover the excess.

**"Mistake" includes mistake of law.**

:::case Sales Tax Officer v. Kanhaiya Lal Mukundlal Saraf (AIR 1959 SC 135)
__Facts.__ A firm paid sales tax on forward contracts, believing it was due. Later the levy was held invalid. The firm claimed a refund.

__Held.__ The word "**mistake**" in s. 72 is used without qualification and includes a **mistake of law** as well as fact. The tax was refundable. (Section 21 — no relief for mistake of Pakistani law — applies to the **validity of contracts**, not to recovery of money under s. 72.)
:::

**"Coercion"** in s. 72 has been read in a **wider**, general sense — not only the technical meaning in s. 15 — to cover payments made under **pressure** of an illegal demand (*illus. (b)*), e.g., an unlawful tax or fee paid **under protest** to avoid penalties.

**Limits:** a refund may be refused where the payer has **passed on** the burden to others (the doctrine of **unjust enrichment** applies both ways); and for relief on the ground of mistake, limitation is three years from when the mistake becomes known to the plaintiff (Art. 96, Limitation Act).

## 11.3 Quantum meruit — "as much as he has earned"

A claim for **reasonable remuneration** for work done, where there is no enforceable claim under the contract for the agreed price. It arises:

1. where a contract is **discovered to be void** or **becomes void** (ss. 65, 70);
2. where one party **prevents** the other from completing (s. 53) — the other may claim for work done;
3. where work is done and accepted **without a fixed price** (s. 70);
4. where a **divisible** contract is partly performed and the benefit accepted (s. 65, illus. (b), (c)).

:::case Planché v. Colburn ((1831) 8 Bing 14)
__Facts.__ The plaintiff agreed to write a book for a series "The Juvenile Library" for £100. After he had done much of the work, the publishers **abandoned** the series.

__Held.__ He could recover **£50** on a **quantum meruit** for the work done, as the defendants had prevented him from completing.
:::

**Not available** to a party who **himself** is in breach of an **entire** contract (a "lump sum" contract), unless the other party has **accepted** the partial performance voluntarily (*Sumpter v. Hedges* [1898] 1 QB 673 — builder abandoned work; the owner completed it himself; no quantum meruit for the builder's work, since the owner had no real choice about "accepting" it).

:::case Fibrosa Spolka Akcyjna v. Fairbairn Lawson Combe Barbour Ltd. ([1943] AC 32, House of Lords)
__Facts.__ A Polish company paid £1,000 in advance for machinery. The contract was frustrated by the German occupation of Gdynia in 1939.

__Held.__ Because there was a **total failure of consideration**, the buyer could recover the £1,000 paid in advance. (In Pakistan, **s. 65** gives this result directly.)
:::

|Point|Contract|Quasi-contract|
|Basis|Agreement of parties|Imposed by law (equity / unjust enrichment)|
|Consent|Essential|Not needed|
|Right|Usually in personam, flows from promise|In personam, to money or restoration|

:::recap Chapter 11 in five lines
- Obligation imposed by law to prevent unjust enrichment (*Moses v. Macferlan*); compensated as if a contract (s. 73 para 3).
- s. 68 necessaries — from property; s. 69 interested payer — reimbursement (lease-in-Sind illustration).
- s. 70 lawful, non-gratuitous act, benefit enjoyed — compensation (*B.K. Mondal*); s. 71 finder = bailee.
- s. 72 mistake (including law — *Kanhaiya Lal Saraf*) or coercion (wide sense) — repay.
- Quantum meruit (*Planché v. Colburn*; not for party in breach of entire contract — *Sumpter v. Hedges*); total failure of consideration (*Fibrosa*; s. 65).
:::

# Chapter 12 · Breach of contract and remedies (ss. 73 to 75)

## 12.1 Kinds of breach

1. **Actual breach** — failure to perform on the due date, or defective performance.
2. **Anticipatory breach** — refusal or disablement **before** the due date (s. 39; *Hochster*).

**Remedies available to the innocent party:**

{w:28,72}
|Remedy|Law|
|**Rescission** — treat the contract as ended, and refuse further performance|ss. 39, 64, 75; Specific Relief Act ss. 35–38|
|**Damages / compensation**|ss. 73–75|
|**Specific performance** — order to perform the contract|Specific Relief Act 1877, ss. 12–30 (where damages are not adequate — e.g., immovable property, unique goods)|
|**Injunction** — prohibitory or mandatory, including to enforce a negative covenant|SRA ss. 52–57; O. XXXIX CPC|
|**Quantum meruit**|ss. 65, 70|
|**Restitution** of money paid|ss. 64, 65, 72|

:::maxim Ubi jus ibi remedium — where there is a right, there is a remedy
The law gives the innocent party a remedy for every breach — at least **nominal damages**, even where no loss is proved.
:::

## 12.2 Compensation for breach — s. 73

> "73. When a contract has been broken, the party who suffers by such breach is entitled to receive, from the party who has broken the contract, compensation for any loss or damage caused to him thereby, which **naturally arose in the usual course of things** from such breach, or which the parties **knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result** from the breach of it.
> Such compensation is **not** to be given for any **remote and indirect** loss or damage sustained by reason of the breach.
> [Para 3 — quasi-contracts, as above.]
> Explanation.— In estimating the loss or damage arising from a breach of contract, the **means which existed of remedying the inconvenience** caused by the non-performance of the contract must be taken into account."

### The rule in Hadley v. Baxendale

:::case Hadley v. Baxendale ((1854) 9 Exch 341)
__Facts.__ The plaintiffs' mill stopped because its **crankshaft** broke. They gave it to the defendants, carriers, to take to engineers as a pattern for a new one. The carriers **delayed** delivery. The mill stayed idle for longer, and the plaintiffs claimed **lost profits**. The carriers were **not told** that the mill could not work without the shaft.

__Held.__ The lost profits could **not** be recovered. Damages are recoverable only for loss that:
1. **arises naturally**, according to the usual course of things, from the breach (**first limb** — general damages); or
2. may reasonably be supposed to have been in the **contemplation of both parties**, at the time they made the contract, as the probable result of the breach (**second limb** — special damages, based on **special knowledge** communicated).

A mill-owner might well have a **spare** shaft; so the stoppage was not a natural consequence, and the special circumstance had not been communicated.
:::

**Section 73 is the statutory form of Hadley v. Baxendale.** The two limbs correspond exactly to "naturally arose in the usual course of things" and "the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result".

:::case Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd. v. Newman Industries Ltd. ([1949] 2 KB 528)
__Facts.__ A boiler for a laundry business was delivered **five months late**. The laundry claimed (a) the ordinary profits it would have earned and (b) the exceptional profits from lucrative **dyeing contracts** with the Ministry of Supply.

__Held.__ The **ordinary** profits were recoverable — the sellers, being engineers, knew a laundry needed the boiler for immediate use. The **exceptional** profits from the Government contracts were **not**, as the sellers did not know of them. Recoverable loss is that which was **reasonably foreseeable** as liable to result, given the knowledge actually possessed.
:::

:::case Koufos v. C. Czarnikow Ltd. — The Heron II ([1969] 1 AC 350, House of Lords)
__Held.__ In **contract**, the test of remoteness is narrower than in tort: the loss must have been in the reasonable contemplation of the parties as "**not unlikely**" to result (a "serious possibility" or "real danger"), not merely reasonably foreseeable. A shipowner who delayed delivery of sugar was liable for the **fall in market price** at the port, as he must have known sugar was likely to be sold on arrival.
:::

### Kinds of damages

1. **Ordinary / general** — natural consequence of the breach (first limb).
2. **Special** — from special circumstances **communicated** at the time of contracting (second limb).
3. **Nominal** — a token sum where a right is infringed but no real loss is proved (*ubi jus ibi remedium*).
4. **Exemplary / punitive** — **not** ordinarily given in contract, because damages are **compensatory**, not to punish. Exceptions in English law: breach of **promise to marry**, and **dishonour of a cheque** by a banker with sufficient funds (injury to credit).
5. **Damages for mental distress** — generally not awarded in contract (*Addis v. Gramophone Co.* [1909] AC 488), except where the very object of the contract was **pleasure, relaxation or peace of mind** (*Jarvis v. Swans Tours* [1973] QB 233 — a ruined holiday).

### Principles of assessment

1. **Compensation, not punishment** — put the innocent party, so far as money can, in the position he would have been in **had the contract been performed** (*Robinson v. Harman* (1848) 1 Exch 850).
2. **Market rule** — in sale of goods, the difference between contract and market price **at the time and place of the breach** (illus. (a), (c); SGA ss. 56–57 — Topic 4).
3. **Duty to mitigate** (Explanation to s. 73) — the innocent party must take **reasonable steps** to reduce his loss and cannot recover loss he could reasonably have avoided (*British Westinghouse Electric v. Underground Electric Railways* [1912] AC 673).
4. **Loss must be proved** — the burden is on the claimant; damages must not be speculative, but **difficulty of assessment** is no reason to refuse damages (*Chaplin v. Hicks* [1911] 2 KB 786 — loss of a **chance** in a beauty contest compensated).
5. **Remoteness** — indirect and remote losses excluded (s. 73, para 2).
6. **Expenditure wasted** — the claimant may instead claim **reliance** expenses (wasted expenditure) in some cases (*Anglia Television v. Reed* [1972] 1 QB 60).

:::eg A local example of s. 73
Ali, a textile exporter in Faisalabad, orders a dyeing machine from Bilal Engineering for delivery by 1 March. He tells Bilal that he has an export order from a German buyer worth Rs. 20 million that he can fulfil only with the new machine. The machine arrives on 1 June; the order is lost. Bilal is liable for (i) the **normal profits** lost during the delay (first limb) and (ii) the lost profit on the **German order**, because that special circumstance was **communicated** at the time of contract (second limb; illus. (j), (l)). Ali must show he could not **mitigate** — e.g., by hiring another machine.
:::

## 12.3 Penalty and liquidated damages — s. 74

> "74. When a contract has been broken, if a **sum is named** in the contract as the amount to be paid in case of such breach, or if the contract contains any other **stipulation by way of penalty**, the party complaining of the breach is entitled, **whether or not actual damage or loss is proved** to have been caused thereby, to receive from the party who has broken the contract **reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount so named** or, as the case may be, the penalty stipulated for.
> Explanation.— A stipulation for **increased interest from the date of default** may be a stipulation by way of penalty.
> Exception.— When any person enters into any **bail-bond, recognizance** or other instrument of the same nature, or, under the provisions of any law, or under the orders of the Federal Government or of any Provincial Government, gives any **bond for the performance of any public duty** or act in which the public are interested, he shall be liable, upon breach of the condition of any such instrument, to pay the **whole sum** mentioned therein.
> Explanation.— A person who enters into a **contract with Government** does not necessarily thereby undertake any public duty, or promise to do an act in which the public are interested."

### English law vs our s. 74

- In **English law**, a stipulated sum is either (i) **liquidated damages** — a genuine **pre-estimate** of loss, enforceable in full, or (ii) a **penalty** — a sum **in terrorem** (to frighten), unenforceable; only actual loss is recovered (*Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre v. New Garage* [1915] AC 79; now restated in *Cavendish Square Holding v. Makdessi* [2015] UKSC 67 — whether the clause protects a **legitimate interest** and is not **out of all proportion**).
- Under **s. 74**, this distinction is **abolished** for practical purposes. Whether the sum is called penalty or liquidated damages, the court awards **reasonable compensation** up to the **named sum** as a **ceiling**.

:::case Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Dass (AIR 1963 SC 1405)
__Facts.__ Under an agreement to sell a house in Delhi, the buyer paid **Rs. 1,000 as earnest money** and **Rs. 24,000** towards the price, all to be forfeited on his default. The buyer defaulted; the seller forfeited the whole Rs. 25,000.

__Held.__
1. Section 74 does away with the English distinction between penalty and liquidated damages; the court awards **reasonable compensation not exceeding** the sum named.
2. The **earnest money** (Rs. 1,000) could be forfeited — it is a **guarantee** of performance.
3. The **Rs. 24,000** was part of the **price**, not earnest; its forfeiture was a stipulation **by way of penalty**. As the seller proved **no loss**, it had to be **refunded**.
4. "Whether or not actual damage or loss is proved" means that the court may award reasonable compensation even where the **exact** loss cannot be proved — but the court must still be satisfied that some **legal injury** resulted.
:::

:::case Maula Bux v. Union of India (AIR 1970 SC 1955)
__Held.__ Forfeiture of a reasonable sum paid as **earnest money** does not attract s. 74. But a **security deposit** for due performance is subject to s. 74; the Government could forfeit only reasonable compensation, and since it **could have proved** its loss and did not, it could not forfeit the deposit.
:::

### Pakistani illustrations (d)–(g) — Act VI of 1899

1. **(d)** A bond for Rs. 1,000 at **12 %**, with **75 %** from the date of default — a **penalty**; only reasonable compensation.
2. **(e)** A borrower to repay with 10 maunds of grain, or **20 maunds** on default — a **penalty**.
3. **(f)** A **loan of Rs. 1,000** by five monthly instalments, with the **whole becoming due on default** — **not** a penalty (an **acceleration clause** merely brings forward what is already owed).
4. **(g)** A **borrowing of Rs. 100** with a bond for **Rs. 200** by five yearly instalments of Rs. 40, whole due on default — **a penalty** (the bond amount itself is inflated).

The **Exception** — **bail-bonds**, **recognizances** and **bonds for public duties** — the **whole sum** is recoverable (illus. (c): recognizance of Rs. 500 forfeited — whole penalty payable). But under the second Explanation, an ordinary **contract with Government** (e.g., a supply or works contract) does not by itself involve a public duty.

### Earnest money in Pakistani property deals

Earnest money (*bayana*) is the advance paid as a **guarantee** that the buyer will complete. If the **buyer** defaults, the seller may **forfeit** a **reasonable** earnest money; if the **seller** defaults, the buyer may claim its **return** — often with an agreed **double** amount. Pakistani courts generally apply *Fateh Chand*: a **reasonable** earnest may be forfeited, but forfeiture of a **large part of the price** is a penalty, and only reasonable compensation is allowed.

### Interest and riba

Section 73 illus. (n) and s. 74 illus. (d) speak of interest. In Pakistan, **interest (riba)** is the subject of **Article 38(f)** of the Constitution (as amended by the **26th Amendment, 2024**, setting **1 January 2028** as the target to eliminate riba) and of the **Federal Shariat Court's judgment of April 2022**, which declared laws permitting interest repugnant to the Injunctions of Islam with effect from a future date. Banking disputes are decided under the **Financial Institutions (Recovery of Finances) Ordinance 2001**, which speaks of **"cost of funds"**. In an exam, you may note that **"reasonable compensation"** under s. 74 for delayed payment is today increasingly framed in these terms. Do **not** say that all interest is presently void under the Contract Act — the position is still evolving.

## 12.4 Compensation on rightful rescission — s. 75

> "75. A person who **rightly rescinds** a contract is entitled to **compensation** for any damage which he has sustained through the non-fulfilment of the contract."

*Illustration:* the singer absent on the sixth night; B rescinds and **may claim compensation** (while paying for the five nights sung under s. 65, illus. (c)).

**Point.** Rescission and damages are **not alternatives** — a party who rightly rescinds **does not lose** his right to compensation (contrast the old view that rescission wipes out the contract entirely).

:::recap Chapter 12 in eight lines
- Remedies: rescission, damages, specific performance, injunction, quantum meruit, restitution (*ubi jus ibi remedium*).
- s. 73 = *Hadley v. Baxendale*: natural loss + loss in contemplation from communicated special knowledge; no remote / indirect loss.
- *Victoria Laundry* (ordinary vs exceptional profits); *The Heron II* ("not unlikely").
- Use the illustrations (a)–(r): market rule, interest only for money (n), sub-sales ignored (o), special knowledge (j), (l).
- Compensatory not punitive; mental distress only for pleasure contracts (*Jarvis*); nominal damages; duty to mitigate (Explanation; *British Westinghouse*); loss of chance (*Chaplin v. Hicks*).
- s. 74: named sum = ceiling; reasonable compensation whether or not loss proved; penalty / liquidated distinction abolished (*Fateh Chand*); earnest money (*Maula Bux*).
- Pakistani illus. (d)–(g): penal interest and inflated bonds are penalties; acceleration of an actual loan is not. Exception: bail-bonds / public duty bonds — whole sum.
- s. 75: rightful rescission + compensation.
:::

# Chapter 13 · Terms of a contract — intention and relative importance

This chapter answers the **KP High Court 2026** question on "the **intention of the parties** and the **relative importance of contractual terms**". It draws on the Contract Act (ss. 39, 55), the **Sale of Goods Act 1930** (ss. 11–13 — Topic 4), and the common law.

## 13.1 Finding the intention of the parties

The court's task in interpreting a contract is to find the **intention of the parties** — but judged **objectively**: what a **reasonable person**, with the background knowledge available to the parties, would understand the words to mean — not what one party secretly intended.

:::maxim Verba chartarum fortius accipiuntur contra proferentem — words are construed most strongly against the party who put them forward
Where a term (especially an exclusion clause) is **ambiguous**, it is read **against** the party who drafted it and relies on it — the **contra proferentem** rule.
:::

Rules of interpretation:

1. Read the contract **as a whole**; give effect to **every part** if possible (*ut res magis valeat quam pereat* — it is better that a thing should have effect than be made void).
2. Words in their **ordinary** meaning, technical words in their technical sense, in their **commercial context**.
3. **Specific** words prevail over general; **handwritten / typed** additions over printed standard terms.
4. Where there is ambiguity, look at the **surrounding circumstances** and the **conduct** of the parties.
5. **Oral evidence** to vary a written contract is excluded, subject to exceptions — **Arts. 102–103, Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984** (the "parol evidence" rule; formerly ss. 91–92, Evidence Act).

## 13.2 Express and implied terms

- **Express terms** — stated in words, orally or in writing.
- **Implied terms** —
  1. **implied in fact**, to give **business efficacy** to the contract (*The Moorcock* (1889) 14 PD 64 — a wharf owner impliedly undertook that the river-bed at the jetty was safe for the ship to lie on at low tide), or terms so obvious that an "**officious bystander**" would say "of course!" (*Shirlaw v. Southern Foundries* [1939] 2 KB 206);
  2. **implied by law** — e.g., the conditions and warranties implied in a sale of goods (**SGA ss. 14–17**: title, description, quality and fitness, sample);
  3. **implied by custom or trade usage**, if reasonable, certain and notorious, and not inconsistent with the express terms.

## 13.3 Conditions, warranties and innominate terms

Not every term is of equal importance. The consequence of a breach depends on the **class** of term broken.

![Relative importance of contractual terms](terms_map.png)

### Condition

A term **essential to the main purpose** of the contract. Breach gives the innocent party the right to **treat the contract as repudiated** (terminate) **and** claim damages. **SGA s. 12(2):** "A condition is a stipulation essential to the main purpose of the contract, the breach of which gives rise to a right to treat the contract as repudiated."

### Warranty

A term **collateral** to the main purpose. Breach gives only a right to **damages**, not to terminate. **SGA s. 12(3):** "A warranty is a stipulation collateral to the main purpose of the contract, the breach of which gives rise to a claim for damages but not to a right to reject the goods and treat the contract as repudiated."

**How to tell which is which — SGA s. 12(4):** "Whether a stipulation in a contract of sale is a condition or a warranty depends in each case on the **construction of the contract**. A stipulation may be a condition, though called a warranty in the contract." The label used by the parties is **not conclusive**.

:::case Poussard v. Spiers and Pond ((1876) 1 QBD 410)
__Facts.__ An opera singer engaged for the leading role fell ill and could not appear for the **first week**, including the **opening night**. The producers engaged a substitute and refused her services when she recovered.

__Held.__ Her failure to appear at the start went to the **root** of the contract — breach of a **condition**. The producers could **terminate**.
:::

:::case Bettini v. Gye ((1876) 1 QBD 183)
__Facts.__ A singer engaged for a season was required to arrive **six days before** the first performance for **rehearsals**. He arrived only **two days** early. The employer refused to accept him.

__Held.__ The rehearsal term was **subsidiary** — a **warranty**; its breach did not go to the root. The employer could claim **damages** but could not terminate.
:::

### Innominate (intermediate) terms

Some terms cannot be classified in advance; the effect of breach depends on the **seriousness of its consequences**.

:::case Hongkong Fir Shipping Co. v. Kawasaki Kisen Kaisha ([1962] 2 QB 26)
__Facts.__ A ship chartered for 24 months was to be "**seaworthy**". Because of an incompetent engine-room crew and old engines, it lost about **20 weeks** to repairs. The charterers terminated.

__Held.__ "Seaworthiness" is an **innominate term** — it can be broken in trivial or serious ways. The test is whether the breach deprived the innocent party of **substantially the whole benefit** intended. Here it did not (the charter still had a long time to run). The charterers were **not entitled** to terminate; they were liable for wrongful repudiation.
:::

:::case L. Schuler AG v. Wickman Machine Tool Sales Ltd. ([1974] AC 235, House of Lords)
__Held.__ A clause calling the obligation to visit customers weekly a "**condition**" did not by that word alone make **every** minor breach a ground for termination. The more unreasonable the result, the more clearly the parties must have intended it. The **label** is strong evidence, but **not conclusive**.
:::

:::case The Mihalis Angelos ([1971] 1 QB 164)
__Held.__ An "**expected ready to load**" clause in a charter-party is a **condition**: commercial certainty requires that the charterer can terminate if it is broken. In **mercantile** contracts, **time** clauses are usually conditions (*Bunge Corp. v. Tradax Export* [1981] 1 WLR 711).
:::

### Under the Contract Act

The Contract Act does not use the words "condition" and "warranty", but reaches similar results:

1. **s. 39** — a refusal or disablement to perform "**in its entirety**" allows termination; partial failure gives damages.
2. **s. 55** — failure to perform at the fixed time makes the contract **voidable** only if **time was of the essence** — i.e., if the time clause is in effect a **condition**; otherwise only **compensation** (like a warranty).
3. **s. 53** — prevention by the other party.

### When a condition is treated as a warranty — SGA s. 13

The buyer may **waive** a condition, or **elect** to treat its breach as a breach of warranty; and where the buyer has **accepted** the goods (in a non-severable contract), a breach of condition can only be treated as a breach of warranty (SGA s. 13). (Topic 4.)

## 13.4 Exemption (exclusion) clauses — a short note

Standard-form contracts (bus tickets, laundry receipts, bank forms, insurance policies) often contain clauses **excluding** or **limiting** liability. Courts control them by:

1. **Incorporation** — a clause binds only if it is part of the contract: a **signed** document binds even if unread (*L'Estrange v. Graucob* [1934] 2 KB 394), unless there was misrepresentation (*Curtis v. Chemical Cleaning* [1951] 1 KB 805); an **unsigned** ticket or notice binds only if **reasonable notice** was given **before or at** the time of contracting (*Parker v. South Eastern Railway* (1877) 2 CPD 416; *Olley v. Marlborough Court Hotel* [1949] 1 KB 532 — notice in the hotel room came too late).
2. **Construction** — read **contra proferentem**; clear words are needed to exclude liability for **negligence**.
3. **Fundamental breach** — whether a clause covers even a total failure of performance is a question of **construction** (*Photo Production v. Securicor Transport* [1980] AC 827).
4. **Statute and public policy** — a clause contrary to s. 23 (e.g., excluding liability for **fraud**), or to a statute (e.g., the bailee's minimum duty of care under **ss. 151–152**, as held in *Lily White v. R. Munuswami*, AIR 1966 Mad 13 — a laundry's clause limiting liability to 50 % of the market price of lost goods was invalid), is not enforced. Consumer protection laws (e.g., the **Islamabad Consumers Protection Act 1995**) also protect consumers from unfair terms and defective services.

- Intention is judged objectively; read the contract as a whole; *contra proferentem*; parol evidence rule (Arts. 102–103 QSO).
- Implied terms: business efficacy (*The Moorcock*), officious bystander (*Shirlaw*), by law (SGA ss. 14–17), by custom.
- Condition — essential, breach allows termination + damages (SGA s. 12(2); *Poussard*). Warranty — collateral, damages only (s. 12(3); *Bettini*). Label not conclusive (s. 12(4); *Schuler*).
- Innominate term — depends on seriousness of consequences (*Hongkong Fir*); mercantile time clauses are conditions (*The Mihalis Angelos*; *Bunge v. Tradax*).
- Contract Act analogues: s. 39 (refusal in entirety), s. 55 (time of essence), s. 53 (prevention); SGA s. 13 (condition treated as warranty).
- Exemption clauses: incorporation (*L'Estrange*; *Parker*; *Olley*), construction, fundamental breach (*Photo Production*), public policy and statute (*Lily White*).
:::
