
# Answer 4 · Agreements without consideration are void — the exceptions in s. 25 (LHC 2023)

:::glance LAHORE HIGH COURT 2023 · CIVIL LAW-I · Q. 3 · 20 MARKS
Agreements without consideration are void. What exceptions are provided in section 25 of the Contract Act 1872? Discuss the illustrations.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](c4_map.png)

## Decoding the question

The question has three layers: (i) the **rule** — consideration is essential and its absence makes an agreement void (*ex nudo pacto non oritur actio*); (ii) the **three exceptions** in s. 25 with their conditions; and (iii) the **illustrations (a)–(g)**, which the examiner expressly asks you to discuss. Add the two Explanations and the other no-consideration cases in the Act to complete the picture.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — the rule

"An agreement made without consideration is **void**, unless—" (s. 25). Consideration is the **price of the promise** (s. 2(d)). The common law expresses the rule in the maxim *ex nudo pacto non oritur actio* — no action arises from a bare promise. The reason is that the law enforces **bargains**, not gratuitous promises, which are often made casually and without deliberation.

*Illustration (a):* "A promises, for no consideration, to give to B Rs. 1,000. This is a void agreement."

### 2. Meaning of consideration — s. 2(d)

Consideration is the act, abstinence or promise of the promisee **or any other person**, done **at the desire of the promisor**, which may be past, present or future. It must be real but need not be adequate (*Durga Prasad v. Baldeo*, (1880) ILR 3 All 221 — act done at a third party's desire is no consideration; *Chinnaya v. Ramayya*, (1882) ILR 4 Mad 137 — consideration may move from a third person).

### 3. Exception 1 — natural love and affection — s. 25(1)

#### i) Text

An agreement without consideration is valid if "it is expressed **in writing** and **registered** under the law for the time being in force for the registration of documents, and is made on account of **natural love and affection** between parties standing in a **near relation** to each other".

#### ii) Conditions

1. It is **in writing**;
2. it is **registered** (Registration Act 1908);
3. it is made on account of **natural love and affection**;
4. the parties are in a **near relation** (e.g., parent and child, husband and wife, brothers).

All four must exist together.

#### iii) Illustration (b)

"A, for natural love and affection, promises to give his son, B, Rs. 1,000. A puts his promise to B into writing and registers it. This is a contract."

#### iv) Case

Near relationship alone is not enough; love and affection must be the **motive**. In *Rajlukhy Dabee v. Bhootnath Mookerjee* ((1900) 4 CWN 488) a husband's registered promise to pay his wife maintenance, made after **quarrels** and separation, was held void: there was no natural love and affection behind it.

### 4. Exception 2 — compensation for past voluntary service — s. 25(2)

#### i) Text

"…it is a promise to **compensate**, wholly or in part, a person who has **already voluntarily done something** for the promisor, or something which the promisor was **legally compellable** to do."

#### ii) Conditions

1. The promisee did the act **voluntarily** — without the promisor's request (if it was at his request, it would be ordinary past consideration under s. 2(d));
2. the act was done **for the promisor**, or was something the promisor was legally bound to do;
3. the promisor was **in existence** and **capable** of contracting when the act was done;
4. the promisor **later** promises to compensate.

#### iii) Illustrations (c) and (d)

(c) "A finds B's purse and gives it to him. B promises to give A Rs. 50. This is a contract." (d) "A supports B's infant son. B promises to pay A's expenses in so doing. This is a contract." In (d), B was legally bound to maintain his son; A's voluntary act relieved B of that duty.

#### iv) Rationale

The law gives effect to a **moral obligation** that the promisor himself has recognised by a later promise.

### 5. Exception 3 — promise to pay a time-barred debt — s. 25(3)

#### i) Text

"…it is a promise, made **in writing** and **signed** by the person to be charged therewith, or by his agent generally or specially authorized in that behalf, to pay wholly or in part a **debt** of which the creditor might have enforced payment but for the **law for the limitation** of suits."

#### ii) Conditions

1. A **debt** (a definite sum) existed;
2. it was enforceable **but for limitation** — i.e., already **time-barred**;
3. there is an express **promise to pay** it, wholly or in part (not a mere acknowledgment);
4. the promise is **in writing** and **signed** by the debtor or his authorised agent.

#### iii) Illustration (e)

"A owes B Rs. 1,000, but the debt is barred by the Limitation Act. A signs a written promise to pay B Rs. 500 on account of the debt. This is a contract." B may sue for Rs. 500 on the **new promise** — not for Rs. 1,000.

#### iv) Distinction from s. 19, Limitation Act

{w:30,35,35}
|Point|s. 19 Limitation Act|s. 25(3) Contract Act|
|Timing|Acknowledgment **before** limitation expires|Promise **after** limitation has expired|
|Nature|Acknowledgment of liability is enough|Must be an express **promise to pay**|
|Effect|**Fresh period** for the **old** debt|A **new contract**; suit on the new promise|

### 6. Explanation 1 — completed gifts

"Nothing in this section shall affect the validity, as between the donor and donee, of any **gift actually made**." A gift that has been **completed** (by delivery or by registered transfer under the Transfer of Property Act, or by declaration, acceptance and delivery of possession under Muslim law — hiba) needs no consideration. Only a **promise** to make a gift is void.

### 7. Explanation 2 — inadequacy of consideration

"An agreement to which the consent of the promisor is freely given is **not void merely because the consideration is inadequate**; but the inadequacy … may be taken into account by the Court in determining the question whether the consent of the promisor was freely given."

*Illustration (f):* A agrees to sell a horse worth Rs. 1,000 for Rs. 10; consent freely given — a valid contract. *Illustration (g):* same, but A says his consent was not free — the **inadequacy is evidence** the court considers on the question of free consent (coercion, undue influence, fraud).

### 8. Other cases where consideration is not needed

1. **Agency** — "No consideration is necessary to create an agency" (s. 185).
2. **Gratuitous bailment** — valid; the bailee owes duties (ss. 148, 151).
3. **Guarantee** — anything done for the principal debtor is sufficient consideration for the surety (s. 127).
4. **Remission** — the promisee may remit performance or accept any satisfaction (s. 63).
5. **Charitable subscriptions** acted on — *Kedarnath Bhattacharji v. Gorie Mahomed* ((1886) ILR 14 Cal 64) held such a subscription enforceable once liabilities were incurred on its faith; contrast *Abdul Aziz v. Masum Ali* ((1914) ILR 36 All 268).

### 9. Conclusion

Section 25 lays down the rule that an agreement without consideration is void, and then makes three **exceptions**: a **written and registered** promise out of **natural love and affection** between near relatives; a promise to **compensate past voluntary service**; and a **written, signed** promise to pay a **time-barred debt**. Completed gifts are untouched, and inadequacy of consideration does not avoid a contract freely made. The illustrations (a)–(g) are the clearest guide to each rule.

## Why this answer scores

- The rule, the **three exceptions** and the **two Explanations** each have their own heading, as the question invites.
- **Every illustration (a)–(g)** is quoted and placed under the right exception — exactly what "discuss the illustrations" means.
- The **s. 19 Limitation Act** comparison table answers the most common confusion.
- The final section shows **wider knowledge** (ss. 63, 127, 185; charitable subscriptions) without drifting from the question.

# Answer 5 · Agreements by minors and persons of unsound mind and their legal effects (KPHC 2026)

:::glance KP HIGH COURT 2026 · CIVIL LAW-I · PART III · Q. 8 · 20 MARKS
Discuss the provisions of law regarding agreements by minors and agreements by persons of unsound mind and their legal effects on the transactions.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](c5_map.png)

## Decoding the question

"Provisions of law" means ss. 10, 11, 12, 64, 65 and 68 of the Contract Act, the **Majority Act 1875**, s. 41 of the **Specific Relief Act**, and related rules (agency, partnership, negotiable instruments). "Legal effects on the transactions" means: is the agreement void or voidable? Can it be ratified? Can money paid be recovered? Can the minor be estopped? What about necessaries? Answer both halves (minor and unsound mind) with cases.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — capacity is an essential of contract

Section 10 requires that a contract be made by "parties **competent** to contract". Section 11 defines competence:

> "Every person is competent to contract who is of the **age of majority** according to the law to which he is subject, and who is of **sound mind**, and is not disqualified from contracting by any law to which he is subject."

The law protects minors and persons of unsound mind because they cannot properly judge their own interests.

### 2. Who is a minor

Under the **Majority Act 1875**, s. 3, a person attains majority on completing **18 years**; if a **guardian** of his person or property has been appointed by a court, or his property is under a **Court of Wards**, at **21 years**. Matters of marriage, dower, divorce and adoption are excluded (s. 2).

### 3. Nature of a minor's agreement — void ab initio

The Act says a minor is "not competent" but not what the effect is. The **Privy Council** settled it in *Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose* ((1903) ILR 30 Cal 539):

1. A minor mortgaged his house to a money-lender for Rs. 20,000, of which about Rs. 8,000 was advanced; the lender's agent **knew** of the minority.
2. Held: a minor's agreement is **absolutely void**, not merely voidable, because competence is an essential of contract (ss. 10–11).
3. The lender could not recover the money under **s. 64 or s. 65**, which presuppose a contract between competent parties.
4. Relief under **s. 41, Specific Relief Act** (compensation on cancellation) is **discretionary** and was refused because the lender knew of the minority.

### 4. Effects of a minor's agreement

#### i) No enforcement against the minor

Neither the minor nor his estate can be sued on the agreement.

#### ii) No ratification on majority

A void agreement cannot be ratified. A **fresh promise** for **fresh consideration** after majority is a new contract.

#### iii) No estoppel

A minor who **misrepresents his age** is not estopped from pleading minority (*Sadiq Ali Khan v. Jai Kishori*, AIR 1928 PC 152). Estoppel (Art. 114, Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984) cannot override the statute.

#### iv) Restitution of benefits obtained by fraud

Equity prevents a minor from using minority as a **sword**. In *Khan Gul v. Lakha Singh* (AIR 1928 Lah 609, FB), a minor who obtained Rs. 17,500 by falsely posing as an adult was ordered to **refund** it, though the sale could not be enforced. The Allahabad Full Bench in *Ajudhia Prasad v. Chandan Lal* (AIR 1937 All 610) limited restitution to cases where the **minor is the plaintiff**. The English rule allows only **traceable** property to be recovered (*R. Leslie Ltd. v. Sheill*, [1914] 3 KB 607).

#### v) No specific performance either way

Not against the minor; nor in his favour, for want of **mutuality** (*Mir Sarwarjan v. Fakhruddin Mahomed Chowdhury*, (1912) ILR 39 Cal 232, PC).

#### vi) Minor as beneficiary

A minor may be a **promisee**, **payee** or **endorsee** (s. 26, Negotiable Instruments Act), or a **mortgagee**, and can enforce a contract under which he has fully performed (*Raghava Chariar v. Srinivasa*, (1917) ILR 40 Mad 308, FB).

#### vii) Minor as agent and in partnership

A minor may be an **agent**, binding the principal, though not liable to the principal (s. 184). He may be **admitted to the benefits** of a partnership with all partners' consent (s. 30, Partnership Act 1932).

#### viii) Guardian's contracts

A contract by the minor's **guardian**, within his powers and for the minor's **benefit**, binds the minor's estate (*Subrahmanyam v. Subba Rao*, AIR 1948 PC 95), subject to court permission for alienations by court-appointed guardians (s. 29, Guardians and Wards Act 1890).

#### ix) Torts

A minor is liable for torts, but not where the tort is the contract in disguise (*Leslie v. Sheill*).

### 5. Necessaries — s. 68

> "If a person, incapable of entering into a contract, or any one whom he is legally bound to support, is supplied by another person with **necessaries suited to his condition in life**, the person who has furnished such supplies is entitled to be **reimbursed from the property** of such incapable person."

1. The liability is **quasi-contractual**, limited to the minor's **property** — no personal liability.
2. "Necessaries" are goods and services suited to his **status** and his **actual needs** at the time — food, clothes, education, medical care, legal defence of his property; not luxuries or an excess supply (*Nash v. Inman*, [1908] 2 KB 1 — eleven fancy waistcoats).

### 6. Persons of unsound mind — s. 12

> "A person is said to be of sound mind for the purpose of making a contract if, at the time when he makes it, he is **capable of understanding** it and of **forming a rational judgment** as to its effect upon his interests.
> A person who is usually of unsound mind, but occasionally of sound mind, may make a contract when he is of sound mind.
> A person who is usually of sound mind, but occasionally of unsound mind, may not make a contract when he is of unsound mind."

#### i) The test

The two-part test — **understanding** and **rational judgment** — is applied **at the time of contracting**.

#### ii) Illustrations

(a) A patient in a lunatic asylum may contract in **lucid intervals**. (b) A sane man **delirious with fever** or **so drunk** that he cannot understand cannot contract while that state lasts.

#### iii) Burden of proof

Sanity is presumed; the person alleging unsoundness must prove it. If a person is shown to be **usually** of unsound mind, the burden shifts to the one who relies on a **lucid interval**.

### 7. Effects of an agreement by a person of unsound mind

1. It is **void**, applying the same reasoning as *Mohori Bibee* (ss. 10–12).
2. **Necessaries** supplied to him or his dependants are reimbursable from his **property** (s. 68, illustrations (a) and (b)).
3. Where a **manager or guardian** of his property has been appointed under the **Mental Health Ordinance 2001**, transactions must go through that manager.
4. Partial weakness of mind that falls short of s. 12 may still make the contract **voidable** for **undue influence**, where the other party dominated a person "whose mental capacity is temporarily or permanently affected by reason of age, illness, or mental or bodily distress" (s. 16(2)(b)).

### 8. Comparison

{w:28,36,36}
|Point|Minor|Person of unsound mind|
|Incapacity|Permanent until majority (18 / 21)|May be temporary; lucid intervals allowed|
|Test|Age|Understanding + rational judgment at the time (s. 12)|
|Effect|Void ab initio (*Mohori Bibee*)|Void|
|Necessaries|From property (s. 68)|From property (s. 68)|
|Ratification|Not possible|Not possible, but may contract afresh when sane|
|Agent|Can be an agent (s. 184)|Cannot be responsible as agent (s. 184)|

### 9. Illustration

Hamza, aged 17, buys a motorcycle worth Rs. 300,000 on instalments from a dealer in Rawalpindi, stating that he is 19. He pays Rs. 50,000 and defaults. The agreement is **void**; the dealer cannot sue for the price or rely on estoppel. But the court may order Hamza to **return the motorcycle** (traceable property obtained by fraud — *Khan Gul*; *Leslie v. Sheill*). If the motorcycle was a **necessary** for his travel to college, given his condition in life, the dealer may be reimbursed its **reasonable value** from Hamza's property (s. 68).

### 10. Conclusion

A minor's agreement is **void ab initio**; it cannot be enforced, ratified or made binding by estoppel, though a minor may be ordered to restore benefits obtained by fraud, and is liable for necessaries out of his property. A person of unsound mind is in the same position, except that he may contract validly during a **lucid interval**, and weakness short of unsoundness is dealt with by the law of **undue influence**. These rules protect the vulnerable at the cost of those who deal with them — who act at their own risk.

## Why this answer scores

- It quotes **ss. 11, 12 and 68** and states the **Majority Act** rule precisely.
- *Mohori Bibee* is given its **four points**, and the "effects" are listed one by one as the question requires.
- The **Lahore Full Bench** (*Khan Gul*) is a Pakistani-heritage authority examiners look for.
- The **comparison table** and the **illustration** show application, not just recall.

# Answer 6 · When is consent not free? With illustrations (SHC 2022)

:::glance SINDH HIGH COURT 2022 · CIVIL LAW · Q. 2 (alternative) · 20 MARKS
Under what circumstances can it be said that a contract is not by free consent? Give illustrations.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](c6_map.png)

## Decoding the question

"Circumstances" = the five factors of s. 14 — **coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation and mistake** — each with its definition, ingredients, a case and an **illustration** (use the Act's own). Explain the **"but for"** causation test and the **effect** of each (voidable or void), including the s. 19 Exception. A table at the end is efficient.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — consent and free consent

"Two or more persons are said to consent when they agree upon the **same thing in the same sense**" (s. 13). Consent is **free** when it is not caused by coercion (s. 15), undue influence (s. 16), fraud (s. 17), misrepresentation (s. 18) or mistake (ss. 20–22) (s. 14). Free consent is an essential of a contract (s. 10).

### 2. Causation — the "but for" test

"Consent is said to be so caused when it **would not have been given but for** the existence of such coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake" (s. 14). A defect that did not influence the decision does not affect the contract (s. 19 Explanation).

### 3. Coercion — s. 15

#### i) Definition

"'Coercion' is the committing, or threatening to commit, any act **forbidden by the Pakistan Penal Code** or the **unlawful detaining, or threatening to detain, any property**, to the prejudice of any person whatever, with the intention of causing any person to enter into an agreement." It is immaterial whether the PPC is in force where the coercion is used (Explanation).

#### ii) Illustration and cases

*Statutory illustration:* A, on an English ship on the high seas, makes B agree by criminal intimidation; A sues at Karachi — coercion. *Ranganayakamma v. Alwar Setti* ((1889) ILR 13 Mad 214): a young widow made to adopt a boy before her husband's body could be removed for cremation — coercion. *Chikham Amiraju v. Seshamma* ((1917) ILR 41 Mad 33): threat of suicide held coercion (then an offence).

#### iii) Effect

**Voidable** at the option of the coerced party (s. 19); money paid under coercion is recoverable (s. 72).

### 4. Undue influence — s. 16

#### i) Definition

A contract is induced by undue influence "where the relations subsisting between the parties are such that one of the parties is **in a position to dominate the will** of the other and **uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage**" (s. 16(1)). Domination is presumed where there is **real or apparent authority**, a **fiduciary relation**, or the other's **mental capacity is affected** by age, illness or distress (s. 16(2)). If the transaction is **unconscionable**, the **burden shifts** to the dominant party (s. 16(3)).

#### ii) Illustrations and case

(a) A father obtains from his son, just of age, a bond for more than is due; (b) a medical attendant extracts an unreasonable fee from an old, sick patient; (c) a village money-lender's unconscionable fresh loan — burden on lender; (d) a bank's high rate in a tight money market — **not** undue influence. *Raghunath Prasad v. Sarju Prasad* ((1923) 51 IA 101, PC) laid down the **three questions**: domination, use of the position, and burden on unconscionability.

#### iii) Effect

**Voidable**; may be set aside **absolutely** or on **just terms** if the party has received a benefit (s. 19A; illus. (b) — repay Rs. 100 with reasonable interest).

### 5. Fraud — s. 17

#### i) Definition

Acts by a party (or with his connivance, or by his agent) with **intent to deceive** or to induce a contract: a **false suggestion** of fact by one not believing it; **active concealment**; a **promise without intention** to perform; any other act **fitted to deceive**; or acts the law declares fraudulent.

#### ii) Silence

"Mere silence … is not fraud, unless … it is the **duty** of the person keeping silence to speak, or unless his silence is, in itself, **equivalent to speech**." Illustrations: (a) a horse known to be unsound sold at auction without a word — not fraud; (b) the buyer is the seller's daughter just of age — duty to speak; (c) "If you do not deny it, I shall assume the horse is sound" — silence equals speech; (d) traders with private price information — no duty.

#### iii) Case and effect

*Derry v. Peek* ((1889) 14 App Cas 337): fraud requires a false statement made **knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly**. Effect: **voidable**, or the party may affirm and insist on being placed as if the statement were true (s. 19; illus. (c) — estate said to be free from mortgage); damages in **deceit**.

### 6. Misrepresentation — s. 18

#### i) Definition

(1) A **positive assertion**, not warranted by the maker's information, of what is untrue, **though he believes it**; (2) a **breach of duty** that, without intent to deceive, gains an advantage by misleading another; (3) **innocently causing** a party to make a mistake as to the **substance** of the subject matter.

#### ii) Illustration and effect

*s. 19 illus. (a):* A falsely represents that 500 maunds of indigo are made yearly at his factory — voidable. *Illus. (b):* B checks the accounts which show 400 maunds, and still buys — **not voidable** (Exception: the truth was discoverable with ordinary diligence; and the statement did not cause the consent).

### 7. Mistake — ss. 20–22

1. **Bilateral mistake of essential fact** — the agreement is **void** (s. 20). Illustrations: cargo already lost on its way to **Karachi**; a horse already dead; a life estate when the life had ended. An erroneous opinion about **value** is not a mistake of fact (Explanation). *Couturier v. Hastie* ((1856) 5 HLC 673).
2. **Mistake of Pakistani law** — not voidable; **foreign law** — like a mistake of fact (s. 21).
3. **Unilateral mistake** — not voidable (s. 22), except mistake as to **identity** where it is vital (*Cundy v. Lindsay*, (1878) 3 App Cas 459) and *non est factum* (*Foster v. Mackinnon*, (1869) LR 4 CP 704).

### 8. Summary table

{w:22,40,38}
|Factor|Key test|Effect|
|Coercion (s. 15)|PPC act or unlawful detention of property|Voidable (s. 19); refund (s. 72)|
|Undue influence (s. 16)|Domination + unfair advantage; burden shift|Voidable; set aside on terms (s. 19A)|
|Fraud (s. 17)|Intentional deception; silence only with duty|Voidable or affirm; damages|
|Misrepresentation (s. 18)|Innocent but unwarranted false statement|Voidable or affirm; s. 19 Exception|
|Mistake (ss. 20–22)|Bilateral essential fact|Void; law / unilateral — no relief|

### 9. Conclusion

Consent is not free when it is **caused** by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake. The first four make the contract **voidable** at the option of the party misled or pressured; a bilateral mistake of essential fact makes the agreement **void**. The law thus protects the **genuineness** of the will behind every contract.

## Why this answer scores

- It begins with **s. 13 and s. 14**, including the often-missed **"but for"** causation rule.
- Each factor has **definition → illustration → case → effect** — a repeatable pattern.
- The **statutory illustrations** are used extensively, as the question asks for illustrations.
- The **summary table** gives the examiner the whole picture at a glance.

# Answer 7 · Aslam's mother and the bank guarantee (LHC 2017)

:::glance LAHORE HIGH COURT 2017 · CIVIL LAW-I · Q. 1 · 20 MARKS
Aslam is a young businessman who has just graduated from University. He decides to start up a business, which organizes marriage functions within Lahore — seeing an opportunity in a local market. Aslam approaches Bank for obtaining a loan to secure office premises. The bank is impressed by his business plan, but given his inexperience, is only willing to lend him Rs. 20.00 Million, if the loan is guaranteed.
One evening, Aslam and a representative from Bank go to visit Aslam's mother in a village. Aslam's mother (Sakina) is seventy years old and she has been in the village for nine years after a car accident in which her husband was killed. As a result of the accident, she suffered a major brain hemorrhage and has lost most memories of her life, and suffers from some language comprehension difficulties. She is very passive and she was heavily reliant on her husband for emotional and financial support.
Aslam shows her mother the contract and tells her: "The Bank just wants to make sure that everything will be okay with my new business. They just want to make sure that someone will pay them if it goes broke, but I am sure it won't come to that. Can you just sign here?" Her signatures are obtained on the deed of guarantee.
Is Aslam's mother liable under the guarantee? Explain your opinion with reasons.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](c7_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Every fact is a clue. **Age 70, brain haemorrhage, memory loss, language comprehension difficulties** → capacity (s. 12) and s. 16(2)(b). **Passive and reliant** → domination; **son** → relationship of influence. **"I am sure it won't come to that"** → misrepresentation of the nature and risk of the guarantee. **Bank representative present, no independent advice** → whether the **bank** is affected (*Etridge*). **"Just sign here"** → *non est factum*. Examine each line of defence, and give a clear opinion on whether she is liable.

## The model answer

### 1. Issues

1. Was a **contract of guarantee** formed (s. 126)?
2. Did Sakina have **capacity** to contract (ss. 11–12)?
3. Was her consent caused by **undue influence** (ss. 16, 19A)?
4. Was it caused by **misrepresentation** or **fraud** (ss. 17–19)?
5. Can she plead **non est factum**?
6. Is the **bank** affected by Aslam's conduct, given the circumstances?

### 2. The contract of guarantee

"A 'contract of guarantee' is a contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in case of his default" (s. 126). The loan to Aslam is sufficient **consideration** for the surety (s. 127). On its face, the deed signed by Sakina is a valid guarantee for Rs. 20 million. The question is whether it binds her. (Guarantee is studied in full in Topic 4.)

### 3. Capacity — s. 12

A person is of sound mind for contracting if, **at the time**, she is "capable of understanding it and of forming a rational judgment as to its effect upon [her] interests" (s. 12). Sakina has suffered a **major brain haemorrhage**, has **lost most of her memories** and has **language comprehension difficulties**. If medical evidence shows she could not understand the deed or judge its effect on her interests, she was **incompetent**, and the guarantee is **void** (ss. 10–12; *Mohori Bibee* principle). The **burden** lies on her to prove unsoundness, but these facts give her a strong case.

### 4. Undue influence — s. 16

#### i) Position to dominate

Under s. 16(2)(b), a person is deemed to be in a position to dominate where he contracts with a person "whose mental capacity is temporarily or permanently affected by reason of **age, illness, or mental or bodily distress**". Sakina is **70**, **brain-injured**, **passive**, and was **reliant** on her family. Her **son** — on whom she would naturally rely after her husband's death — clearly had **influence** over her will.

#### ii) Unfair advantage

The guarantee exposed her (and possibly her property) to liability for **Rs. 20 million** for a **risky new business**, with **no benefit** to her. On its face it is **unconscionable** (s. 16(3)).

#### iii) Burden of proof

Since the transaction is unconscionable and the dominant party was in a position to dominate, "the burden of proving that such contract was not induced by undue influence shall lie upon the person in a position to dominate" (s. 16(3)). Article 127 of the Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984 also places on a person in **active confidence** the burden of proving **good faith**. The bank, claiming under the deed, must show she acted freely with full understanding.

#### iv) Authority

*Raghunath Prasad v. Sarju Prasad* ((1923) 51 IA 101, PC): domination, unfair use, and the shifting burden. *Lloyds Bank v. Bundy* ([1975] QB 326): an elderly farmer's guarantee of his son's company debts, given on the bank manager's word without independent advice, was **set aside**.

### 5. Misrepresentation and fraud — ss. 17–18

Aslam told her that the bank "just wants to make sure that everything will be okay" and "I am sure it won't come to that". This **misstated the nature and risk** of a guarantee — a binding promise to pay **Rs. 20 million** on default — as a mere formality. If Aslam believed it, it is **misrepresentation** (s. 18(1), and s. 18(3) — innocently causing a mistake as to the **substance** of the agreement); if he knew better, it is **fraud** (s. 17(1)). The bank representative was present and **did not correct** it; his **silence**, where the circumstances made it his duty to speak, may itself be fraudulent (s. 17 Explanation). Her consent is therefore **voidable** (s. 19). The s. 19 Exception (means of discovering the truth with ordinary diligence) does not help the bank: given her **comprehension difficulties**, she could not discover the truth with ordinary diligence.

### 6. Non est factum

If Sakina did not understand that the document was a **guarantee** at all, she may plead *non est factum* — "it is not my deed" (*Foster v. Mackinnon*, (1869) LR 4 CP 704). Under *Saunders v. Anglia Building Society* ([1971] AC 1004), the plea succeeds only if the document was **radically different** from what she believed and she was **not careless**. Given her disability and her son's description of it as a formality, this plea is arguable, and it would make the deed **void**.

### 7. Is the bank affected?

The bank will argue that Aslam's undue influence and misrepresentation are **his** wrongs, not the bank's. But:

1. **Agency / presence.** The bank's representative **was present**, took part in obtaining the signature, and knew the circumstances (s. 17 covers fraud committed "with his connivance, or by his agent").
2. **Put on inquiry.** In *Royal Bank of Scotland v. Etridge (No. 2)* ([2001] UKHL 44) and *Barclays Bank v. O'Brien* ([1994] 1 AC 180), a bank taking a guarantee from a person in a **non-commercial relationship** with the debtor (wife, elderly parent) is **put on inquiry**. It must take reasonable steps to ensure the surety understood the transaction — normally through **independent legal advice**. The bank did nothing of the kind.
3. **Pardanashin / illiterate women rule.** Pakistani courts protect **old, illiterate or dependent women**. The person relying on the deed must prove that it was **explained** to her, that she **understood** it, and that she had **independent advice** (*Farid-un-Nisa v. Mukhtar Ahmad*, (1925) 52 IA 342, PC; *Mst. Janat Bibi v. Sikandar Ali*, PLD 1990 SC 642). Sakina, an elderly, injured village woman, falls squarely within this protection.

The bank therefore **takes subject to** the undue influence and misrepresentation, and cannot enforce the guarantee.

### 8. Remedies

Sakina may **resist** any suit on the guarantee, and may sue for **cancellation** of the deed (s. 39, Specific Relief Act) or a **declaration** that it is void or voidable and has been avoided. Rescission is communicated like a proposal (s. 66). As she received **no benefit**, there is nothing to restore under s. 64 or s. 19A.

### 9. Opinion

Sakina is **not liable** under the guarantee. On the facts, the deed is **void** if she lacked capacity (s. 12) or can establish *non est factum*; and in any case it is **voidable** at her option for **undue influence** (ss. 16, 19A) and **misrepresentation** (ss. 18, 19), which bind the bank because its representative was present and it failed to ensure that she received **independent advice** (*Etridge*; *Farid-un-Nisa*). The burden lies on the bank to prove otherwise (s. 16(3); Art. 127 QSO), which on these facts it cannot discharge.
