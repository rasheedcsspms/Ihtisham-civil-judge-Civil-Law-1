
# Chapter 5 · Agency (ss. 182 to 238)

## 5.1 Meaning

> "182. An '**agent**' is a person employed **to do any act for another** or **to represent another in dealings with third persons**. The person for whom such act is done, or who is so represented, is called the '**principal**'."

The essence of agency is **representation**: the agent brings the principal into **legal relations with third persons**. What the agent does within his authority is, in law, done by the principal.

:::maxim Qui facit per alium facit per se — he who acts through another acts himself
The principal is bound by, and can enforce, the contracts his agent makes within his authority (s. 226). This maxim is the foundation of the whole law of agency.
:::

### Agent distinguished

{w:22,78}
|Relationship|Difference from agency|
|**Servant**|A servant works under the master's **control** as to the manner of work and does not usually make contracts for him; an agent **represents** the principal in dealings with third persons. A person may be both (a salesman).|
|**Independent contractor**|Produces a result by his own methods; does not bind the employer in contracts with others.|
|**Bailee**|Holds goods for a purpose; does not represent the bailor in dealings (though one person may be both — a factor).|
|**Trustee**|Holds legal title for beneficiaries; an agent holds no title and acts in the principal's name.|

## 5.2 Who may employ and who may be an agent

- **s. 183** — "Any person who is of the **age of majority** … and who is of **sound mind**, may employ an agent." A minor cannot appoint an agent (his guardian may act for him).
- **s. 184** — "As between the principal and third persons **any person may become an agent**, but no person who is not of the age of majority and of sound mind can become an agent, **so as to be responsible to his principal**." A minor agent **binds the principal** to third parties, but is **not liable to the principal** for negligence or breach.
- **s. 185** — "**No consideration** is necessary to create an agency." (A gratuitous agent is still bound by the agent's duties.)

## 5.3 Creation of agency

![How an agency is created](agency_creation.png)

### (1) Express agency — s. 187

Authority "given by **words spoken or written**" — e.g., a **power of attorney**, a letter of appointment, an oral instruction. A power of attorney is governed by the **Powers of Attorney Act 1882** and must be properly executed (and, for registered transactions, authenticated under the Registration Act 1908, s. 33).

**Construction of powers of attorney.** Courts construe a power of attorney **strictly**: the attorney can do only what is **expressly** authorised and what is **necessary** to carry it out (s. 188). In particular, an attorney may not **gift** the principal's property, or sell it **to himself or his relatives**, unless clearly authorised — and then only with full disclosure to the principal (s. 215). Pakistani superior courts have repeatedly refused to uphold transfers made by attorneys in their own favour, or in favour of their close relatives, without the principal's informed consent (see, e.g., *Fida Muhammad v. Pir Muhammad Khan*, PLD 1985 SC 341).

### (2) Implied agency — s. 187

Authority "**inferred from the circumstances** of the case; and things spoken or written, or the **ordinary course of dealing**, may be accounted circumstances of the case." *Illustration:* A owns a shop in **Quetta**, lives in **Karachi**, and visits the shop occasionally; B, who manages the shop, habitually orders goods from C in A's name and pays for them out of A's funds with A's knowledge — B has an **implied authority** to order goods from C.

Kinds of implied agency:

1. **From conduct or relationship** — e.g., a wife's implied authority to buy necessaries for the household on her husband's credit (where they live together and he has not forbidden it).
2. **Incidental authority — s. 188.** "An agent having an authority to do an act has authority to do **every lawful thing which is necessary** in order to do such act. An agent having an authority to carry on a business has authority to do every lawful thing necessary for the purpose, or **usually done** in the course of conducting such business." *Illus. (a):* an agent to recover a debt at **Karachi** may adopt any legal process and give a valid discharge; *(b):* an agent to carry on a shipbuilding business may buy timber and hire workmen.
3. **Customary authority** — authority according to the usage of the trade or place (s. 211 — custom of the business).

### (3) Agency by estoppel (holding out) — s. 237

> "237. When an agent has, without authority, done acts or incurred obligations to third persons on behalf of his principal, the principal is bound by such acts or obligations if he has **by his words or conduct induced such third persons to believe** that such acts and obligations were within the scope of the agent's authority."

*Illus. (a):* A consigns goods to B for sale with **secret** instructions not to sell below a fixed price; C, ignorant of them, buys below that price — A is **bound**. *Illus. (b):* B sells negotiable instruments endorsed in blank in violation of A's private orders — the sale is good.

This is **apparent** or **ostensible** authority (*Freeman & Lockyer v. Buckhurst Park Properties*, [1964] 2 QB 480 — a director allowed by the board to act as managing director bound the company to architects he engaged). Its elements: (i) a **representation** by the **principal** (not the agent); (ii) **reliance** by the third party; (iii) **change of position**.

### (4) Agency by necessity — s. 189

> "189. An agent has authority, **in an emergency**, to do all such acts for the purpose of **protecting his principal from loss** as would be done by a person of **ordinary prudence**, in his own case, under similar circumstances."

*Illus. (a):* an agent for sale may have goods **repaired** if necessary. *Illus. (b):* A consigns provisions to B at **Lahore**, directing him to send them immediately to C at **Rawalpindi**; B may **sell** them at Lahore if they will not bear the journey without spoiling.

Conditions (from the cases): (i) a real **emergency**; (ii) it is **impracticable to communicate** with the principal (*Springer v. Great Western Railway*, [1921] 1 KB 257 — a carrier who sold tomatoes without trying to contact the owner was liable); (iii) the agent acts **bona fide** in the principal's interest; (iv) the action is **reasonable and prudent**. Classic example: *Great Northern Railway v. Swaffield* ((1874) LR 9 Ex 132) — a railway which stabled a horse when no one came to collect it could recover the stable charges.

### (5) Agency by ratification — ss. 196 to 200

> "196. Where acts are done by one person on behalf of another, but **without his knowledge or authority**, he may elect to **ratify** or to **disown** such acts. If he ratify them, the same effects will follow **as if they had been performed by his authority**."

**Conditions of valid ratification:**

1. The act was done **on behalf of** the person ratifying — an agent acting for himself cannot be "ratified" (*Keighley, Maxsted & Co. v. Durant*, [1901] AC 240 — an **undisclosed** principal cannot ratify).
2. The principal was **in existence** and **competent** at the time of the act (*Kelner v. Baxter* (1866) LR 2 CP 174 — a company cannot ratify a **pre-incorporation** contract; it must make a new one).
3. Ratification may be **express or implied** (s. 197). *Illus. (a):* B sells to C goods bought for him without authority — implied ratification. *Illus. (b):* B accepts interest on a loan of his money made without authority — ratification.
4. With **full knowledge** — "No valid ratification can be made by a person whose knowledge of the facts of the case is **materially defective**" (s. 198).
5. Of the **whole** transaction — "A person ratifying any unauthorised act done on his behalf ratifies the **whole** of the transaction of which such act formed a part" (s. 199).
6. It must not **injure a third person** — an act which, if authorised, would subject a third person to damages or terminate his rights, **cannot** by ratification be given that effect (s. 200). *Illus. (a):* an unauthorised demand for a chattel cannot be ratified so as to make the holder liable for refusing it. *Illus. (b):* an unauthorised notice to quit cannot be ratified to bind the tenant.
7. Within a **reasonable time**, and of an act the principal **could lawfully** have authorised (a **void** or **illegal** act cannot be ratified).

**Effect — relation back.** Ratification **relates back** to the date of the act (*Bolton Partners v. Lambert* (1889) 41 Ch D 295 — the third party who had made an offer accepted by an unauthorised agent could not withdraw before the principal ratified).

## 5.4 Kinds of agents

1. **General agent** (authority for all business of a kind — e.g., a manager) and **special agent** (for a particular act).
2. **Mercantile agent** — "having in the customary course of business as such agent authority either to sell goods, or to consign goods for the purposes of sale, or to buy goods, or to raise money on the security of goods" (SGA s. 2(9)): **factors**, **brokers**, **commission agents**, **auctioneers**.
3. **Del credere agent** — for an extra commission, he **guarantees** to the principal that the buyers he introduces will **pay** (a combination of agency and guarantee).
4. **Universal agent** — unlimited authority (rare; e.g., a general power of attorney).
5. **Sub-agent** and **substituted agent** (5.5).

## 5.5 Sub-agents and substituted agents — ss. 190 to 195

:::maxim Delegatus non potest delegare — a delegate cannot delegate
An agent is chosen for his personal qualities; he cannot hand over his work to another without authority.
:::

- **s. 190** — "An agent cannot lawfully employ another to perform acts which he has expressly or impliedly undertaken to perform **personally**, unless by the **ordinary custom of trade** a sub-agent may, or, from the **nature of the agency**, a sub-agent must, be employed."
- **s. 191** — a **sub-agent** is "a person employed by, and acting under the control of, the original agent in the business of the agency".
- **s. 192** — where properly appointed: the **principal** is bound by the sub-agent's acts as regards third persons; the **agent** is responsible to the principal for the sub-agent's acts; the **sub-agent** is responsible to the agent, but **not to the principal, except for fraud or wilful wrong**.
- **s. 193** — a sub-agent appointed **without authority**: the agent stands as **principal** to him and is responsible to both principal and third persons; the principal is **not** bound.
- **s. 194** — a **substituted agent**: where the agent, holding authority to **name another person to act for the principal**, names him, that person is **not a sub-agent** but the **principal's own agent** for that part of the business. *Illus. (a):* a solicitor directed to sell by auction and to employ an auctioneer names C — C is A's agent. *Illus. (b):* a merchant in **Chittagong** authorised to recover money instructs a solicitor — the solicitor is A's.
- **s. 195** — in selecting a substituted agent the agent must use the discretion of a man of ordinary prudence; if he does, he is **not responsible** for the substitute's negligence. *Illus. (a):* a ship surveyor of good reputation chooses an unseaworthy ship — the surveyor, not the merchant, is liable.

## 5.6 Duties of the agent to the principal

{w:8,92}
|s.|Duty|
|211|**Follow directions** — or, in their absence, the **custom** of the business at the place; if he departs, he must make good any loss and account for any profit. *Illus. (b):* a broker who sells on credit where the custom is cash, to a buyer who becomes insolvent, must make good the loss.|
|212|**Skill and diligence** — as much skill as is generally possessed by persons in similar business (unless the principal knew of his want of skill); always reasonable diligence; compensation for **direct** (not remote) consequences of neglect. *Illus. (a):* an agent in London who delays remitting money to a merchant in **Islamabad** is liable for the money, interest and direct loss — not for the merchant's later insolvency.|
|213|**Render proper accounts** on demand.|
|214|**Communicate** with the principal in cases of difficulty and seek instructions.|
|215|**Not deal on his own account** without the principal's informed consent (5.7).|
|216|**Hand over secret profits** — the principal may claim any benefit the agent obtained by dealing on his own account. *Illus.:* B tells A the house cannot be bought, buys it himself — A may compel B to sell it to him at cost.|
|218|**Pay over** all sums received for the principal (subject to lawful deductions — s. 217).|
|209|On the principal's **death or insanity**, take reasonable steps to protect the interests entrusted to him.|
|—|**Not to delegate** (s. 190); **not to disclose confidential information**; **not to set up an adverse title** to the principal's property.|

## 5.7 Section 215 — the agent who deals on his own account

> "215. If an agent **deals on his own account in the business of the agency**, without first obtaining the **consent** of his principal and **acquainting him with all material circumstances** which have come to his own knowledge on the subject, the principal may **repudiate** the transaction, if the case shows either that any **material fact has been dishonestly concealed** from him by the agent, or that the dealings of the agent have been **disadvantageous** to him."

*Illus. (a):* A directs B to sell A's estate; B buys it **for himself** in the name of C. A may repudiate if B dishonestly concealed a material fact or the sale was disadvantageous. *Illus. (b):* B, before selling, finds a **mine** on the estate, conceals it, and buys the estate with A's permission — A may **repudiate or adopt** the sale.

**Rationale.** The agent is a **fiduciary**. "No man can serve two masters": as seller for the principal he should get the **highest** price; as buyer for himself he wants the **lowest**. The law removes the temptation.

**Elements for repudiation:**

1. The agent dealt **on his own account** (directly, or through a nominee or relative);
2. in the **business of the agency**;
3. **without** first obtaining the principal's **consent** after disclosing **all material circumstances**; and
4. **either** a material fact was **dishonestly concealed**, **or** the dealing was **disadvantageous** to the principal.

**Remedies:** repudiate (rescind) the transaction (s. 215) **or** affirm it and claim the **profit** (s. 216) — e.g., *Armstrong v. Jackson* ([1917] 2 KB 822): a stockbroker instructed to buy shares sold his **own** shares to the client — the client could rescind and recover the price, even years later.

## 5.8 Rights of the agent

{w:8,92}
|s.|Right|
|217|**Retainer** — retain from sums received in the business all money due to himself for advances, expenses and remuneration.|
|219|**Remuneration** — due on **completion** of the act, unless otherwise agreed; he may detain money received on account of goods sold though not all have been sold.|
|220|**No remuneration for misconducted business** — *Illus. (a):* remuneration for recovering Rs. 1,00,000 and investing Rs. 90,000 well, but none for the Rs. 10,000 badly invested, and he must make good the Rs. 2,000 loss.|
|221|**Lien** — retain the principal's goods, papers and other property (movable or immovable) received by him, until his commission, disbursements and services **in respect of the same** are paid.|
|222|**Indemnity against the consequences of all lawful acts** done in exercise of his authority. *Illus. (a):* B, at **Singapore**, contracts under A's instructions; A fails to send the goods; B, defending the buyer's suit with A's authority, pays damages and costs — A must indemnify.|
|223|**Indemnity for acts done in good faith**, even if they injure third persons. *Illus. (a):* a court officer who seizes goods on a decree-holder's representation and is sued by the true owner must be indemnified by the decree-holder.|
|224|**No indemnity for criminal acts** — *Illus.:* A employs B to beat C, or to publish a libel — no indemnity, even if expressly promised.|
|225|**Compensation for injury** caused by the principal's neglect or want of skill — *Illus.:* a bricklayer hurt by scaffolding badly put up by the employer.|
|205–206|**Compensation** for revocation without sufficient cause of an agency for a fixed period, and for want of reasonable notice.|

## 5.9 Principal and third parties

1. **General rule — s. 226.** Contracts made and acts done by an agent "may be enforced in the same manner, and will have the same legal consequences, as if the contracts had been entered into and the acts done by the principal in person." *Illus. (b):* payment to an agent authorised to receive money discharges the payer.
2. **Excess of authority — ss. 227, 228.** If the authorised part is **separable**, only that part binds the principal (*illus.:* insurance on the ship authorised, on the cargo not — A pays only the ship premium). If **not separable**, the principal may repudiate the **whole** (*illus.:* 500 sheep authorised; 500 sheep and 200 lambs bought for one sum — A may repudiate).
3. **Notice to the agent — s. 229.** Notice or information obtained by the agent **in the course of the business** has the same effect as notice to the principal (*illus. (a)*); not information he obtained **before** the agency (*illus. (b)*).
4. **Agent's fraud or misrepresentation — s. 238.** Misrepresentations and frauds by agents **acting in the course of their business** affect agreements as if made by the principal; not those **outside their authority**. *Illus. (a):* an unauthorised misrepresentation by a sales agent — the contract is **voidable** by C. (The principal is also liable in tort for the agent's fraud in the course of employment — *Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co.*, [1912] AC 716.)

## 5.10 Personal liability of the agent — ss. 230 to 236

> "230. In the absence of any contract to that effect, an agent **cannot personally enforce** contracts entered into by him on behalf of his principal, **nor is he personally bound** by them."

**Presumed contract to the contrary (agent personally liable):**

1. the contract is made by an agent for the sale or purchase of goods for a **merchant resident abroad**;
2. the agent **does not disclose the name** of his principal;
3. the principal, though disclosed, **cannot be sued** (e.g., a foreign sovereign, or a minor).

Other cases of personal liability: the agent **expressly** agrees to be liable; he signs a **negotiable instrument** in his own name without indicating he signs as agent (s. 28, Negotiable Instruments Act); he acts for a principal who **does not exist** (pre-incorporation — *Kelner v. Baxter*); the agent's authority is **coupled with an interest**.

### Undisclosed and unnamed principals

- **s. 231** — where the third party **neither knows nor suspects** that he is dealing with an agent, the **principal may require performance**, but the third party has against the principal **the same rights as against the agent** (e.g., set-off). If the principal discloses himself **before completion**, the third party may **refuse** to fulfil the contract if he can show that he would not have contracted had he known.
- **s. 232** — the principal obtains performance **subject to the rights and obligations** between the agent and the third party. *Illus.:* A, owing B Rs. 500, sells B rice worth Rs. 1,000 as C's undisclosed agent — C cannot compel B to take the rice without allowing the **set-off**.
- **s. 233** — where the agent is **personally liable**, the third party may hold **either** him **or** the principal, **or both**, liable. *Illus.:* A may sue either B (the agent) or C (the principal), or both, for the price of cotton.
- **s. 234** — if the third party **induces** the agent to act on the belief that only the principal will be liable (or vice versa), he **cannot afterwards** hold the other liable (election / estoppel).
- **s. 235** — a **pretended agent** who untruly represents that he is authorised is liable to compensate the third party for loss, if the alleged principal does not ratify (**breach of warranty of authority** — *Collen v. Wright* (1857) 8 E & B 647).
- **s. 236** — a person who contracts as **agent** but is in fact acting **on his own account** cannot require performance.

## 5.11 Termination of agency — ss. 201 to 210

> "201. An agency is terminated by the principal **revoking** his authority; or by the agent **renouncing** the business of the agency; or by the business of the agency being **completed**; or by either the principal or agent **dying** or becoming of **unsound mind**; or by the principal being **adjudicated an insolvent** …"

### Modes

1. **By act of the parties** — revocation by the principal (s. 203; express or implied — s. 207: *A empowers B to let his house, then lets it himself — implied revocation*); renunciation by the agent; mutual agreement.
2. **By operation of law** — completion of the business; expiry of the period; **death** or **insanity** of either; **insolvency** of the principal; destruction of the subject matter; the agency becoming **unlawful** (e.g., the principal becoming an alien enemy).

### Irrevocable agency — agency coupled with interest — s. 202

> "202. Where the agent has himself an **interest in the property** which forms the subject-matter of the agency, the agency cannot, in the absence of an express contract, be terminated to the **prejudice of such interest**."

*Illus. (a):* A authorises B to sell A's land and to **pay himself** out of the proceeds the debts due to him from A — A cannot revoke, nor is it terminated by A's **insanity or death**. *Illus. (b):* A consigns cotton to B, who has made **advances** on it, and asks B to sell and repay himself — irrevocable.

The interest must exist **at the time of creation** of the agency; mere expectation of commission is **not** such an interest.

### Partly exercised authority — ss. 203, 204

The principal may revoke **before** the authority has been exercised so as to bind him (s. 203), but **not after it has been partly exercised**, as regards acts and obligations arising from acts already done (s. 204). *Illus. (a):* B buys cotton in **his own name**, becoming personally liable — A cannot revoke B's authority to pay for it out of A's money. *Illus. (b):* B buys in **A's name** without personal liability — A can revoke B's authority to pay.

### Compensation and notice — ss. 205 to 206

Where the agency is for a **fixed period**, the party who revokes or renounces **without sufficient cause** must compensate the other (s. 205). **Reasonable notice** must be given; otherwise the resulting damage must be made good (s. 206).

### When termination takes effect — ss. 208 to 210

- **s. 208** — termination does not take effect as regards the **agent** before it becomes **known to him**, or as regards **third persons** before it becomes **known to them**. *Illus. (a):* B sells goods after A's letter of revocation is sent but before it reaches him — the sale binds A and B gets his commission. *Illus. (b):* C, who knew of B's original authority (to sell cotton at **Karachi**) but not of its revocation, pays B — the payment is good against A. *Illus. (c):* B pays C after A's death but before hearing of it — good against A's executor.
- **s. 209** — on the principal's **death or insanity**, the agent must take reasonable steps to protect the interests entrusted to him.
- **s. 210** — termination of the agent's authority terminates the authority of all **sub-agents** he appointed.
