
# Chapter 1 · Contract of indemnity (ss. 124, 125)

## 1.1 Meaning

> "124. A contract by which one party promises to **save the other from loss** caused to him by the **conduct of the promisor himself**, or by the **conduct of any other person**, is called a 'contract of indemnity'."

*Illustration:* A contracts to indemnify B against the consequences of any proceedings which C may take against B in respect of a certain sum of 200 rupees. This is a contract of indemnity.

The person who promises to make good the loss is the **indemnifier** (promisor); the person protected is the **indemnity-holder** or **indemnified** (promisee).

:::maxim Indemnity — "to make whole"
The word comes from the Latin *indemnis* — unhurt. The purpose of indemnity is to restore the indemnity-holder to the position he would have been in had the loss not occurred — no more, no less.
:::

### Essentials

1. **Two parties** — the indemnifier and the indemnity-holder.
2. A **promise to save from loss**.
3. The loss is caused by the **conduct of the promisor** or of **any other person**.
4. All the essentials of a valid contract (s. 10) — free consent, competence, lawful object, consideration.
5. It may be **express** or **implied** (e.g., an agent's implied right to indemnity — s. 222; a principal debtor's implied promise to indemnify the surety — s. 145).

## 1.2 The narrow Pakistani definition vs the wider English concept

Section 124 covers only loss caused by **human conduct** — of the promisor or of another person. It does **not**, in terms, cover loss from **accidents** or **natural events** (fire, flood, earthquake) not caused by human conduct. In English law, indemnity is wider: a promise to save another from loss **however caused** (*Adamson v. Jarvis* (1827) 4 Bing 66 — an auctioneer who sold goods on the instructions of a person who was not the owner was held entitled to be indemnified by that person against the true owner's claim).

**Consequence.** Contracts of **insurance** against fire or marine risks (loss not caused by human conduct) fall outside the literal words of s. 124. They are valid nonetheless — as **contingent contracts** (s. 31) and under the **Insurance Ordinance 2000** — and the courts apply to them the **general principles of indemnity** recognised in equity. The definition in s. 124 is therefore **not exhaustive**.

## 1.3 Rights of the indemnity-holder — s. 125

> "125. The promisee in a contract of indemnity, **acting within the scope of his authority**, is entitled to recover from the promisor— (1) all **damages** which he may be compelled to pay in any suit in respect of any matter to which the promise to indemnify applies; (2) all **costs** which he may be compelled to pay in any such suit if, in bringing or defending it, he did not contravene the orders of the promisor, and acted as it would have been **prudent** for him to act in the absence of any contract of indemnity, or if the promisor authorized him to bring or defend the suit; (3) all sums which he may have paid under the terms of any **compromise** of any such suit, if the compromise was not contrary to the orders of the promisor, and was one which it would have been prudent for the promisee to make in the absence of any contract of indemnity, or if the promisor authorized him to compromise the suit."

{w:22,78}
|Right|Condition|
|**Damages** paid in a suit|The suit concerns a matter covered by the indemnity|
|**Costs** of the suit|He obeyed the promisor's orders and acted **prudently**, or was authorised to bring / defend the suit|
|**Compromise** sums|The compromise was not against the promisor's orders and was **prudent**, or was authorised|

### When can the indemnity-holder sue — before or after paying?

The old English rule was "**indemnify** means **reimburse**" — the holder had to pay first. Equity took a more practical view.

:::case Gajanan Moreshwar Parelkar v. Moreshwar Madan Mantri (AIR 1942 Bom 302)
__Facts.__ The plaintiff, at the defendant's request, mortgaged his own leasehold property to secure a debt for which the defendant was responsible, and the defendant promised to indemnify him. Before the plaintiff had paid anything, he sued the defendant to compel him to pay off the mortgage.

__Held.__ (Chagla J) "If his liability has become **absolute**, he is entitled either to call upon the indemnifier to save him from that liability and pay it off, or to pay it off and sue the indemnifier." An indemnity would be of little value if the holder had to pay first and then sue.

__Ratio.__ The indemnity-holder may sue **as soon as his liability becomes absolute**, even before he has actually paid.
:::

## 1.4 Rights of the indemnifier

The Act is silent, but by analogy with the surety's rights (s. 141) and the doctrine of **subrogation**, an indemnifier who has paid **steps into the shoes** of the indemnity-holder and may enforce the latter's rights against the person who caused the loss. (In insurance, this is the insurer's right of subrogation.)

## 1.5 Indemnity and guarantee distinguished

{w:22,39,39}
|Point|Indemnity (s. 124)|Guarantee (s. 126)|
|Parties|**Two** — indemnifier and indemnity-holder|**Three** — creditor, principal debtor, surety|
|Number of contracts|One contract|Three contracts (creditor–debtor, creditor–surety, debtor–surety implied)|
|Nature of liability|**Primary** and independent|**Secondary** — arises only on the principal debtor's **default**|
|Purpose|To save from **loss**|To secure a **debt or performance** of a third person|
|Existing debt|No existing debt needed|Presupposes a debt or liability of the principal debtor|
|Request|The indemnifier acts on his own account|The surety usually acts at the **request** of the principal debtor|
|After payment|Indemnifier may enforce the holder's rights against the wrongdoer (subrogation)|Surety steps into the creditor's shoes against the principal debtor (s. 140)|

:::eg Indemnity or guarantee?
Two friends go to a shop. A says to the shopkeeper: "Let B have the goods; **I will see you paid**." This is an **indemnity** — A's liability is primary. If A says: "Let B have the goods; **if he does not pay you, I will**" — this is a **guarantee** — A's liability arises only on B's default (*Birkmyr v. Darnell* (1704) 1 Salk 27).
:::

:::recap Chapter 1 in five lines
- Indemnity: a promise to save another from loss caused by the promisor or any other person (s. 124).
- s. 124 is narrower than English law (*Adamson v. Jarvis*) — loss by accident is outside its words; insurance is valid as a contingent contract and under general indemnity principles.
- Holder's rights: damages, prudent costs, prudent compromises (s. 125).
- He may sue once his liability becomes absolute, before payment (*Gajanan Moreshwar*).
- Indemnity: two parties, primary liability; guarantee: three parties, secondary liability (*Birkmyr v. Darnell*).
:::

# Chapter 2 · Contract of guarantee (ss. 126 to 147)

## 2.1 Meaning

> "126. A 'contract of guarantee' is a contract to **perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in case of his default**. The person who gives the guarantee is called the '**surety**': the person in respect of whose default the guarantee is given is called the '**principal debtor**', and the person to whom the guarantee is given is called the '**creditor**'. A guarantee may be either **oral or written**."

![The triangle of a contract of guarantee](guarantee_triangle.png)

### The three contracts

1. **Creditor and principal debtor** — the main contract (loan, sale on credit, employment).
2. **Creditor and surety** — the surety's promise to pay or perform on default.
3. **Principal debtor and surety** — an **implied** promise by the principal debtor to **indemnify** the surety (s. 145).

## 2.2 Essentials of a valid guarantee

1. **Three parties** — creditor, principal debtor and surety, each with distinct roles.
2. **An existing or future liability** of the principal debtor — the guarantee is **collateral** to a primary obligation. If there is no principal debt at all, there is nothing to guarantee.
3. **Consideration** — s. 127: "Anything done, or any promise made, **for the benefit of the principal debtor** may be a sufficient consideration to the surety for giving the guarantee." The surety need not himself receive any benefit.
4. **Consent** of all — and the surety's consent must be **free**: a guarantee obtained by the creditor's **misrepresentation** (s. 142) or **concealment** of material facts (s. 143) is invalid.
5. **Competence** of the surety and creditor (ss. 11–12).
6. **Form** — oral or written (s. 126). But as a guarantee is an instrument of **financial obligation**, if reduced to writing it **should be attested by two men, or one man and two women** (Art. 17(2)(a), Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984), and attesting witnesses are needed to prove it (Art. 79). Banks always take written, stamped guarantees.
7. **Condition precedent** — if the surety agreed that the creditor should not act on the guarantee **until a co-surety joins**, and he does not join, the guarantee is **not valid** (s. 144).

### Consideration — the illustrations to s. 127

- (a) B asks A to sell goods to him on credit; A agrees **provided C guarantees** payment; C promises **in consideration of A's promise to deliver** — **sufficient**.
- (b) A has already sold goods to B; C asks A to **forbear to sue** B for a year and promises to pay if B defaults; A agrees — **sufficient** (forbearance at the surety's request).
- (c) A has already sold goods to B; C afterwards, **without consideration**, agrees to pay if B defaults — **void**. (Past consideration that was not for the benefit of the principal debtor at the surety's request does not support a later guarantee.)

## 2.3 Kinds of guarantee

1. **Specific (simple) guarantee** — for a **single** debt or transaction; ends when that debt is paid.
2. **Continuing guarantee** — "a guarantee which extends to a **series of transactions**" (s. 129).
3. **Prospective** (future debts) and **retrospective** (existing debts) guarantees.
4. **Fidelity guarantee** — for the honest conduct of an employee (illustrations to s. 133).
5. **Bank guarantee / performance guarantee** — a bank's promise to pay a beneficiary on demand (e.g., bid bonds and performance bonds in public works).

### Continuing guarantee — the illustrations to s. 129

- (a) A promises B to be responsible, up to Rs. 5,000, for the due collection and payment by C of the rents of B's zamindari — **continuing**.
- (b) A guarantees payment to a tea-dealer, up to £100, "for any tea he may **from time to time** supply to C" — **continuing**; A is liable up to £100 for a later unpaid supply of £200.
- (c) A guarantees the price of **five sacks of flour** to be delivered and paid for in a month — **not continuing**; A is not liable for a later unpaid delivery of four sacks.

### Revocation of a continuing guarantee

1. **By notice** — "A continuing guarantee may at any time be revoked by the surety, as to **future transactions**, by notice to the creditor" (s. 130). The surety remains liable for transactions **already entered into** (illus. (a): Rs. 2,000 of bills already discounted; illus. (b): a bill already accepted before the notice).
2. **By the surety's death** — "operates, in the absence of any contract to the contrary, as a revocation … so far as regards future transactions" (s. 131). His estate remains liable for past transactions.
3. **By the modes that discharge any surety** (ss. 133–139 — below).

## 2.4 Nature and extent of the surety's liability — s. 128

> "128. The liability of the surety is **co-extensive** with that of the principal debtor, unless it is otherwise provided by the contract."

*Illustration:* A guarantees to B the payment of a bill of exchange by C, the acceptor; the bill is dishonoured — A is liable not only for the amount of the bill but also for **interest and charges**.

### What "co-extensive" means

1. **Not more** than the principal debtor's liability — the surety is liable for no more than the debtor owes (subject to any limit in the guarantee).
2. **Not less** (unless limited) — the surety is liable for the whole debt, with interest and charges.
3. **Limit by contract** — the guarantee may cap the amount ("up to Rs. 5 million") or the period.
4. **Immediate** — the surety's liability arises **as soon as the principal debtor defaults**. The creditor need **not** first sue the principal debtor or exhaust his remedies against him.

:::case Bank of Bihar Ltd. v. Damodar Prasad (AIR 1969 SC 297)
__Facts.__ A bank lent money to a borrower on the guarantee of a surety. On default, the trial court passed a decree against both but directed that the bank should first proceed against the principal debtor and only then against the surety.

__Held.__ The direction was wrong. "The very object of the guarantee is defeated if the creditor is asked to postpone his remedies against the surety." The surety's liability is **immediate**; it is not deferred until the creditor exhausts his remedies against the principal debtor.
:::

### Where the principal debtor is not liable

1. **Principal debtor a minor.** The minor's debt is void (*Mohori Bibee*). The authorities are divided. An older Bombay view (*Kashiba v. Shripat Narshiv* (1894) ILR 19 Bom 697) held the surety not liable because there was no valid principal debt; the better view is that, where the surety knew of the incapacity, his promise is construed as a **promise to indemnify** (a primary liability), so that he is liable as a **principal debtor**.
2. **Principal debt void or illegal** — no liability on the surety (except as above).
3. **Discharge of the principal debtor by operation of law** (e.g., insolvency discharge) — does **not** discharge the surety, because it is not the creditor's act (contrast s. 134).
4. **Debt time-barred against the principal debtor** — the surety's liability is a separate contract; limitation runs separately against each (a suit against the surety may still be in time).

### Two persons primarily liable — s. 132

Where two persons contract with a third to undertake a liability, and agree between themselves that one shall be liable only on the default of the other, the third person's rights against **each** are not affected — even if he knew of the internal arrangement. *Illustration:* A and B make a **joint and several promissory note** to C; A is in fact surety for B, and C knows it — this is no answer to C's suit against A.

### Bank guarantees and performance bonds

A bank guarantee payable "**on demand**" is an **independent** contract between the bank and the beneficiary. The bank must pay on a demand made in terms of the guarantee, irrespective of disputes between the beneficiary and the contractor. Courts restrain encashment only in exceptional cases — **established fraud** of which the bank has notice, or **irretrievable injustice** (*U.P. State Sugar Corporation v. Sumac International*, (1997) 1 SCC 568). Pakistani courts follow the same approach (e.g., *National Construction Ltd. v. Aiwan-e-Iqbal Authority*, PLD 1994 SC 311).

## 2.5 Discharge of the surety

![How a surety is discharged](surety_discharge.png)

### (1) By revocation — ss. 130, 131

Notice (as to future transactions) and death (as to future transactions) — for **continuing** guarantees only.

### (2) By variance in the terms of the contract — s. 133

> "133. Any **variance**, made **without the surety's consent**, in the terms of the contract between the principal debtor and the creditor, **discharges the surety as to transactions subsequent to the variance**."

**Rationale.** The surety agreed to guarantee a **particular** contract. The creditor and debtor cannot, behind his back, change the risk he took.

*Illustrations:*
- (a) A is surety for B as a **bank manager**; B's salary is raised and he is made liable for one-fourth of losses on overdrafts — A is **discharged** from later losses.
- (b) A guarantees B's conduct in an office whose duties are defined by an Act; a later Act **materially alters** the office — A is discharged as to the future.
- (c) B, a clerk on a fixed salary, is moved to **commission** — A is not liable for B's later misconduct.
- (d) A's continuing guarantee for oil supplied on **credit**; B and C switch to **cash** supplies with payments applied to old debts — A is not liable for later supplies.
- (e) C contracts to lend B Rs. 5,000 on **1 March**; C pays on **1 January** — A is discharged (C could now sue B before 1 March).

A variance that is **not material** and does **not prejudice** the surety, or one made **with** his consent, does not discharge him.

### (3) By release or discharge of the principal debtor — s. 134

> "134. The surety is discharged by any **contract between the creditor and the principal debtor, by which the principal debtor is released**, or by any **act or omission of the creditor**, the legal consequence of which is the discharge of the principal debtor."

*Illustrations:* (a) B makes a composition with his creditors (including C), assigning his property in return for release — A, the surety, is **discharged**; (b) B diverts a stream needed to grow the indigo A was to deliver — C, the surety for A, is **discharged**; (c) B fails to supply the timber needed for A to build — C, A's surety, is **discharged**.

### (4) By composition, giving time, or promise not to sue — ss. 135–137

> "135. A contract between the creditor and the principal debtor, by which the creditor makes a **composition** with, or **promises to give time** to, or **not to sue**, the principal debtor discharges the surety, **unless the surety assents** to such contract."

But:

- **s. 136** — a contract to give time made with a **third person**, not with the principal debtor, does **not** discharge the surety (*illus.:* C, holder of an overdue bill, contracts with M to give time to B — A, the surety, is not discharged).
- **s. 137** — "**Mere forbearance** on the part of the creditor to sue the principal debtor or to enforce any other remedy against him does not, in the absence of any provision in the guarantee to the contrary, discharge the surety." *Illus.:* C does not sue B for a year after the debt became payable — A is **not** discharged.

The difference: a **binding promise** to give time (s. 135) ties the creditor's hands and prevents the surety from paying and suing the debtor at once; mere **inaction** (s. 137) does not.

### (5) Release of one co-surety — s. 138

"Where there are co-sureties, a release by the creditor of one of them does **not discharge the others**; neither does it free the surety so released from his responsibility to the other sureties."

### (6) By the creditor's act or omission impairing the surety's eventual remedy — s. 139

> "139. If the creditor does any act which is **inconsistent with the rights of the surety**, or omits to do any act which his **duty to the surety** requires him to do, and the **eventual remedy of the surety** himself against the principal debtor is thereby **impaired**, the surety is discharged."

*Illustrations:*
- (a) C **prepays** the last two instalments to B, a shipbuilder, without A's knowledge — A, B's surety, is discharged.
- (b) C sells B's furniture held as security, but through **misconduct and wilful negligence** realises only a small price — A is discharged.
- (c) B promised to see once a month that the apprentice M made up the cash; he **omits** to do so, and M embezzles — A, the surety for M's fidelity, is not liable.

### (7) By loss of securities — s. 141

If the creditor **loses**, or **without the surety's consent parts with**, a security held at the time of the guarantee, "the surety is **discharged to the extent of the value** of the security" (s. 141; see 2.6).

### (8) By invalidity of the guarantee — ss. 142–144

- **s. 142** — a guarantee obtained by **misrepresentation** made by the creditor, or with his knowledge and assent, concerning a **material part** of the transaction, is **invalid**.
- **s. 143** — a guarantee obtained by the creditor "by means of **keeping silence** as to material circumstances" is **invalid**. *Illus. (a):* A takes C's guarantee for clerk B's accounts without telling C that B had **already failed to account** — invalid. *Illus. (b):* B and C secretly agree that B will pay Rs. 5 per ton above market price to liquidate an old debt — concealed from the surety A — A is not liable.
- **s. 144** — the co-surety who was to join does not join — **not valid**.

**Rationale of s. 143.** A guarantee is not a contract *uberrimae fidei* in the full sense (unlike insurance), but the creditor must disclose **unusual** facts about the principal debtor's past conduct or the transaction that the surety would naturally expect not to exist.

## 2.6 Rights of the surety

### (A) Against the principal debtor

1. **Subrogation — s. 140.** "Where a guaranteed debt has become due, or default of the principal debtor to perform a guaranteed duty has taken place, the surety, upon **payment or performance of all that he is liable for**, is **invested with all the rights which the creditor had** against the principal debtor." He steps into the creditor's shoes — including securities and priorities.
2. **Indemnity — s. 145.** "In every contract of guarantee there is an **implied promise by the principal debtor to indemnify the surety**; and the surety is entitled to recover from the principal debtor whatever sum he has **rightfully paid** under the guarantee, but no sums which he has paid wrongfully." *Illus. (a):* costs of a reasonable defence — recoverable; *(b):* costs of an unreasonable defence — not recoverable; *(c):* a surety who pays Rs. 2,000 when only a smaller amount of rice was supplied can recover from B only the price of the rice actually supplied.
3. **Quia timet relief** (in equity) — once the debt is due, the surety may ask the court to compel the principal debtor to pay the creditor, so as to relieve himself.

### (B) Against the creditor

1. **Benefit of securities — s. 141.** "A surety is entitled to the benefit of **every security which the creditor has** against the principal debtor **at the time when the contract of suretyship is entered into**, whether the surety knows of the existence of such security or not; and, if the creditor **loses**, or, **without the consent of the surety, parts with** such security, the surety is discharged to the extent of the value of the security." *Illus. (a):* C cancels a mortgage of B's furniture — A discharged to the value of the furniture; *(b):* C withdraws execution against B's goods — A discharged; *(c):* a **further** security taken **after** the guarantee is given up — A **not** discharged (it was not held at the time of the guarantee).
2. **Set-off** — the surety may set off any debt the creditor owes to the **principal debtor** (where the debtor could have) or to himself.
3. **The discharges of ss. 133–139** operate as defences.

### (C) Against co-sureties — ss. 146, 147

- **s. 146** — co-sureties for the same debt, "either jointly or severally, and whether under the same or different contracts, and whether with or without the knowledge of each other", must, in the absence of contrary contract, **contribute equally**. *Illus. (a):* A, B and C, sureties for Rs. 3,000 — Rs. 1,000 each; *(b):* if they agreed one-quarter, one-quarter and one-half — Rs. 250, Rs. 250 and Rs. 500.
- **s. 147** — co-sureties bound in **different sums** contribute **equally so far as the limits of their respective obligations permit**. *Illus.:* A Rs. 10,000, B Rs. 20,000, C Rs. 40,000 — on a default of **Rs. 30,000**, each pays **Rs. 10,000**; on **Rs. 40,000**, A pays 10,000 and B and C 15,000 each; on **Rs. 70,000**, each pays his full limit.

:::recap Chapter 2 in nine lines
- Guarantee: perform the promise / discharge the liability of a third person on default (s. 126); three parties, three contracts; oral or written (attestation — Art. 17 QSO).
- Consideration: anything done for the benefit of the principal debtor (s. 127; illus. (a)–(c)).
- Kinds: specific, continuing (s. 129); revocation by notice (s. 130) or death (s. 131) as to future transactions.
- Liability co-extensive (s. 128) and immediate — no need to sue debtor first (*Bank of Bihar v. Damodar Prasad*); s. 132 joint promisors.
- Bank guarantees independent; injunction only for fraud / irretrievable injustice.
- Discharge: variance (s. 133), release (s. 134), composition / time / not to sue (s. 135; not s. 136 or mere forbearance s. 137), creditor impairing remedy (s. 139), loss of security (s. 141); co-surety release does not discharge (s. 138).
- Invalid guarantees: misrepresentation (s. 142), concealment (s. 143), co-surety not joining (s. 144).
- Surety's rights: subrogation (s. 140), indemnity (s. 145), securities (s. 141), set-off, quia timet.
- Co-sureties contribute equally, within their limits (ss. 146–147).
:::
