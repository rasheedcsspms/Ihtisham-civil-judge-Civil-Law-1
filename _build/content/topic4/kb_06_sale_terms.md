
# Chapter 6 · The contract of sale of goods (SGA ss. 1 to 10)

## 6.1 Background and relation to the Contract Act

Sections 76–123 of the Contract Act once dealt with sale of goods. They were **repealed** and replaced by the **Sale of Goods Act 1930** (modelled on the English Sale of Goods Act 1893). The SGA "extends to the whole of Pakistan" (s. 1(2)). By **s. 3**, "the unrepealed provisions of the Contract Act, 1872, save in so far as they are inconsistent with the express provisions of this Act, shall continue to apply to contracts for the sale of goods" — so offer, acceptance, capacity, free consent, consideration and the rules on damages (ss. 73–74) still apply.

## 6.2 Key definitions — s. 2

{w:26,74}
|Term|Definition (s. 2)|
|**Buyer / seller**|A person who buys or agrees to buy / sells or agrees to sell goods (s. 2(1), (13)).|
|**Delivery**|"**voluntary transfer of possession** from one person to another" (s. 2(2)).|
|**Deliverable state**|Such a state that the buyer would under the contract be **bound to take delivery** (s. 2(3)).|
|**Document of title**|Bill of lading, dock-warrant, warehouse-keeper's or wharfinger's certificate, railway receipt, delivery order, and any document used in the ordinary course of business as proof of possession or control of goods (s. 2(4)).|
|**Future goods**|Goods to be manufactured, produced or acquired by the seller **after** the contract (s. 2(6)).|
|**Goods**|"every kind of **moveable property** other than **actionable claims and money**; and includes **electricity, water, gas**, stock and shares, growing crops, grass and things attached to or forming part of the land which are agreed to be severed before sale or under the contract of sale" (s. 2(7)).|
|**Insolvent**|A person who has ceased to pay his debts in the ordinary course of business, or cannot pay them as they fall due (s. 2(8)).|
|**Mercantile agent**|An agent having in the customary course of business authority to sell, consign for sale, buy, or raise money on the security of goods (s. 2(9)).|
|**Price**|"the **money consideration** for a sale of goods" (s. 2(10)).|
|**Property**|"the **general property** in goods, and not merely a special property" — i.e., **ownership** (s. 2(11)).|
|**Specific goods**|Goods **identified and agreed upon** at the time the contract is made (s. 2(14)).|

## 6.3 Sale and agreement to sell — s. 4

> "4. (1) A contract of sale of goods is a contract whereby the seller **transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods** to the buyer **for a price**. There may be a contract of sale between one part-owner and another.
> (2) A contract of sale may be absolute or conditional.
> (3) Where under a contract of sale the property in the goods is **transferred** from the seller to the buyer, the contract is called a **sale**, but where the transfer of the property in the goods is to take place at a **future time** or subject to some **condition** thereafter to be fulfilled, the contract is called an **agreement to sell**.
> (4) An agreement to sell becomes a sale when the time elapses or the conditions are fulfilled subject to which the property in the goods is to be transferred."

### Essentials of a contract of sale

1. **Two parties** — a buyer and a seller (one part-owner may sell to another).
2. **Goods** — movable property within s. 2(7).
3. **Price** — money consideration. Exchange of goods for goods is **barter**, not sale (though part money and part goods may be sale).
4. **Transfer or agreement to transfer** of the **general property** (ownership).
5. All the **essentials of a valid contract** (s. 3; Contract Act s. 10).

![Sale and agreement to sell compared](sale_vs_ats.png)

{w:22,39,39}
|Point|Sale|Agreement to sell|
|Property|Passes **at once** to the buyer|Passes at a **future time** or on a **condition**|
|Nature|An **executed** contract; a conveyance; creates a right **in rem**|An **executory** contract; creates a right **in personam**|
|Risk (s. 26)|With the **buyer** (risk follows property)|With the **seller**|
|Seller's remedy on buyer's default|Suit for the **price** (s. 55)|Suit for **damages** for non-acceptance (s. 56), unless the price is payable on a fixed day (s. 55(2))|
|Buyer's remedy on seller's default|Ownership already his — may also claim the goods themselves (e.g., suit for possession; specific delivery)|Suit for **damages** for non-delivery (s. 57); specific performance in suitable cases (s. 58)|
|Seller's insolvency|Buyer, being owner, can claim the goods from the official receiver|Buyer is only a creditor for damages / refund|
|Buyer's insolvency before payment|Seller may still have lien / stoppage (ss. 46–50); otherwise must prove in insolvency|Seller may withhold the goods (s. 46(2))|
|Resale by seller|Buyer may follow the goods (subject to s. 30(1))|Seller has the property; the second buyer gets title, the first buyer claims damages|
|Goods perish|Loss falls on the **buyer**|The agreement is **avoided** (s. 8)|

### Sale distinguished from similar transactions

{w:24,76}
|Transaction|Difference|
|**Barter / exchange**|Goods for goods; no money price.|
|**Gift**|No price.|
|**Bailment**|Possession only, for a purpose, with return of the **same** goods; ownership stays with the bailor.|
|**Hire-purchase**|The hirer has an **option** to buy; he is not bound to buy, so he has not "**bought or agreed to buy**" (*Helby v. Matthews*, [1895] AC 471 — a hirer who pledged a piano did not pass title to the pawnbroker). Property passes only on payment of the last instalment and exercise of the option.|
|**Contract for work and labour**|The substance is **skill and labour**, with materials ancillary (*Robinson v. Graves*, [1935] 1 KB 579 — an order for a portrait was for work and labour, not sale of goods). Building contracts, repair contracts.|
|**Mortgage, pledge, charge**|A transaction "in the form of a contract of sale" intended to operate by way of **security** is **outside** the SGA (s. 66(3)).|

## 6.4 Formalities — s. 5

> "5. (1) A contract of sale is made by an **offer to buy or sell** goods for a price and the **acceptance** of such offer. The contract may provide for the immediate delivery of the goods or immediate payment of the price or both, or for the delivery or payment by instalments, or that the delivery or payment or both shall be postponed.
> (2) Subject to the provisions of any law for the time being in force, a contract of sale may be made **in writing or by word of mouth**, or partly in writing and partly by word of mouth or may be **implied from the conduct** of the parties."

No special form is required (subject to other laws — e.g., stamp duty on documents; Art. 17 QSO for instruments of financial obligation).

## 6.5 Subject-matter — ss. 6 to 8

1. **Existing goods** — owned or possessed by the seller: **specific**, **ascertained** (identified after the contract) or **unascertained** (generic — "100 bags of DAP fertiliser").
2. **Future goods** — to be manufactured, produced or acquired after the contract (s. 6(1)). "Where by a contract of sale the seller purports to effect a **present sale of future goods**, the contract operates as an **agreement to sell**" (s. 6(3)).
3. **Contingent goods** — goods "the acquisition of which by the seller depends upon a **contingency** which may or may not happen" (s. 6(2)) — e.g., "the cotton I expect to receive from my farm in Sahiwal".

### Perishing of goods

- **s. 7 — before the contract.** "Where there is a contract for the sale of **specific goods**, the contract is **void** if the goods **without the knowledge of the seller** have, at the time when the contract was made, **perished** or become so **damaged** as no longer to answer to their description in the contract." (*Couturier v. Hastie* (1856) 5 HLC 673; Contract Act s. 20.)
- **s. 8 — after an agreement to sell, before risk passes.** "Where there is an **agreement to sell specific goods**, and subsequently the goods **without any fault** on the part of the seller or buyer perish or become so damaged as no longer to answer to their description in the agreement **before the risk passes** to the buyer, the agreement is thereby **avoided**."

If the goods perish **after** property (and risk) has passed, the loss falls on the **buyer** (s. 26; *res perit domino* — the thing perishes for its owner).

## 6.6 Price — ss. 9, 10

- **s. 9** — the price may be (1) **fixed by the contract**, (2) left to be fixed in a **manner agreed**, or (3) determined by the **course of dealing**. Otherwise "the buyer shall pay the seller a **reasonable price**", which is a question of fact.
- **s. 10** — agreement to sell **at the valuation of a third party**: if the third party **cannot or does not** value, the agreement is **avoided** — but if the goods have been delivered to and appropriated by the buyer, he must pay a **reasonable price**. If the valuer is **prevented** by the fault of either party, the other may sue for **damages**.

:::recap Chapter 6 in six lines
- SGA 1930 replaced Contract Act ss. 76–123; the Contract Act applies where not inconsistent (s. 3).
- Goods: movables except actionable claims and money; includes electricity, water, gas (Pakistan), shares, crops (s. 2(7)).
- Sale (property passes now) vs agreement to sell (future / conditional) — risk, remedies, insolvency (s. 4).
- Not sale: barter, gift, bailment, hire-purchase (*Helby v. Matthews*), work and labour (*Robinson v. Graves*), security transactions (s. 66(3)).
- No form required (s. 5); existing, future, contingent goods (s. 6); perishing before contract — void (s. 7); before risk passes — avoided (s. 8).
- Price fixed, by agreed manner, by dealing, or reasonable (s. 9); third-party valuation (s. 10).
:::

# Chapter 7 · Conditions, warranties and caveat emptor (SGA ss. 11 to 17, 62)

## 7.1 Stipulations as to time — s. 11

"Unless a different intention appears from the terms of the contract, stipulations as to **time of payment** are **not** deemed to be of the essence of a contract of sale. Whether any other stipulation as to time is of the essence of the contract or not depends on the **terms of the contract**." In commercial sales, time of **delivery** is usually of the essence (Topic 3, Chapter 13; *Bunge v. Tradax*).

## 7.2 Condition and warranty — s. 12

> "12. (1) A stipulation in a contract of sale with reference to goods which are the subject thereof may be a **condition or a warranty**.
> (2) A **condition** is a stipulation **essential to the main purpose** of the contract, the breach of which gives rise to a right to **treat the contract as repudiated**.
> (3) A **warranty** is a stipulation **collateral to the main purpose** of the contract, the breach of which gives rise to a claim for **damages** but not to a right to reject the goods and treat the contract as repudiated.
> (4) Whether a stipulation in a contract of sale is a condition or a warranty depends in each case on the **construction of the contract**. A stipulation may be a condition, though called a warranty in the contract."

{w:22,39,39}
|Point|Condition|Warranty|
|Importance|Essential to the main purpose|Collateral / subsidiary|
|Remedy|Reject the goods, treat the contract as repudiated, **and** claim damages|Damages only (or set-off against the price — s. 59)|
|Conversion|May be treated as a warranty (s. 13)|A warranty cannot become a condition|
|Example|Car sold as a "2023 model" is a 2015 model|A promise that the car will be serviced free for a year|

## 7.3 When a condition is treated as a warranty — s. 13

1. **By the buyer's choice — s. 13(1).** The buyer may **waive** the condition, or **elect** to treat the breach as a breach of warranty (keep the goods and claim damages).
2. **By compulsion — s. 13(2).** "Where a contract of sale is **not severable** and the buyer has **accepted** the goods or part thereof, the breach of any condition to be fulfilled by the seller can **only be treated as a breach of warranty** … unless there is a term of the contract, express or implied, to that effect." (The Indian words "or where the contract is for specific goods, the property in which has passed to the buyer" were **omitted in Pakistan in 1962** — so in Pakistan the mere passing of property in specific goods does **not** bar rejection; only **acceptance** does.)
3. **Impossibility — s. 13(3).** The section does not affect conditions or warranties whose fulfilment is excused by law (impossibility).

**Acceptance** is defined in s. 42 (Chapter 10): intimation of acceptance, an act inconsistent with the seller's ownership, or retaining the goods beyond a reasonable time without rejecting them — but only after a **reasonable opportunity to examine** (s. 41).

## 7.4 Implied conditions and warranties

The SGA **implies** certain terms into every sale, unless excluded (s. 62).

![Implied conditions and warranties under the Sale of Goods Act](implied_terms.png)

### (1) Title — s. 14

> "14. In a contract of sale, unless the circumstances of the contract are such as to show a different intention there is— (a) an **implied condition** on the part of the seller that, in the case of a sale, he has a **right to sell** the goods and that, in the case of an agreement to sell, he will have a right to sell the goods at the time when the property is to pass; (b) an **implied warranty** that the buyer shall have and enjoy **quiet possession** of the goods; (c) an **implied warranty** that the goods shall be **free from any charge or encumbrance** in favour of any third party not declared or known to the buyer before or at the time when the contract is made."

:::case Rowland v. Divall ([1923] 2 KB 500)
__Facts.__ Rowland bought a car from Divall and used it for **four months**. It turned out to be **stolen**; the police took it back for the true owner.

__Held.__ There was a breach of the **condition as to title**. Rowland could recover the **whole price**, without any deduction for the four months' use: there was a **total failure of consideration**, because he had paid for **ownership** and got none.
:::

:::case Niblett Ltd. v. Confectioners' Materials Co. ([1921] 3 KB 387)
__Held.__ Tins of condensed milk labelled "Nissly" infringed the trade mark of Nestlé, so the buyers could not sell them without being restrained. The seller had **no "right to sell"** within the title condition, though he owned the tins.
:::

### (2) Sale by description — s. 15

> "15. Where there is a contract for the sale of goods **by description**, there is an **implied condition** that the goods shall **correspond with the description**; and, if the sale is by **sample as well as by description** it is not sufficient that the bulk of the goods corresponds with the sample if the goods do not also correspond with the description."

- *Varley v. Whipp* ([1900] 1 QB 513) — a second-hand reaping machine described as "new the previous year and only used to cut 50 or 60 acres" was in fact very old — the buyer could reject.
- *Beale v. Taylor* ([1967] 1 WLR 1193) — a car advertised as a "**1200, white, 1961**" was in fact two halves welded together — breach of description, even though the buyer had seen it.
- *Re Moore & Co. and Landauer & Co.* ([1921] 2 KB 519) — tinned fruit to be packed in cases of **30 tins** delivered partly in cases of **24** — breach of description; the buyer could reject, though the total quantity and value were correct.

### (3) Quality and fitness — s. 16 (and caveat emptor)

> "16. Subject to the provisions of this Act and of any other law for the time being in force, there is **no implied warranty or condition as to the quality or fitness** for any particular purpose of goods supplied under a contract of sale, **except as follows**—
> (1) Where the buyer, expressly or by implication, **makes known to the seller the particular purpose** for which the goods are required, so as to show that the buyer **relies on the seller's skill or judgment**, and the goods are of a description which it is **in the course of the seller's business to supply** (whether he is the manufacturer or producer or not), there is an implied condition that the goods shall be **reasonably fit for such purpose**: Provided that, in the case of a contract for the sale of a specified article under its **patent or other trade name**, there is no implied condition as to its fitness for any particular purpose.
> (2) Where goods are bought **by description** from a seller who **deals in goods of that description** (whether he is the manufacturer or producer or not), there is an implied condition that the goods shall be of **merchantable quality**: Provided that, if the buyer has **examined** the goods, there shall be no implied condition as regards defects which such examination **ought to have revealed**.
> (3) An implied warranty or condition as to quality or fitness for a particular purpose may be annexed by the **usage of trade**.
> (4) An express warranty or condition does not negative a warranty or condition implied by this Act unless inconsistent therewith."

:::maxim Caveat emptor — let the buyer beware
The opening words of s. 16 state the rule: there is **no** implied condition as to quality or fitness. The buyer must examine the goods and satisfy himself; if he buys unsuitable goods, he cannot blame the seller. The rest of s. 16 — and, in Pakistan, **s. 16A** — are the **exceptions**.
:::

**Fitness for purpose — s. 16(1).** Conditions: (i) the buyer makes known the **particular purpose** (for goods with only one obvious purpose — milk, a hot-water bottle — the purpose is made known **by implication**); (ii) he **relies** on the seller's skill or judgment; (iii) the goods are of a kind the seller **deals in**.

:::case Priest v. Last ([1903] 2 KB 148)
__Facts.__ A man bought a **hot-water bottle** from a chemist, asking whether it would stand boiling water. It burst after five days and scalded his wife.

__Held.__ A hot-water bottle has only one purpose, which was therefore made known by implication; the buyer relied on the seller; the seller dealt in such goods. There was a breach of the condition of **fitness for purpose**.
:::

:::case Grant v. Australian Knitting Mills Ltd. ([1936] AC 85, Privy Council)
__Facts.__ Dr Grant bought **woollen underwear** which contained an excess of **sulphites**, a hidden defect. He developed severe dermatitis.

__Held.__ There was a breach of the conditions of **fitness for purpose** (the purpose — to be worn next to the skin — was implied) and of **merchantable quality** (the defect was latent and could not be discovered by ordinary examination). "Merchantable" means that the goods are in such a state that a buyer, fully acquainted with the facts, would buy them **without abatement of price** and without special terms.
:::

:::case Frost v. Aylesbury Dairy Co. ([1905] 1 KB 608)
__Held.__ A dairy that supplied **milk** containing **typhoid germs** was liable for breach of fitness for purpose, although it had taken all reasonable precautions — the condition is **strict**, not based on negligence.
:::

**The trade-name proviso.** Where an article is sold "under its **patent or other trade name**", there is no implied condition as to fitness for a particular purpose. But the proviso applies only where the buyer **does not rely** on the seller's skill — where the buyer relies on the seller, despite naming a brand, the condition applies (*Baldry v. Marshall*, [1925] 1 KB 260 — the buyer asked for a car suitable for touring and was recommended a **Bugatti**; it was unsuitable — the seller was liable).

**Merchantable quality — s. 16(2).** Conditions: (i) sale **by description**; (ii) by a seller who **deals** in such goods; (iii) the goods are **not** merchantable — not saleable as goods of that description at the normal price. **Examination proviso:** if the buyer **examined** the goods, there is no condition as to defects that examination **ought to have revealed** — but **latent** defects are still covered (*Grant*).

### (4) Pakistan's s. 16A — the duty to disclose defects

> "16A. Notwithstanding anything contained in section 16, and save where the parties have entered into an agreement to the contrary, the seller shall be under an **obligation to inform the buyer of any defect** in the goods sold **at the time of the contract**, except in a case where the defect is **obviously known** to the buyer." (Inserted by Act XVIII of 1994.)

**Significance.** In England and India, **mere silence** about a defect is not fraud and does not make the seller liable (Contract Act s. 17 Explanation; *Smith v. Hughes*). In **Pakistan**, s. 16A makes **disclosure of known (and, on its wording, any) defects a legal obligation** of the seller, "notwithstanding" s. 16. The exceptions are: (i) the parties' **agreement to the contrary** (e.g., an "as is where is" sale); and (ii) a defect **obviously known** to the buyer. Breach of s. 16A gives the buyer a claim (at least for damages; and where the non-disclosure goes to the root, rejection) and is strong evidence of fraud by concealment. This is the most important Pakistani modification of **caveat emptor**. It is also in line with the Islamic principle of **khiyar al-'aib** (the buyer's option for a defect), under which a seller must not conceal defects in what he sells.

### (5) Sale by sample — s. 17

A sale is by sample where there is a term, express or implied, to that effect (s. 17(1)). There are implied conditions (s. 17(2)):

1. the **bulk shall correspond with the sample** in quality;
2. the buyer shall have a **reasonable opportunity of comparing** the bulk with the sample;
3. the goods shall be **free from any defect, rendering them unmerchantable, which would not be apparent on reasonable examination** of the sample (*Drummond v. Van Ingen* (1887) 12 App Cas 284 — cloth that matched the sample but had a latent defect in its weave that made it unfit for the buyers' trade).

If the sale is by **sample and description**, the goods must correspond with **both** (s. 15).

### (6) Usage of trade — s. 16(3)

An implied condition or warranty as to quality or fitness may be annexed by the **usage of trade** (e.g., a custom in the cotton trade that bales will be of a certain grade).

## 7.5 Caveat emptor — the rule and its exceptions in one place

{w:34,66}
|Exception|Law|
|Fitness for a particular purpose made known, reliance on seller|s. 16(1) (*Priest v. Last*; *Grant*; *Baldry v. Marshall*)|
|Merchantable quality in a sale by description by a dealer|s. 16(2) (latent defects even after examination)|
|Usage of trade|s. 16(3)|
|Sale by description|s. 15 (*Varley v. Whipp*; *Beale v. Taylor*)|
|Sale by sample|s. 17 (*Drummond v. Van Ingen*)|
|**Duty to disclose defects (Pakistan)**|**s. 16A** (unless excluded by agreement or obviously known)|
|**Fraud** or **misrepresentation** by the seller; **active concealment**|Contract Act ss. 17–19; SGA s. 3|
|Implied condition / warranties as to **title**|s. 14|

## 7.6 Exclusion of implied terms — s. 62

"Where any right, duty or liability would arise under a contract of sale by implication of law, it may be **negatived or varied** by **express agreement** or by the **course of dealing** between the parties, or by **usage**, if the usage is such as to bind both parties to the contract."

Courts construe exclusion clauses **strictly** (*contra proferentem*): a clause excluding **warranties** does not exclude **conditions** (*Wallis, Son & Wells v. Pratt and Haynes*, [1911] AC 394 — seed sold as "common English sainfoin" turned out to be giant sainfoin; a clause excluding "any warranty" did not protect the seller from breach of the **condition** of description). Consumer laws (e.g., the **Islamabad Consumers Protection Act 1995**) may also limit exclusions.
