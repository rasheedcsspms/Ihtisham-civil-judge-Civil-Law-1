
# Answer 1 · Guarantee or indemnity? The supplier, the trader, the surety and the insurer (LHC ASJ 2025)

:::glance LHC ADDITIONAL SESSIONS JUDGES EXAMINATION 2025 · CIVIL LAW-I · PART II · Q. 3 · 20 MARKS
P, a wholesale supplier, is reluctant to deliver goods on credit to Q, a new trader. To secure the transaction, R agrees in writing that if Q defaults, he (R) will pay the price of the goods. Later, Q fails to pay, and P demands the money from R. R argues that his liability is in the nature of indemnity and not of a suretyship guarantee, and therefore, he is not bound as a surety. Separately, S enters into a contract with T, an insurer, who promises to compensate S from any loss suffered due to fire in his warehouse.
(a) Distinguish between a contract of guarantee and a contract of indemnity with reference to the above facts. (b) Is indemnity merely a species of insurance guarantee, or does it have an independent footing under contract law? (c) Discuss the scope of liability on R and T, and the remedies available to P and S, respectively.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](d1_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Two sets of facts are placed side by side so that you **classify** each: R's promise ("if Q defaults, I will pay") is a **guarantee** (s. 126); T's promise to compensate for fire loss is an **indemnity** in the form of **insurance**. Part (b) is conceptual — the place of indemnity and insurance in the Act (the narrow s. 124; insurance as a contingent contract and under the Insurance Ordinance 2000). Part (c) asks for **liability and remedies** of each. R's argument is a trap: even if his promise were an indemnity, he would be **more** liable, not less.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction

Both guarantee and indemnity are contracts to protect a person against loss, and both are **contingent** contracts (s. 31). But the Contract Act treats them separately: indemnity in **s. 124** and guarantee in **s. 126**. The difference decides **who is primarily liable** and what rights each party has.

### 2. The law — definitions

1. **Indemnity — s. 124:** "A contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused to him by the conduct of the promisor himself, or by the conduct of any other person, is called a 'contract of indemnity'."
2. **Guarantee — s. 126:** "A 'contract of guarantee' is a contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in case of his default. The person who gives the guarantee is called the 'surety': the person in respect of whose default the guarantee is given is called the 'principal debtor', and the person to whom the guarantee is given is called the 'creditor'. A guarantee may be either oral or written."

### 3. R's promise — a guarantee

R promised P that "**if Q defaults**, he (R) will pay the price". All the elements of s. 126 are present:

1. **Three parties** — P (creditor), Q (principal debtor), R (surety).
2. A **primary liability** of Q to pay the price under the sale on credit.
3. R's promise is **collateral** and **conditional on Q's default** — a secondary liability.
4. **Consideration** — P's delivery of goods to Q on credit: "anything done … for the benefit of the principal debtor may be a sufficient consideration to the surety" (s. 127; *illustration (a)* — goods sold on credit on the faith of C's guarantee is exactly this case).
5. It is **in writing** (s. 126 allows oral or written; Art. 17 QSO attestation aids proof).

R's contention that his liability is an **indemnity** is **misconceived**. The words "if Q defaults" show a **secondary** obligation dependent on another's primary debt — the hallmark of guarantee (*Birkmyr v. Darnell* (1704) 1 Salk 27: "if he does not pay you, I will" is a guarantee; "I will see you paid" is an indemnity).

### 4. Even if it were an indemnity, R would be liable

An indemnifier's liability is **primary and independent** (s. 124). If R had promised to save P from loss, he would be liable for P's loss **directly**, without even the defences a surety has (ss. 133–141). R's argument therefore **fails either way**: the classification affects the nature of his liability, not its existence.

### 5. T's promise — indemnity (insurance)

T, an insurer, promised to compensate S for **loss due to fire** in his warehouse. This is a contract of **insurance**, which is a contract of **indemnity** in substance: S is to be restored to the position he was in before the loss.

**But note:** s. 124 covers loss caused "by the **conduct** of the promisor himself, or by the **conduct of any other person**". Loss by **accidental fire** is not caused by human conduct, so fire insurance falls **outside the literal words** of s. 124. It is nonetheless a **valid** contract: (i) a **contingent contract** (s. 31 — "A contracts to pay B Rs. 10,000 if B's house is burnt" is the Act's own illustration); (ii) governed by the **Insurance Ordinance 2000**; and (iii) subject to the **general principles of indemnity** that the courts apply in equity (*Adamson v. Jarvis* (1827) — the wider English concept).

### 6. Guarantee and indemnity distinguished on these facts

### 7. Is indemnity merely a species of insurance or guarantee?

1. **Indemnity has an independent footing.** It is a distinct contract under **ss. 124–125**, with its own rights for the indemnity-holder (damages, prudent costs and compromises — s. 125). It arises in many contexts besides insurance — e.g., an agent's right to indemnity (ss. 222–223), a principal debtor's implied indemnity to the surety (s. 145), indemnity bonds given to banks for lost cheques or to registrars for duplicate documents.
2. **Insurance is a species of indemnity**, not the other way round — at least fire, marine and property insurance (life insurance is not indemnity, but a contingent contract to pay a fixed sum). Because s. 124 is narrow, insurance rests also on **s. 31** and the **Insurance Ordinance 2000**.
3. **Guarantee is a separate contract**, though related: in a sense it is a **special kind of protection against loss**, but with **three parties** and a **secondary** liability, and it has its own code (ss. 126–147).

### 8. Scope of R's liability

1. **Co-extensive** with Q's — "The liability of the surety is co-extensive with that of the principal debtor, unless it is otherwise provided by the contract" (s. 128): the unpaid price, and any interest and charges due (illustration to s. 128).
2. **Immediate** — P need not first sue Q or exhaust remedies against him (*Bank of Bihar v. Damodar Prasad*, AIR 1969 SC 297).
3. **Defences** — R is discharged if P varied the contract with Q without R's consent (s. 133), released Q (s. 134), made a binding agreement to give Q time (s. 135), impaired R's remedy (s. 139), or lost securities (s. 141); and the guarantee is invalid if obtained by P's misrepresentation or concealment (ss. 142–143). None appears on the facts.

### 9. Scope of T's liability

1. **Actual loss** caused by fire, up to the **sum insured** — the insured may not profit (principle of indemnity).
2. Subject to the policy's **terms, conditions and exclusions** (e.g., notice of loss, excluded causes).
3. **Insurable interest** — S must have an interest in the warehouse or goods at the time of loss.
4. **Utmost good faith** — non-disclosure of material facts by S may allow T to avoid the policy.
5. **Subrogation and contribution** — on payment, T may claim against anyone responsible for the fire; if other insurers covered the same risk, they contribute rateably.

### 10. Remedies

#### i) P

P may **sue R alone**, or **Q and R jointly** (O. I r. 3 CPC — Topic 1), for the price with interest and costs. He need not first proceed against Q.

#### ii) R, on paying P

R is **subrogated** to all P's rights against Q (s. 140) and may recover from Q all he **rightfully paid** under the **implied indemnity** (s. 145), with the benefit of any securities P held (s. 141).

#### iii) S

S may claim under the policy, and if T repudiates or delays, sue for the **amount of the loss** (or seek the remedies provided by the **Insurance Ordinance 2000**, which provides a special forum for insurance claims and compensation for delayed payment). S cannot recover more than his actual loss.

### 11. Conclusion

R's written promise to pay "if Q defaults" is a **guarantee** under s. 126, supported by consideration under s. 127; his plea that it is an indemnity is wrong and would not help him anyway. R is liable **co-extensively** and **immediately**. T's promise is an **indemnity** in the form of **fire insurance** — valid as a contingent contract and under the Insurance Ordinance, and limited to S's **actual loss**. Indemnity has its own footing in ss. 124–125; insurance is a species of it, and guarantee is a distinct three-party contract.

## Why this answer scores

- It **classifies each set of facts** with the statutory test and *Birkmyr v. Darnell* — the core of the question.
- It turns **R's argument** against him (indemnity is primary liability).
- It answers the **conceptual** part (b) with the narrow s. 124, s. 31 and the Insurance Ordinance.
- It deals with **liability and remedies** for all four persons.

# Answer 2 · The holding company's guarantee — is it valid? Essentials of a guarantee (LHC 2020)

:::glance LAHORE HIGH COURT 2020 · CIVIL LAW-I · PART I · Q. 1 · 20 MARKS
A holding company "AZU Limited Company" executed a guarantee of worth Five Hundred Thousand Rupees on behalf of its subsidiary company "AUB Limited Company" via written contract in favor of AKL Bank promising thereby that in case of default by AUB, AZU will discharge the liability. Bank demanded the compliance of the guarantee executed and subsidiary refused to execute it, therefore, Bank asked the guarantor i.e. holding company to fulfill its promise. AZU company also declined to execute the guarantee demanded.
In your opinion, explain whether there is a valid contract of guarantee between the parties. Also, explain the importance of the essential elements required for the formation of a valid contract of guarantee.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](d2_map.png)

## Decoding the question

The twist is that the surety is the **parent** of the debtor. The examiner wants you to see that the holding and subsidiary companies are **separate legal persons**, so there are genuinely **three parties**; and to test each **essential** of a guarantee against the facts — principal liability, consideration, consent, capacity (corporate powers and authority), form and proof. Explain **why** each essential matters, then give a clear opinion.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction

A guarantee is "a contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in case of his default" (s. 126). AZU's written promise to AKL Bank that "in case of default by AUB, AZU will discharge the liability" is, on its face, a classic guarantee. Whether it is **valid** depends on whether the essentials are satisfied.

### 2. Essential 1 — three distinct parties

A guarantee needs a **creditor**, a **principal debtor** and a **surety** (s. 126). Here: AKL Bank (creditor), AUB (principal debtor), AZU (surety).

**Is a holding company a "third person" in relation to its subsidiary?** Yes. A company is a **separate legal person** from its members, including a parent company that owns its shares (*Salomon v. A. Salomon & Co.*, [1897] AC 22; Companies Act 2017). AZU and AUB are distinct persons; AZU's promise concerns the liability of a **third person**, AUB. The relationship of holding and subsidiary does not merge them. (Courts "lift the veil" only in cases of fraud or sham — not to defeat a genuine guarantee.)

**Importance:** without a separate principal debtor, the promise would be a **primary** obligation (an indemnity or direct debt), and the rules of suretyship would not apply.

### 3. Essential 2 — a principal liability

A guarantee is **collateral** to a primary liability. AUB owes, or will owe, the Bank a debt (a loan or facility) — that is the liability guaranteed. Its existence and enforceability matter: if AUB's debt were **void** (e.g., ultra vires AUB or illegal), there would be nothing to guarantee. The facts show a debt which AUB "refused" to pay — a **default**, not an absence of liability.

**Importance:** the surety's liability is **co-extensive** with the principal's (s. 128); it rises and falls with it.

### 4. Essential 3 — consideration

"Anything done, or any promise made, for the **benefit of the principal debtor** may be a sufficient consideration to the surety for giving the guarantee" (s. 127). The Bank's grant of credit to **AUB** — at AZU's request, or on the faith of AZU's guarantee — is sufficient consideration. AZU need receive nothing itself (though, as parent, it benefits indirectly).

If the guarantee was given **after** the loan, without any further act or forbearance by the Bank at AZU's request, it would lack consideration (illus. (c) to s. 127). The facts suggest that the guarantee **secured** the transaction, so the consideration is present.

**Importance:** without consideration a guarantee is void (s. 25), except where the guarantee is made by a registered deed out of natural love and affection (not relevant to companies).

### 5. Essential 4 — free consent and good faith

The guarantee is invalid if obtained by the creditor's **misrepresentation** about a material part of the transaction (s. 142), or by the creditor's **silence** about material circumstances (s. 143 — e.g., if the Bank knew of AUB's earlier defaults and concealed them). Nothing on the facts suggests misrepresentation or concealment; the burden would be on AZU to prove it.

**Importance:** the surety takes on another's risk; he must know the **true nature** of that risk.

### 6. Essential 5 — capacity and authority of a company

A company can act only within its **powers** (its memorandum and the Companies Act) and through **authorised** persons.

1. **Power to guarantee.** The memorandum of a holding company usually authorises it to guarantee the obligations of its subsidiaries. A guarantee **ultra vires** the company would be void.
2. **Corporate approval.** Under the **Companies Act 2017**, investments in **associated companies** — a term that covers guarantees and similar support — generally require approval of the members by **special resolution** (s. 199). If the required approval was not obtained, AZU may argue the guarantee is unauthorised.
3. **Authority of signatories.** The guarantee must be signed by persons authorised by the **board**. A bank dealing in good faith may rely on the company's apparent authority (the "**indoor management**" rule — *Royal British Bank v. Turquand* (1856) 6 E & B 327), unless the irregularity was apparent.

**Importance:** capacity is an essential of every contract (ss. 10–11); a company's capacity is defined by statute and its constitution.

### 7. Essential 6 — form and proof

A guarantee "may be either **oral or written**" (s. 126). Here it is **written**, which is the usual practice of banks. As an instrument creating a **financial obligation**, it should be **attested by two men, or one man and two women** (Art. 17(2)(a), Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984), and, if denied, proved by calling attesting witnesses (Art. 79). It must also bear proper **stamp duty** (Stamp Act 1899 — a guarantee is a stamped instrument; an unstamped instrument is inadmissible until the duty and penalty are paid). These go to **proof and admissibility**, not to the existence of the contract.

### 8. Essential 7 — no condition precedent unfulfilled

If AZU had given the guarantee on condition that a **co-surety** (e.g., a director's personal guarantee) would join, and he did not, the guarantee would be invalid (s. 144). No such condition appears.

### 9. Application — the opinion

1. AKL Bank, AUB and AZU are **three separate persons**; the promise is to discharge **AUB's** liability on **default** — a guarantee (s. 126).
2. The credit given to AUB is **consideration** (s. 127).
3. There is **no indication** of misrepresentation, concealment, or an unfulfilled condition (ss. 142–144).
4. Subject to AZU's **corporate power and approval** (memorandum; Companies Act 2017, s. 199) and the **authority** of its signatories (protected for a bona fide bank by the indoor management rule), the guarantee is **valid**.
5. AUB has **defaulted** ("refused"); AZU's liability has therefore **arisen**. AZU's refusal is a **breach** of the guarantee.

### 10. Conclusion

There is a **valid contract of guarantee** between AKL Bank and AZU for AUB's liability up to Rs. 500,000. The essentials — **three distinct parties**, a **principal liability**, **consideration**, **free consent**, **capacity**, and proper **form** — each serve a purpose: they ensure that the surety knowingly undertakes a **secondary** obligation, for value, for a real debt of another. AZU's only possible escape lies in corporate-law defects (lack of power or approval), which a bank dealing in good faith can usually meet.

## Why this answer scores

- It spots the **separate legal personality** point, which the "holding company" facts are designed to test (*Salomon*).
- Each **essential** has a heading, the statutory basis, an application and an "importance" line — exactly what the question asks.
- It adds **company-law** and **evidence** dimensions (s. 199 Companies Act 2017; Art. 17 QSO; stamp duty) that a judicial examiner expects.
- It gives a clear **opinion**, with the only realistic defence identified.

# Answer 3 · Advising the bank — options for enforcing the guarantee and the guarantor's liability (LHC 2020)

:::glance LAHORE HIGH COURT 2020 · CIVIL LAW-I · PART I · Q. 2 · 20 MARKS
(On the same facts as Answer 2.) Write an opinion advising the Bank on the various possible option(s) available for the enforcement of the contract of guarantee along with the liability of the guarantor.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](d3_map.png)

## Decoding the question

An **opinion** to a client — so write it in that form (to, from, facts, advice). Cover: the **nature and extent** of AZU's liability (s. 128, the Rs. 500,000 cap, immediacy); the **forum** — a bank's recovery suit lies to the **Banking Court** under the **Financial Institutions (Recovery of Finances) Ordinance 2001**; the **options** (sue AZU alone, sue both, enforce securities, set-off and banker's lien, demand under the guarantee); the **defences** AZU may raise (discharge — ss. 133–139, 141); and practical **steps** (notice of demand, limitation).

## The model answer

### 1. Heading

**To:** The Manager, AKL Bank. **Subject:** Enforcement of AZU Limited's guarantee of AUB Limited's liability (Rs. 500,000). **Facts:** AUB has defaulted; AZU, the guarantor, has refused your demand.

### 2. The nature of AZU's liability

1. **Co-extensive** — "The liability of the surety is co-extensive with that of the principal debtor, unless it is otherwise provided by the contract" (s. 128). AZU is liable for AUB's debt with **interest and charges** (illustration to s. 128) — but **not beyond the limit** of Rs. 500,000 fixed in the guarantee (plus costs, and interest on its own default if the guarantee so provides).
2. **Immediate** — AZU's liability arose on AUB's default. The Bank is **not bound** to sue AUB first or exhaust remedies against it (*Bank of Bihar v. Damodar Prasad*, AIR 1969 SC 297: "the very object of the guarantee is defeated if the creditor is asked to postpone his remedies against the surety").
3. **Separate contract** — the guarantee is a contract between the Bank and AZU; AZU cannot rely on any internal arrangement with AUB (s. 132 principle).
4. **Continuing or specific?** If the guarantee covers a running facility ("all sums from time to time due"), it is **continuing** (s. 129); AZU may revoke it for the **future** by notice (s. 130), but remains liable for what was already advanced.

### 3. Option 1 — a recovery suit against AZU alone

The Bank may sue **AZU alone** on the guarantee. Since AKL Bank is a "**financial institution**" and the claim arises from a **finance** and a **guarantee** for it, the suit lies to the **Banking Court** under the **Financial Institutions (Recovery of Finances) Ordinance 2001**, which has exclusive jurisdiction over such claims against a **customer** — a term that includes a **surety or guarantor** of the finance. Under that Ordinance:

1. the plaint must be supported by a **statement of account** and the documents relied on;
2. the defendant may defend only with **leave**, by an application for leave to defend raising substantial questions (s. 10) — otherwise a decree follows;
3. the decree is executed by the Banking Court, which has wide powers (including sale of mortgaged or pledged property).

(If the Bank were not a financial institution, the suit would be an ordinary recovery suit, or a **summary suit** under O. XXXVII CPC if based on a negotiable instrument — Topic 2.)

### 4. Option 2 — a suit against AUB and AZU jointly

The Bank may join **AUB and AZU** as defendants (O. I r. 3 CPC) and obtain a decree against **both, jointly and severally**. It can then execute against either. This is often the best option: one suit, one decree, full security.

### 5. Option 3 — enforce securities, set-off and the banker's lien

1. **Securities** — if AUB or AZU gave any pledge, mortgage or hypothecation, the Bank may enforce it (pledge — sale on reasonable notice, s. 176).
2. **Banker's general lien** — a banker may retain, as security for a **general balance of account**, goods and securities bailed to it (s. 171).
3. **Set-off / combination of accounts** — if AZU keeps a credit balance with the Bank and the guarantee permits, the Bank may set it off against AZU's liability once due.
4. **Caution:** the Bank must not **release** or **lose** any security held when the guarantee was given, or AZU will be discharged to that extent (s. 141).

### 6. Option 4 — a demand under the guarantee and notice

Before suing, the Bank should serve a written **demand** on AZU in the manner the guarantee requires (many guarantees make a written demand a **condition** of liability), and a **legal notice**. A demand also fixes the date from which interest for default may run and starts limitation where the guarantee makes payment due "on demand".

### 7. Limitation

A suit on a guarantee is governed by the **Limitation Act 1908** — generally **three years**, running from the date of **default / demand** (depending on the terms of the guarantee). The Bank should act promptly; an **acknowledgment** in writing by AZU within the period gives a fresh period (s. 19, Limitation Act).

### 8. Defences AZU may raise — and how to meet them

|Defence|Advice|
|Variance in AUB's facility without AZU's consent (s. 133)|Check whether limits, rates or terms were changed after the guarantee; most bank guarantees contain AZU's **advance consent** to variations.|

### 9. AZU's rights after payment

The Bank should be aware that, once AZU pays, AZU is **subrogated** to the Bank's rights against AUB (s. 140), is entitled to the Bank's **securities** (s. 141), and may recover from AUB under the **implied indemnity** (s. 145). The Bank should therefore be ready to **assign** the securities to AZU on full payment.

### 10. Advice in summary

1. Serve a written **demand** under the guarantee on AZU (and AUB).
2. Preserve all **securities**; make no arrangement with AUB without AZU's consent.
3. File a **recovery suit in the Banking Court** under the FIRFO 2001 against **AUB and AZU jointly and severally** for the amount due, limited as against AZU to Rs. 500,000 plus costs.
4. Meanwhile, exercise any **set-off** or **lien** available (s. 171).
5. Oppose any application for leave to defend that raises only the defences above, which are not made out on the facts.

### 11. Conclusion

AZU's liability is **co-extensive** with AUB's (within the Rs. 500,000 limit) and **immediate**. The Bank may proceed against AZU **without first suing AUB**, preferably by a joint recovery suit in the **Banking Court**, while preserving its securities and avoiding any variance or release that could discharge the guarantor.

## Why this answer scores

- It is written as an **opinion** with practical steps — what the question asks for.
- It states the **extent and immediacy** of liability with s. 128 and *Bank of Bihar*.
- It identifies the right **forum** (Banking Court under FIRFO 2001), which separates a judicial candidate from an LL.B answer.
- It anticipates AZU's **defences** (ss. 133–144) and tells the Bank how to avoid them.

# Answer 4 · Rights of the surety, and when the surety is discharged (PU LL.B)

:::glance PUNJAB UNIVERSITY LL.B PART III · LAW OF CONTRACT · 2019 (ANNUAL) Q. 4 AND 2019 (SUPPLEMENTARY) Q. 7 · 20 MARKS
Define guarantee. What are the rights of a surety? · Define indemnity and guarantee. When is a surety discharged from liability?
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](d4_map.png)

## Decoding the question

A two-part theory question: **rights** of the surety (against the principal debtor, the creditor and co-sureties) and **discharge** (by revocation, by the creditor's conduct, by invalidity). Quote the **sections** and their **illustrations**, which are almost all the authority you need, and give the **rationale**: the surety is a "favoured debtor" whose risk must not be increased behind his back.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — the surety as a favoured debtor

A guarantee is a contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person on default (s. 126). The surety receives nothing for himself; he helps the principal debtor obtain credit. The law therefore **protects** him: it gives him strong **rights** once he pays, and **discharges** him if the creditor changes or damages his position without his consent.

### 2. Rights against the principal debtor

1. **Subrogation — s. 140.** On payment or performance of all that he is liable for, the surety "is invested with **all the rights which the creditor had** against the principal debtor" — including securities, priorities and decrees.
2. **Indemnity — s. 145.** "In every contract of guarantee there is an **implied promise by the principal debtor to indemnify the surety**; and the surety is entitled to recover from the principal debtor whatever sum he has **rightfully paid** under the guarantee, but no sums which he has paid wrongfully." *Illus. (a):* reasonable defence costs recoverable; *(b):* costs of a groundless defence not recoverable; *(c):* only the price of goods actually supplied.
3. **Quia timet** — once the debt is due, the surety may ask the court to direct the principal debtor to pay the creditor and relieve him.

### 3. Rights against the creditor

1. **Benefit of securities — s. 141.** The surety is entitled to every security which the creditor has **at the time of the guarantee**, whether he knows of it or not; if the creditor **loses** it or **parts with** it without the surety's consent, the surety is **discharged to the extent of its value**. *Illus. (a):* cancelling a mortgage of the debtor's furniture; *(b):* withdrawing execution — surety discharged; *(c):* giving up a security taken **later** — not discharged.
2. **Set-off** — the surety may set off any claim the principal debtor (or he himself) has against the creditor.
3. **Discharge** in the circumstances of ss. 133–139 (below).
4. **Not to be sued for more** than the principal debtor owes (s. 128) or beyond the limit of the guarantee.

### 4. Rights against co-sureties

1. **Equal contribution — s. 146.** Co-sureties for the same debt, "either jointly or severally, and whether under the same or different contracts, and whether with or without the knowledge of each other", contribute **equally**, unless otherwise agreed. *Illus. (a):* Rs. 3,000 — Rs. 1,000 each; *(b):* agreed shares one-quarter, one-quarter, one-half — Rs. 250, 250, 500.
2. **Different sums — s. 147.** Co-sureties bound in different sums pay **equally as far as their limits permit**. *Illus.:* A (Rs. 10,000), B (Rs. 20,000), C (Rs. 40,000): on a Rs. 30,000 default — Rs. 10,000 each; on Rs. 40,000 — A 10,000, B and C 15,000 each; on Rs. 70,000 — each his full limit.
3. **Release of one** — does not discharge the others or free the released surety from contribution (s. 138).

### 5. Discharge by revocation (continuing guarantees)

1. **Notice — s. 130:** "A continuing guarantee may at any time be revoked by the surety, as to future transactions, by notice to the creditor." He remains liable for past transactions (*illus. (a), (b)*).
2. **Death — s. 131:** the surety's death revokes a continuing guarantee as to future transactions, unless otherwise agreed.

### 7. Discharge by invalidity of the guarantee

1. **Misrepresentation** by the creditor (or with his knowledge) about a material part of the transaction — invalid (s. 142).
2. **Concealment** — keeping silence as to **material circumstances** — invalid (s. 143; *illus. (a):* clerk's earlier defaults concealed; *(b):* secret overcharge to clear an old debt).
3. **Co-surety** who was to join does not join — invalid (s. 144).

### 8. Other modes of discharge

1. **Performance** — payment by the principal debtor or the surety.
2. **Novation** of the principal contract (s. 62) — the old debt, and the guarantee, end.
3. **Discharge of the principal debt by the creditor's act** (s. 134). But discharge of the principal debtor **by operation of law** (e.g., insolvency) does **not** discharge the surety.

### 9. Rationale

The surety agreed to bear a **defined risk**. The creditor may not, without the surety's consent, **increase** that risk (variance, giving time), **destroy** the surety's recourse (release, loss of securities, impairment), or obtain the guarantee by **deceit** (misrepresentation, concealment). Each rule protects the surety's eventual right to recover from the principal debtor.

### 10. Conclusion

A surety who pays steps into the creditor's shoes (s. 140), takes the benefit of every security (s. 141), recovers from the principal debtor (s. 145), and shares the burden equally with co-sureties (ss. 146–147). He is discharged by revocation of a continuing guarantee (ss. 130–131), by variance, release, binding time or impairment of his remedy (ss. 133–139), by loss of securities (s. 141), and where the guarantee was obtained by misrepresentation or concealment (ss. 142–144) — but not by mere forbearance or by the release of a co-surety.
