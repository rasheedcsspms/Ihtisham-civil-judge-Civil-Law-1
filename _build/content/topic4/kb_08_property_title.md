
# Chapter 8 · Transfer of property and risk (SGA ss. 18 to 26)

## 8.1 Why the moment of passing of property matters

"Property" in the SGA means **ownership** (s. 2(11)). The time at which property passes from seller to buyer decides:

1. **Risk** — risk passes with property (s. 26): *res perit domino*.
2. **Remedies** — the seller can sue for the **price** only when property has passed (s. 55(1)); otherwise for **damages** (s. 56).
3. **Insolvency** — whether the goods belong to the buyer's or the seller's estate.
4. **Third parties** — whether a creditor of the seller can attach the goods; whether the buyer can resell.

:::maxim Res perit domino — the thing perishes for its owner
Whoever owns the goods when they are lost or damaged bears the loss, unless the parties agree otherwise.
:::

## 8.2 The basic rules — ss. 18 and 19

1. **Unascertained goods — s. 18.** "Where there is a contract for the sale of **unascertained goods**, **no property** in the goods is transferred to the buyer **unless and until the goods are ascertained**." (*Healy v. Howlett & Sons* [1917] 1 KB 337 — 20 boxes of mackerel from a larger consignment of 190 had not been set aside for the buyer when they went bad; property and risk had not passed.)
2. **Specific or ascertained goods — s. 19.** Property passes "at such time as the parties to the contract **intend** it to be transferred". To find the intention, "regard shall be had to the **terms** of the contract, the **conduct** of the parties and the **circumstances** of the case" (s. 19(2)). "Unless a different intention appears", the **rules in ss. 20–24** apply (s. 19(3)).

## 8.3 The rules for ascertaining intention — ss. 20 to 24

|s.|Situation|Property passes|
|20|**Unconditional** contract for **specific goods** in a **deliverable state**|**When the contract is made** — even if payment or delivery, or both, are postponed.|
|21|Specific goods; the seller must **do something** to put them into a deliverable state|When **that thing is done and the buyer has notice** of it. (*Underwood Ltd. v. |24|Goods sent **on approval** or "**sale or return**"|(a) When the buyer **signifies approval** or does an **act adopting** the transaction (e.g., **pledging** the goods — *Kirkham v. Attenborough* [1897] 1 QB 201); (b) if he retains them **without notice of rejection** — on the expiry of the **fixed time**, or of a **reasonable time** (*Poole v. Smith's Car Sales* [1962] 1 WLR 744).|

## 8.4 Reservation of the right of disposal — s. 25

1. The seller may, by the terms of the contract or appropriation, **reserve the right of disposal** until conditions (usually **payment**) are fulfilled. Property then does **not** pass, even after delivery to the buyer or a carrier (s. 25(1)) — the basis of "**retention of title**" clauses.
2. Where goods are shipped or sent by railway and the **bill of lading or railway receipt** is made deliverable to the **order of the seller** or his agent, the seller is **prima facie** deemed to reserve the right of disposal (s. 25(2), as substituted in Pakistan in 1962).
3. Where the seller draws a **bill of exchange** on the buyer and sends it with the bill of lading or railway receipt, the buyer must **return** the documents if he does not honour the bill; if he wrongfully retains them, property does **not** pass to him (s. 25(3)).

## 8.5 Risk — s. 26

> "26. Unless otherwise agreed, the goods remain at the **seller's risk until the property therein is transferred** to the buyer, but when the property therein is transferred to the buyer, the goods are at the **buyer's risk whether delivery has been made or not**:
> Provided that, where delivery has been **delayed through the fault** of either buyer or seller, the goods are at the risk of the **party in fault** as regards any loss which might not have occurred but for such fault:
> Provided also that nothing in this section shall affect the duties or liabilities of either seller or buyer as a **bailee** of the goods of the other party."

**Points:**

1. **Risk follows property**, not possession — unless the parties agree otherwise (risk may pass before or after property: *Sterns Ltd. v. Vickers Ltd.* [1923] 1 KB 78 — buyer of part of a quantity of spirit in a tank bore the risk of deterioration though property had not passed).
2. **Delay** — the party at fault bears the risk of loss caused by the delay (*Demby Hamilton & Co. v. Barden* [1949] 1 All ER 435 — buyer who delayed taking delivery of apple juice bore the risk when it went bad).
3. A party holding the other's goods is still a **bailee** and must take reasonable care (Contract Act s. 151).
4. **Goods in transit** — where the seller agrees to deliver at a distant place at his own risk, the buyer still bears risk of deterioration **necessarily incident to transit** (s. 40); where goods go by sea, the seller must give notice enabling the buyer to insure, or the goods are at his risk (s. 39(3)).

:::eg Who bears the loss?
On 1 June, Kamran agrees to buy from Waseem a specific used car parked in Waseem's showroom in Blue Area for Rs. 4 million, delivery and payment on 5 June. On 3 June the car is destroyed in a fire, without anyone's fault. The contract was **unconditional**, for **specific goods** in a **deliverable state** — property passed on **1 June** (s. 20), and risk with it (s. 26). Kamran must **pay the price** (s. 55(1)), though he got nothing. If Waseem had agreed to **repaint** the car before delivery and had not done so, property would not have passed (s. 21), the loss would fall on Waseem, and the agreement would be **avoided** (s. 8).
:::

:::recap Chapter 8 in five lines
- Property = ownership; its passing decides risk, remedies, insolvency and third-party rights; *res perit domino*.
- Unascertained goods — no property until ascertained (s. 18; *Healy v. Howlett*); specific goods — as intended (s. 19).
- Rules: specific + deliverable + unconditional — at contract (s. 20); to be put in deliverable state — when done and notice (s. 21; *Underwood*); to ascertain price — when done and notice (s. 22); unascertained — unconditional appropriation with assent; carrier (s. 23); approval / sale or return (s. 24; *Kirkham v. Attenborough*).
- Reservation of right of disposal; order bills of lading / railway receipts (s. 25).
- Risk follows property unless agreed; delay — party at fault; bailee's duty preserved (s. 26; *Demby Hamilton*).
:::

# Chapter 9 · Transfer of title — nemo dat quod non habet (SGA ss. 27 to 30)

## 9.1 The rule

:::maxim Nemo dat quod non habet — no one can give what he does not have
A person who is not the owner cannot transfer a better title than he himself has. The buyer from a thief, or from a person who merely holds the goods, gets no ownership against the true owner.
:::

> "27. Subject to the provisions of this Act and of any other law for the time being in force, where goods are sold by a person who is **not the owner** thereof and who does not sell them under the **authority or with the consent** of the owner, the buyer acquires **no better title** to the goods than the seller had, **unless the owner of the goods is by his conduct precluded from denying the seller's authority to sell**:
> Provided that, where a **mercantile agent** is, with the consent of the owner, in possession of the goods or of a document of title to the goods, any sale made by him, when acting in the **ordinary course of business** of a mercantile agent, shall be as valid as if he were expressly authorised by the owner of the goods to make the same; provided that the buyer acts in **good faith** and has not at the time of the contract of sale **notice** that the seller has not authority to sell."

**Rationale and the conflict.** The law must choose between two innocent persons — the **owner** who lost his goods and the **buyer** who paid for them in good faith. The rule protects **ownership** ("security of property"); the exceptions protect **commerce** ("security of transactions") where the owner has contributed to the situation, or where commercial necessity demands it.

## 9.2 The exceptions

### (1) Estoppel — s. 27 (main part)

The owner is "**by his conduct precluded from denying the seller's authority to sell**" — where he has **held out** the seller as owner or as having authority (by words or conduct), and the buyer acted on it.

- *Eastern Distributors Ltd. v. Goldring* ([1957] 2 QB 600) — the owner of a van signed documents enabling a dealer to represent that the van was the dealer's; the finance company that bought it in good faith got title.
- Mere **possession** of goods by the seller is **not** enough for estoppel; nor is mere carelessness in losing them (*Central Newbury Car Auctions v. Unity Finance* [1957] 1 QB 371 — handing over a car and its registration book to a fraudster did not estop the owner).

### (2) Sale by a mercantile agent — s. 27 proviso (and Contract Act s. 178 for pledges)

Conditions:

1. The seller is a **mercantile agent** (s. 2(9));
2. he is **in possession** of the goods or documents of title **with the consent of the owner** — consent obtained by fraud is still consent (*Folkes v. King* [1923] 1 KB 282), but possession must be held **as mercantile agent** (*Pearson v. Rose & Young* [1951] 1 KB 275 — where the car was handed over **without** its registration book, the sale with the book obtained by trickery did not pass title);
3. he sells **in the ordinary course of business** of a mercantile agent (at business premises, in business hours, in the normal way);
4. the buyer acts in **good faith** and **without notice** of the lack of authority.

### (3) Sale by one of joint owners — s. 28

"If one of several joint owners of goods has the **sole possession** of them **by permission of the co-owners**, the property in the goods is transferred to any person who buys them of such joint owner **in good faith** and has not at the time of the contract of sale notice that the seller has not authority to sell."

### (4) Sale by a person in possession under a voidable contract — s. 29

"When the seller of goods has obtained possession thereof under a contract **voidable under section 19 or section 19A** of the Contract Act, 1872, but the contract **has not been rescinded** at the time of the sale, the buyer acquires a **good title** to the goods, provided he buys them in good faith and without notice of the seller's defect of title."

- *Phillips v. Brooks Ltd.* ([1919] 2 KB 243) — a rogue who obtained a ring by impersonation in a **face-to-face** sale had a **voidable** title; the pawnbroker who took it in good faith before rescission got good title. Similarly *Lewis v. Averay* ([1972] 1 QB 198).
- Contrast *Cundy v. Lindsay* ((1878) 3 App Cas 459) — where the contract is **void** for mistake as to identity, the rogue gets **no** title at all and s. 29 does not apply.
- The owner must rescind **before** the resale — e.g., by informing the police, where the rogue cannot be found (*Car and Universal Finance Co. v. Caldwell* [1965] 1 QB 525).

### (5) Seller in possession after sale — s. 30(1)

"Where a person, **having sold goods, continues or is in possession** of the goods or of the documents of title to the goods, the **delivery or transfer** by that person or by a mercantile agent acting for him, of the goods or documents of title under any **sale, pledge or other disposition** thereof to any person receiving the same **in good faith and without notice of the previous sale** shall have the same effect as if the person making the delivery or transfer were expressly authorised by the owner of the goods to make the same."

The first buyer, who left the goods with the seller, loses to the second buyer who took **delivery** in good faith; the first buyer's remedy is against the seller (*Pacific Motor Auctions v. Motor Credits* [1965] AC 867, PC).

### (6) Buyer in possession — s. 30(2)

"Where a person, **having bought or agreed to buy goods, obtains, with the consent of the seller, possession** of the goods or the documents of title to the goods, the delivery or transfer by that person … under any sale, pledge or other disposition thereof to any person receiving the same in good faith and **without notice of any lien or other right of the original seller** in respect of the goods shall have effect as if such lien or right did not exist."

This defeats the unpaid seller's **lien** or reserved right of disposal, where the buyer has possession with his consent (*Newtons of Wembley v. Williams* [1965] 1 QB 560). It does **not** apply to a **hirer** under hire-purchase, who has not "bought or agreed to buy" (*Helby v. Matthews*).

### (7) Other exceptions under other laws

1. **Finder** of goods — may sell in the cases in Contract Act **s. 169**.
2. **Pawnee** — may sell on default after reasonable notice (Contract Act **s. 176**); pledges by mercantile agents and voidable-title holders (**ss. 178, 178A**).
3. **Unpaid seller** — resale after lien or stoppage gives the new buyer good title (SGA **s. 54(3)**).
4. **Court sales** — a purchaser at an execution sale (CPC O. XXI) gets the judgment-debtor's title, subject to the rules on setting aside (Topic 2).
5. **Negotiable instruments** — a holder in due course gets good title despite defects in his transferor's title (Negotiable Instruments Act ss. 9, 58).
6. **Official receivers / liquidators** selling under statutory powers.

**No market overt in Pakistan.** The old English rule that a buyer in good faith in an open market ("market overt") got good title has **no** counterpart in the Pakistani SGA.

:::eg A stolen motorbike
A thief steals Ali's motorbike in G-9 and sells it to Bashir, who buys in good faith from a roadside dealer. Ali traces it. **Nemo dat** applies — the thief had no title, so Bashir has none (s. 27). None of the exceptions apply: the thief was not a mercantile agent in possession with Ali's consent, there was no voidable contract (s. 29), and Ali did not hold the thief out as owner (estoppel). Bashir must return the bike and sue the thief (and the dealer, for breach of the **condition as to title**, s. 14(a); *Rowland v. Divall*).
:::

:::recap Chapter 9 in six lines
- *Nemo dat quod non habet* — a non-owner without authority passes no better title (s. 27); protects ownership.
- Estoppel by the owner's conduct (*Eastern Distributors v. Goldring*; not mere possession — *Central Newbury*).
- Mercantile agent in possession with consent, ordinary course, good faith (s. 27 proviso; *Folkes v. King*; *Pearson v. Rose & Young*); pledges — Contract Act s. 178.
- Joint owner in sole possession (s. 28); voidable contract not yet rescinded (s. 29; *Phillips v. Brooks* vs *Cundy v. Lindsay*; *Caldwell*).
- Seller in possession (s. 30(1)); buyer in possession with consent (s. 30(2); not a hirer — *Helby v. Matthews*).
- Others: finder (s. 169), pawnee (s. 176), unpaid seller resale (s. 54(3)), court sales, holder in due course; no market overt.
:::
