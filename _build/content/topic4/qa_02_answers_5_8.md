
# Answer 5 · Bailment, bailor and bailee — the obligations of each (LHC 2023)

:::glance LAHORE HIGH COURT 2023 · CIVIL LAW-I · Q. 2 · 20 MARKS
Define expressions 'Bailment', 'Bailor', and 'Bailee'. Illustrate the respective obligations of 'Bailor' and 'Bailee' as discussed in the Contract Act.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](d5_map.png)

## Decoding the question

"Define" — s. 148 with its essentials and what is **not** bailment. "Illustrate the respective obligations" — every duty of the **bailor** (ss. 150, 158, 159, 164) and of the **bailee** (ss. 151–157, 160–163, 165–167), each with the **statutory illustration**. A short note on the bailee's **rights** (lien) rounds off the answer.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction

Bailment is one of the most common contracts of daily life — the car left at a workshop, clothes at a laundry, goods in a warehouse, a book lent to a friend. Chapter IX of the Contract Act (ss. 148–181) lays down the duties and rights of the parties.

### 2. Definitions — s. 148

"A '**bailment**' is the delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall, when the purpose is accomplished, be returned or otherwise disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them. The person delivering the goods is called the '**bailor**'. The person to whom they are delivered is called the '**bailee**'." By the Explanation, a person **already in possession** who contracts to hold as bailee becomes a bailee.

### 3. Essentials

1. **Goods** — movables only.
2. **Delivery of possession** — actual or constructive (s. 149); ownership stays with the bailor.
3. **Purpose** — custody, repair, carriage, use, security.
4. **Contract** — express or implied (by law for finders — s. 71; State seizures — *State of Gujarat v. Memon Mahomed Haji Hasam*, AIR 1967 SC 1885).
5. **Return** of the **same goods**, or disposal as directed.

**Not bailment:** a deposit of **money** in a bank (the bank need not return the same notes); a **sale**; a **servant's** custody of his master's goods.

### 4. Duties of the bailor

#### i) To disclose known faults — s. 150

The bailor must disclose faults he is **aware** of which **materially interfere with use** or expose the bailee to **extraordinary risks**; otherwise he is liable for damage arising **directly** from them. *Illus. (a):* A lends a horse he knows to be **vicious** — liable for B's injury.

#### ii) In bailment for hire — liability even for unknown faults — s. 150 (second para.)

"If the goods are bailed for hire, the bailor is responsible for such damage, **whether he was or was not aware** of the existence of such faults." *Illus. (b):* A hires an unsafe carriage from B, who does not know of the defect — B is liable.

#### iii) To repay necessary expenses in gratuitous bailment — s. 158

Where the bailee receives **no remuneration**, the bailor must repay **necessary expenses** incurred for the bailment.

#### iv) To indemnify for early recall of a gratuitous loan — s. 159

The lender may recall a gratuitous loan **at any time**, but if the borrower, acting on the loan for a specified time, would suffer **loss exceeding the benefit** derived, the lender must **indemnify** him for the excess.

#### v) To indemnify for defective title — s. 164

The bailor is responsible for any loss the bailee suffers because the bailor **was not entitled** to make the bailment, receive back the goods or give directions.

#### vi) To receive back the goods

The bailor must take back the goods when the purpose is accomplished (otherwise the bailee may claim reasonable charges for custody).

### 5. Duties of the bailee

#### i) To take reasonable care — ss. 151, 152

The bailee must take "as much care of the goods bailed to him as a **man of ordinary prudence** would, under similar circumstances, take of **his own goods** of the same bulk, quality and value" (s. 151). If he does, he is **not responsible** for loss, destruction or deterioration, in the absence of a special contract (s. 152). The standard is the **same in all bailments**, gratuitous or for reward. A clause attempting to reduce liability below this standard has been held invalid (*Lily White v. R. Munuswami*, AIR 1966 Mad 13 — laundry's "50 % of value" clause).

#### ii) Not to act inconsistently with the conditions — s. 153

The bailment becomes **voidable** at the bailor's option. *Illus.:* a horse hired for B's **own riding** is driven in his carriage — A may terminate.

#### iii) Not to make unauthorised use — s. 154

The bailee is liable for **any damage** arising **from or during** unauthorised use, **even without negligence**. *Illus. (a):* a horse lent for B's own riding is ridden carefully by C, who is a member of B's family, and falls by accident — B is liable. *Illus. (b):* a horse hired in **Karachi** to go to **Hyderabad** is taken to **Khairpur** and falls — A is liable.

#### iv) Not to mix the goods with his own — ss. 155–157

With consent — proportionate interest (s. 155). Without consent, separable — the bailee bears separation costs and damage (s. 156; *illus.:* 100 marked bales of cotton). Without consent, inseparable — the bailee must **compensate** (s. 157; *illus.:* Cape flour worth Rs. 45 mixed with country flour worth Rs. 25).

#### v) To return the goods — s. 160

"It is the duty of the bailee to return, or deliver according to the bailor's directions, the goods bailed, **without demand**, as soon as the time … has expired, or the purpose … has been accomplished."

#### vi) Liability for failure to return in time — s. 161

If, by his default, the goods are not returned at the proper time, he is responsible for **any loss, destruction or deterioration from that time** — even by accident (*Shaw & Co. v. Symmons & Sons*, [1917] 1 KB 799 — books destroyed by fire after the bookbinder failed to return them in time).

#### vii) To deliver any increase or profit — s. 163

*Illus.:* a cow left with B has a **calf** — B must deliver both.

#### viii) Not to deny the bailor's title, and to deal properly with competing claims

The bailee is **estopped** from denying the bailor's title (Art. 115 QSO principle). But he may return the goods to **one joint owner** (s. 165); is protected if he returns them **in good faith** to a bailor without title (s. 166); and where a third person claims them, that person may apply to the **court** to stop delivery and decide title (s. 167).

### 6. Rights of the bailee (in brief)

1. **Particular lien** for labour or skill on the goods (s. 170; *illus. (a):* the jeweller who cuts a diamond; *(b):* not the tailor who gave credit).
2. **General lien** for bankers, factors, wharfingers, High Court attorneys and policy-brokers (s. 171).
3. **Compensation** under ss. 150, 159, 164; **expenses** under s. 158.
4. **Suit against wrongdoers** (s. 180), with apportionment (s. 181).

### 7. Termination of bailment

On expiry of the time or accomplishment of the purpose (s. 160); at the bailor's option on inconsistent use (s. 153); on recall of a gratuitous loan (s. 159); on the death of either party in a **gratuitous** bailment (s. 162); and on destruction of the goods.

### 8. Illustration

Hassan leaves his car at Ahmed Motors, I-9, for a service, for a fee. Ahmed is a **bailee for reward**. He must keep the car with ordinary prudence (s. 151) — e.g., lock it inside the workshop at night; he must not use it for his own errands (s. 154); he must return it when the service is done (s. 160); and he has a **particular lien** for his service charges (s. 170). If the car is stolen from the open street because Ahmed left it unlocked, he is liable; if it is stolen in an armed robbery of a properly secured workshop, he is not (s. 152).

### 9. Conclusion

Bailment is the delivery of goods for a purpose on a contract to return them (s. 148). The **bailor** must disclose faults (strictly, if for hire), repay expenses in gratuitous bailments, indemnify on early recall and for defective title. The **bailee** must take the care of a prudent man, avoid unauthorised use and mixing, return the goods with any increase on time, and respect the bailor's title. These reciprocal duties keep the goods safe while they are in another's hands.

## Why this answer scores

- **Definitions** and **essentials** first, with what is *not* bailment — clarity for the examiner.
- Every **duty** has its section and **statutory illustration**, as "illustrate" requires.
- It notes the **strict** liabilities (s. 150 for hire; s. 154 unauthorised use; s. 161 after default) — points weak answers miss.
- A brief note on **rights** and **termination** completes the picture without drifting.

# Answer 6 · Express and implied authority of an agent; s. 215 — agent dealing on his own account (LHC 2023)

:::glance LAHORE HIGH COURT 2023 · CIVIL LAW-I · Q. 1 · 20 MARKS
What do the 'express' and 'implied' authority of an agent mean? Explain factors and circumstances where the authority of the agent is said to be implied. Discuss the circumstances envisaged by section 215 of the Contract Act, where the principal may repudiate the transaction undertaken by the agent, dealing on his own account in the business of the agency.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](d6_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Three parts: (i) **definitions** of express and implied authority (ss. 186–187); (ii) the **factors** from which authority is implied — the circumstances, conduct, course of dealing, incidental authority (s. 188), emergency (s. 189), custom (s. 211), and the related ostensible authority (s. 237); (iii) **s. 215** — its conditions, the two alternative grounds for repudiation, the illustrations and the related s. 216. Pakistani practice on **powers of attorney** adds value.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — authority is the heart of agency

An agent is "a person employed to do any act for another or to represent another in dealings with third persons" (s. 182). The principal is bound only by acts within the agent's **authority**. "The authority of an agent may be expressed or implied" (s. 186).

### 2. Express authority — s. 187

"An authority is said to be **express** when it is given by **words spoken or written**." Examples: a **power of attorney** authorising the attorney to sell a plot; a letter appointing a distributor; an oral instruction to buy shares. Its extent is found by **construing the words**.

### 3. Implied authority — s. 187

"An authority is said to be **implied** when it is to be **inferred from the circumstances** of the case; and **things spoken or written**, or the **ordinary course of dealing**, may be accounted circumstances of the case."

*Illustration:* A owns a shop in **Quetta**, lives in **Karachi**, and visits occasionally. The shop is managed by B, who habitually orders goods from C in A's name and pays for them out of A's funds **with A's knowledge**. B has an **implied authority** to order goods from C in A's name for the shop.

### 4. Factors and circumstances from which authority is implied

1. **Course of dealing** — repeated conduct known to and accepted by the principal (the Quetta illustration).
2. **Position or relationship** — a manager, a partner (Partnership Act), a wife managing the household (authority to buy necessaries on the husband's credit), a master of a ship.
3. **Incidental authority — s. 188.** An agent authorised to do an act may do "every lawful thing which is necessary" for it; an agent to carry on a business may do "every lawful thing necessary for the purpose, or **usually done** in the course of conducting such business". *Illus. (a):* an agent to recover a debt at **Karachi** may take legal proceedings and give a discharge; *(b):* an agent to run a shipbuilding business may buy timber and hire workmen.
4. **Custom and usage** — authority according to the custom of the trade at the place (s. 211; *illus. (b):* a broker in a cash-only trade may not sell on credit).
5. **Emergency — s. 189.** In an emergency, an agent may do all acts a prudent man would do to protect his principal from loss. *Illus. (b):* provisions consigned from **Lahore** to **Rawalpindi** may be sold at Lahore if they will not survive the journey (*Great Northern Railway v. Swaffield* (1874) LR 9 Ex 132).
6. **Ratification** of past acts may imply authority for similar future acts (ss. 196–197).

### 5. Ostensible (apparent) authority — s. 237

Closely related is **ostensible authority**: where the principal "by his **words or conduct**" has induced third persons to believe that the agent's acts were within his authority, the principal is **bound**, even if he had secretly limited it (s. 237; *illus. (a):* goods sold below a secret reserve price; *Freeman & Lockyer v. Buckhurst Park Properties*, [1964] 2 QB 480). Implied authority is **real** authority inferred between principal and agent; ostensible authority is authority **as it appears** to the third party.

### 6. Construction of powers of attorney

Express authority in a power of attorney is construed **strictly**. An attorney may do only what is clearly authorised and what is necessary to do it (s. 188). Pakistani superior courts have repeatedly refused to uphold transactions in which attorneys transferred the principal's property to **themselves or their close relatives** without the principal's informed consent (see, e.g., *Fida Muhammad v. Pir Muhammad Khan*, PLD 1985 SC 341) — which leads directly to s. 215.

### 7. Section 215 — the text

"If an agent **deals on his own account in the business of the agency**, without first obtaining the **consent** of his principal and **acquainting him with all material circumstances** which have come to his own knowledge on the subject, the principal may **repudiate** the transaction, if the case shows either that any **material fact has been dishonestly concealed** from him by the agent, or that the dealings of the agent have been **disadvantageous** to him."

### 8. Circumstances in which the principal may repudiate

1. The agent **deals on his own account** — directly, or through a **nominee** or relative (*illus. (a):* B buys the estate "in the name of C").
2. The dealing is **in the business of the agency** (he was employed to sell, and buys; employed to buy, and sells his own goods).
3. He did so **without first** obtaining the principal's **consent** after disclosing **all material circumstances** known to him.
4. **Either** (a) a **material fact** was **dishonestly concealed** (*illus. (b):* B finds a **mine** on the estate, conceals it, and buys with A's permission — A may repudiate or adopt), **or** (b) the dealing was **disadvantageous** to the principal (e.g., a sale below market value).

Consent obtained **without full disclosure** is no consent for this purpose (*illus. (b)*).

### 9. Rationale

An agent is a **fiduciary**. As seller for the principal he should get the highest price; as buyer for himself he wants the lowest. "No man can serve two masters." Section 215 removes the temptation by allowing the principal to undo self-dealing unless it was fully disclosed and consented to.

### 10. Related remedies — s. 216 and case law

1. **s. 216** — the principal may claim **any benefit** the agent derived from dealing on his own account (*illus.:* B tells A the house cannot be bought and buys it himself — A may compel B to sell it to him at cost).
2. *Armstrong v. Jackson* ([1917] 2 KB 822) — a broker instructed to buy shares sold the client **his own** shares; the client rescinded and recovered the price.
3. The principal may alternatively **affirm** the transaction (*illus. (b)* — "repudiate or adopt").

### 11. Conclusion

Express authority is given in words; implied authority is inferred from the circumstances, conduct, course of dealing, position, necessity, custom and the incidental needs of the task (ss. 186–189, 211). Where an agent turns his position to his own account — buying or selling for himself in the business of the agency without informed consent — **s. 215** allows the principal to repudiate the transaction if a material fact was dishonestly concealed or the dealing was disadvantageous, and **s. 216** lets him claim the profit.

## Why this answer scores

- It quotes **ss. 186–188 and 215** and uses the **Quetta** and **mine** illustrations from the Pakistani text.
- The "factors" part is answered with a **numbered list** of sources of implied authority, as asked.
- It distinguishes **implied** from **ostensible** authority — a refinement examiners notice.
- It sets out the **elements** of s. 215, its **rationale**, and the companion remedy in **s. 216**.

# Answer 7 · Modes of creation and termination of agency (PU LL.B)

:::glance PUNJAB UNIVERSITY LL.B PART III · LAW OF CONTRACT · 2018 (SUPPLEMENTARY) · Q. 8 · 20 MARKS
What are the different modes of creation and termination of agency?
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](d7_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Two lists, each with sections, illustrations and a case: **creation** — express, implied, estoppel, necessity, ratification (with its conditions); **termination** — by act of the parties and by operation of law, plus **irrevocable** agency (s. 202, s. 204), **compensation and notice** (ss. 205–206) and **when termination takes effect** (s. 208).

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction

An agent is "a person employed to do any act for another or to represent another in dealings with third persons" (s. 182). Any adult of sound mind may employ an agent (s. 183); any person may be an agent as regards third persons (s. 184); and **no consideration** is needed (s. 185).

### 2. Creation (1) — express agency

By **words spoken or written** (s. 187) — e.g., a power of attorney under the **Powers of Attorney Act 1882**. Construed strictly.

### 3. Creation (2) — implied agency

**Inferred from the circumstances**, conduct or course of dealing (s. 187 — the Quetta shop illustration); including **incidental** authority (s. 188) and agency arising from **relationships** (a wife's authority to buy necessaries; partners in a firm).

### 4. Creation (3) — agency by estoppel

Where the principal by **words or conduct** induces third persons to believe that someone is his agent, he is **bound** (s. 237; *illus. (a)* — secret reserve price; *Freeman & Lockyer v. Buckhurst Park Properties*, [1964] 2 QB 480).

### 5. Creation (4) — agency by necessity

In an **emergency**, an agent may do what a prudent man would do to protect the principal from loss (s. 189 — perishable provisions sold at **Lahore**). Conditions: real emergency; **impossibility of communicating** with the principal (*Springer v. Great Western Railway*, [1921] 1 KB 257); good faith; reasonable action (*Great Northern Railway v. Swaffield* (1874) LR 9 Ex 132).

### 6. Creation (5) — agency by ratification

A person may **ratify** acts done on his behalf without authority, with the same effect as if authorised (s. 196). Conditions:

1. the act was done **on behalf of** the principal, who was **in existence** and **competent** at the time (*Kelner v. Baxter* (1866) — no ratification of pre-incorporation contracts; *Keighley, Maxsted & Co. v. Durant*, [1901] AC 240 — undisclosed principal cannot ratify);
2. express or **implied** ratification (s. 197 — selling goods bought for him; accepting interest on a loan);
3. **full knowledge** of the facts (s. 198);
4. ratification of the **whole** transaction (s. 199);
5. not to the **injury of a third person** (s. 200 — unauthorised notice to quit cannot be ratified to bind the tenant);
6. within a reasonable time; of a **lawful** act.

Ratification **relates back** to the date of the act (*Bolton Partners v. Lambert* (1889) 41 Ch D 295).

### 7. Termination — the general rule (s. 201)

"An agency is terminated by the principal **revoking** his authority; or by the agent **renouncing** the business of the agency; or by the business of the agency being **completed**; or by either the principal or agent **dying** or becoming of **unsound mind**; or by the principal being **adjudicated an insolvent**."

### 8. Termination (1) — by act of the parties

1. **Revocation** by the principal — at any time **before** the authority has been exercised so as to bind him (s. 203); express or **implied** (s. 207 — *A empowers B to let his house, then lets it himself*).
2. **Renunciation** by the agent.
3. **Mutual agreement**.

### 9. Termination (2) — by operation of law

1. **Completion** of the business.
2. **Expiry** of the fixed period.
3. **Death** or **insanity** of the principal or agent (on the principal's death or insanity, the agent must still protect the interests entrusted to him — s. 209).
4. **Insolvency** of the principal.
5. **Destruction** of the subject matter, or the agency becoming **unlawful** (e.g., war).
6. Termination of the agent's authority ends **sub-agents'** authority (s. 210).

### 10. Irrevocable agency

1. **Coupled with interest — s. 202.** Where the agent has an **interest in the property** forming the subject matter, the agency cannot, without express contract, be terminated **to the prejudice of that interest** — not even by the principal's death or insanity. *Illus. (a):* authority to sell A's land and pay himself A's debt from the proceeds; *(b):* cotton consigned to a merchant who made advances on it.
2. **Partly exercised — s. 204.** The principal cannot revoke after the authority has been **partly exercised**, as regards acts and obligations already arising. *Illus. (a):* B bought cotton in **his own name** — A cannot revoke B's authority to pay for it; *(b):* bought in A's name — A can revoke.

### 11. Compensation and notice — ss. 205, 206

Where the agency is for a **fixed period**, revocation or renunciation **without sufficient cause** requires **compensation** (s. 205). **Reasonable notice** must be given; otherwise the resulting damage must be made good (s. 206).

### 12. When termination takes effect — s. 208

Termination does not take effect as regards the **agent** before it becomes **known to him**, or as regards **third persons** before it becomes **known to them**. *Illus. (a):* B sells after A's revocation letter is sent but before it reaches him — binding on A, with commission. *Illus. (b):* C, who knew of B's original authority, pays B after secret revocation — good against A. *Illus. (c):* payment by the agent after A's death but before he hears of it — good against the executor.

### 13. Conclusion

An agency is created expressly, impliedly, by estoppel, by necessity or by ratification, without any need for consideration. It ends by revocation, renunciation, completion, death, insanity or insolvency, or by operation of law — but not, to the agent's prejudice, where it is **coupled with an interest** or has been **partly exercised**. Revocation must be on reasonable notice and takes effect only when **known** to the agent and to third persons.

## Why this answer scores

- **Two clear lists**, each item with a section and an illustration.
- The conditions of **ratification** are complete, with the three leading English cases.
- It covers the **exceptions** to termination (ss. 202, 204) and **timing** (s. 208), where most marks are won or lost.

# Answer 8 · Sale and agreement to sell (PU LL.B)

:::glance PUNJAB UNIVERSITY LL.B PART III · LAW OF CONTRACT · 2019 (ANNUAL AND SUPPLEMENTARY), 2020 · Q. 10 · 20 MARKS
Define "sale" and "agreement to sell" and distinguish between the two.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](d8_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Define both under **s. 4**, give the **essentials** of a contract of sale, then **distinguish** on every point — property, nature of rights, risk, remedies, insolvency, resale, perishing — and show how an agreement to sell **becomes** a sale (s. 4(4)) through ss. 18–24. Close with how sale differs from **hire-purchase** and other transactions.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction

"A contract of sale of goods is a contract whereby the seller **transfers or agrees to transfer** the property in goods to the buyer for a price" (s. 4(1), SGA 1930). The words "transfers" and "agrees to transfer" mark the two kinds of contract of sale — a **sale** and an **agreement to sell**.

### 2. Definitions — s. 4(3), (4)

"Where under a contract of sale the property in the goods is **transferred** from the seller to the buyer, the contract is called a **sale**, but where the transfer of the property in the goods is to take place at a **future time** or subject to some **condition** thereafter to be fulfilled, the contract is called an **agreement to sell**." "An agreement to sell **becomes a sale** when the time elapses or the conditions are fulfilled subject to which the property in the goods is to be transferred."

### 3. Essentials of a contract of sale

1. Two parties — buyer and seller (a part-owner may sell to another).
2. **Goods** — movables other than actionable claims and money; in Pakistan including **electricity, water and gas** (s. 2(7)).
3. **Price** — money consideration (s. 2(10)); otherwise barter.
4. Transfer or agreement to transfer of **ownership** ("property" — s. 2(11)).
5. All essentials of a valid contract (s. 3; Contract Act s. 10).

### 4. The differences

|Point|Sale|Agreement to sell|
|Transfer of property|Immediate|Future or conditional|

|Seller's remedy|Suit for the **price** (s. 55(1))|Suit for **damages** (s. 56), unless price is payable on a day certain (s. 55(2))|

|Buyer's insolvency|Seller (if unpaid) may use lien / stoppage (ss. 46–50); otherwise proves for the price|Seller may withhold delivery (s. 46(2))|
|Resale by seller|Second buyer gets title only under s. 30(1) (seller in possession); otherwise the first buyer can follow the goods|Second buyer gets good title; first buyer sues for damages|

### 5. How an agreement to sell becomes a sale

Property passes when the parties **intend** (s. 19), guided by the rules in ss. 20–24: unconditional contract for specific goods in a deliverable state — at the time of contract (s. 20); goods to be put into a deliverable state — when that is done and notified (s. 21); price to be ascertained by weighing — when done and notified (s. 22); unascertained goods — on unconditional appropriation with assent (s. 23); goods on approval — on approval or lapse of time (s. 24). Until then, the contract is an agreement to sell.

### 6. Illustration

On 1 March, Asad agrees to buy "**100 bags of DAP fertiliser** from your Gujranwala godown" from Bilal, delivery on 15 March. The goods are **unascertained** — this is an **agreement to sell** (s. 18). On 10 March Bilal separates 100 bags, marks them "Asad" and Asad's agent approves them — property passes (s. 23) and it becomes a **sale**. If the godown floods on 12 March, the loss falls on **Asad** (s. 26). Had the flood occurred on 5 March, before appropriation, the loss would be **Bilal's**, who must still supply 100 bags from elsewhere.

### 7. Sale distinguished from other transactions

1. **Hire-purchase** — the hirer has an **option** to buy and is not bound to buy; until the last instalment, he is a **bailee** (*Helby v. Matthews*, [1895] AC 471).
2. **Bailment** — possession only; the same goods are returned (Contract Act s. 148).
3. **Barter** — goods for goods; no money price.
4. **Contract for work and labour** — the substance is skill (*Robinson v. Graves*, [1935] 1 KB 579 — a portrait).
5. **Security transactions** in the form of a sale — outside the SGA (s. 66(3)).

### 8. Conclusion

A **sale** transfers ownership at once; an **agreement to sell** promises to transfer it in future or on a condition. The distinction governs **risk, remedies, insolvency** and **third-party rights**. An agreement to sell becomes a sale when the time passes or the condition is fulfilled, under the rules in ss. 18–24.

- It quotes **s. 4** precisely and lists the **essentials**.
- The **ten-point table** covers every difference examiners look for.
- The **illustration** shows the moment of conversion (s. 23) and its effect on **risk** (s. 26).
- It adds the distinction from **hire-purchase** and other transactions, showing breadth.
