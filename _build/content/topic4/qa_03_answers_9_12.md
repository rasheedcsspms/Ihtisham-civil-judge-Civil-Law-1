
# Answer 9 · Nemo dat quod non habet — the principle and its exceptions (KPHC 2026)

:::glance KP HIGH COURT 2026 · CIVIL LAW-I · PART IV (SALE OF GOODS ACT) · Q. 9 · 20 MARKS
Explain in detail the principle and exceptions to the 'Nemo Dat Quod Non Habet' rule, by quoting relevant law from the Sale of Goods Act, 1930.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](d9_map.png)

## Decoding the question

"In detail" and "by quoting relevant law" — so quote **s. 27** in full, explain the **rationale** (the conflict between two innocent parties), and take each exception in turn with its **text, conditions and a case**: estoppel, mercantile agent, joint owner (s. 28), voidable contract (s. 29), seller in possession (s. 30(1)), buyer in possession (s. 30(2)); then the exceptions **under other laws** (finder, pawnee, unpaid seller, court sales). Note the absence of **market overt** in Pakistan.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — the maxim

*Nemo dat quod non habet* — "no one can give what he does not have". A seller who is **not the owner** of goods, and has no authority from the owner, cannot pass ownership to the buyer. The buyer gets **no better title** than the seller had.

### 2. The statutory rule — s. 27

> "Subject to the provisions of this Act and of any other law for the time being in force, where goods are sold by a person who is **not the owner** thereof and who does not sell them **under the authority or with the consent of the owner**, the buyer acquires **no better title** to the goods than the seller had, unless the owner of the goods is by his conduct precluded from denying the seller's authority to sell."

### 3. Rationale — two innocent parties

When a non-owner sells, either the **true owner** or the **innocent buyer** must suffer. The rule favours the owner — **security of property**: a person should not lose his goods without his consent. The exceptions favour the buyer — **security of commercial transactions** — where the owner has **contributed** to the deception (by his conduct or by entrusting the goods), or where trade would otherwise be paralysed.

### 4. Exception 1 — estoppel (s. 27)

The buyer gets a good title where "the owner of the goods is **by his conduct precluded from denying the seller's authority to sell**". The owner must have **represented**, by words or conduct, that the seller was the owner or authorised to sell, and the buyer must have **acted** on it. *Eastern Distributors Ltd. v. Goldring* ([1957] 2 QB 600): an owner who signed documents enabling a dealer to appear as owner of his van was estopped. **Mere possession** by the seller, or the owner's carelessness, is not enough (*Central Newbury Car Auctions v. Unity Finance*, [1957] 1 QB 371).

### 5. Exception 2 — sale by a mercantile agent (s. 27 proviso)

> "Provided that, where a **mercantile agent** is, **with the consent of the owner**, in possession of the goods or of a document of title to the goods, any sale made by him, when acting in the **ordinary course of business** of a mercantile agent, shall be as valid as if he were expressly authorised by the owner of the goods to make the same; provided that the buyer acts in **good faith** and has not at the time of the contract of sale **notice** that the seller has not authority to sell."

**Conditions:** (i) a mercantile agent (s. 2(9)); (ii) in possession of the goods or documents of title **with the owner's consent** — even consent induced by fraud (*Folkes v. King*, [1923] 1 KB 282), but in his **capacity as mercantile agent** (*Pearson v. Rose & Young*, [1951] 1 KB 275); (iii) sale in the **ordinary course** of business; (iv) buyer in **good faith** without notice. The same rule applies to **pledges** by mercantile agents (Contract Act s. 178).

### 6. Exception 3 — sale by one of joint owners (s. 28)

> "If one of several joint owners of goods has the **sole possession** of them **by permission of the co-owners**, the property in the goods is transferred to any person who buys them of such joint owner in good faith and has not at the time of the contract of sale notice that the seller has not authority to sell."

### 7. Exception 4 — sale by a person in possession under a voidable contract (s. 29)

> "When the seller of goods has obtained possession thereof under a contract **voidable under section 19 or section 19A** of the Contract Act, 1872, but the contract **has not been rescinded** at the time of the sale, the buyer acquires a **good title** to the goods, provided he buys them in good faith and without notice of the seller's defect of title."

A seller with a **voidable** title (obtained by fraud, misrepresentation, coercion or undue influence) can pass good title **before** the contract is rescinded (*Phillips v. Brooks*, [1919] 2 KB 243 — the ring obtained by a rogue face to face; *Lewis v. Averay*, [1972] 1 QB 198). Where the contract is **void** — e.g., mistake as to the identity of the buyer in a written contract — the rogue has no title to pass (*Cundy v. Lindsay*, (1878) 3 App Cas 459). The owner can prevent the exception by **rescinding** promptly, even by informing the police where the rogue has absconded (*Car and Universal Finance Co. v. Caldwell*, [1965] 1 QB 525).

### 8. Exception 5 — seller in possession after sale (s. 30(1))

> "Where a person, **having sold goods, continues or is in possession** of the goods or of the documents of title to the goods, the delivery or transfer by that person or by a mercantile agent acting for him, of the goods or documents of title under any **sale, pledge or other disposition** thereof to any person receiving the same in good faith and **without notice of the previous sale** shall have the same effect as if the person making the delivery or transfer were expressly authorised by the owner of the goods to make the same."

The first buyer who left the goods with the seller loses to a second buyer who takes **delivery** in good faith (*Pacific Motor Auctions v. Motor Credits*, [1965] AC 867, PC). The first buyer sues the seller.

### 9. Exception 6 — buyer in possession (s. 30(2))

> "Where a person, **having bought or agreed to buy goods, obtains, with the consent of the seller, possession** of the goods or the documents of title to the goods, the delivery or transfer by that person or by a mercantile agent acting for him, of the goods or documents of title under any sale, pledge or other disposition thereof to any person receiving the same in good faith and without notice of any lien or other right of the original seller in respect of the goods shall have effect as if such lien or right did not exist."

A buyer who has **not yet acquired property** (e.g., the seller reserved title until payment) but has **possession** with the seller's consent can pass a good title to a bona fide purchaser (*Newtons of Wembley v. Williams*, [1965] 1 QB 560). A **hirer** under hire-purchase has not "bought or agreed to buy" and cannot (*Helby v. Matthews*, [1895] AC 471).

### 10. Exceptions under other laws ("subject to … any other law")

1. **Finder of goods** — may sell when the goods are perishing or his charges reach two-thirds of their value (Contract Act s. 169).
2. **Pawnee** — may sell on default after reasonable notice (s. 176); pledges by voidable-title holders are valid (s. 178A).
3. **Unpaid seller** — a buyer on resale after lien or stoppage gets good title against the original buyer (SGA s. 54(3)).
4. **Court and execution sales** — the auction-purchaser takes the judgment-debtor's title (CPC O. XXI).
5. **Negotiable instruments** — a holder in due course gets good title (Negotiable Instruments Act ss. 9, 58).
6. **Official receivers, liquidators and public officers** selling under statutory powers.

### 11. No market overt in Pakistan

English law formerly gave a good title to a buyer in good faith in **market overt** (an open market). The Pakistani SGA has **no** such exception; a buyer from a thief in a public market gets no title.

### 12. Illustration

Rashid entrusts his car to Khalid Motors, a used-car dealer in F-10, to find a buyer at not less than Rs. 5 million. Khalid sells it to Naveed, who pays Rs. 4.8 million in good faith at the showroom. Naveed gets **good title**: Khalid is a **mercantile agent** in possession with the owner's consent, selling in the **ordinary course of business** to a bona fide buyer (s. 27 proviso); Rashid's remedy is against Khalid. But if a thief had stolen the car from Rashid's driveway and sold it to Naveed through the same showroom, **nemo dat** would apply — Naveed would get no title.

### 13. Conclusion

The rule in s. 27 protects **ownership**: a non-owner without authority passes no title. The exceptions protect **commerce** where the owner's own conduct or consent made the fraud possible — estoppel, mercantile agents, joint owners, voidable titles, sellers and buyers in possession (ss. 27–30) — and where other laws authorise sale (finders, pawnees, unpaid sellers, courts). Each exception requires the buyer to act **in good faith** and **without notice**.

## Why this answer scores

- It **quotes** each provision, as the question demands.
- Every exception has its **conditions** and a **leading case**.
- It explains the **rationale** (two innocent parties) that ties the exceptions together.
- The **illustration** contrasts an exception (mercantile agent) with the rule (stolen goods).

# Answer 10 · Delivery — definition, types and rules (KPHC 2026)

:::glance KP HIGH COURT 2026 · CIVIL LAW-I · PART IV (SALE OF GOODS ACT) · Q. 10 · 20 MARKS
How does the Sale of Goods Act, 1930, define 'delivery'? What are its types and different rules?
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](d10_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Three parts: **definition** (s. 2(2) with s. 33); **types** (actual, symbolic, constructive — with s. 36(3)); **rules** (ss. 31–44 — concurrent conditions, part delivery, application, place, time, third persons, reasonable hour, expenses, wrong quantity, instalments, carrier, distant place, examination, acceptance, rejection). Distinguish delivery (**possession**) from transfer of **property**.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction

The seller's main duty is "to **deliver** the goods" and the buyer's "to **accept and pay** for them" (s. 31). "Unless otherwise agreed, delivery of the goods and payment of the price are **concurrent conditions**" (s. 32).

### 2. Definition

"**Delivery**" means "**voluntary transfer of possession** from one person to another" (s. 2(2)). Section 33 adds: "Delivery of goods sold may be made by doing **anything which the parties agree** shall be treated as delivery or which has the effect of **putting the goods in the possession** of the buyer or of any person authorised to hold them on his behalf."

**Elements:** (i) a transfer of **possession** (not ownership); (ii) it must be **voluntary** — goods taken by the buyer without the seller's consent are not delivered; (iii) to the buyer **or his authorised agent**.

### 3. Delivery and transfer of property distinguished

Delivery passes **possession**; the passing of **property** (ownership) is governed by ss. 18–26 and depends on the parties' intention. Property may pass **before** delivery (s. 20 — specific goods in a deliverable state) and delivery may occur **before** property passes (s. 25 — reservation of the right of disposal; goods on approval, s. 24).

### 4. Types of delivery

#### i) Actual delivery

Physical handing over of the goods to the buyer or his agent — the shopkeeper hands over the goods at the counter.

#### ii) Symbolic delivery

Handing over something that gives **control** of the goods: the **keys** of a godown; a **document of title** (bill of lading, railway receipt, warehouse receipt — s. 2(4)); the registration documents and keys of a vehicle.

#### iii) Constructive delivery (attornment)

No physical change, but the person in possession **acknowledges** that he holds the goods **for another**: (a) the seller, after sale, agrees to hold as the buyer's bailee; (b) the buyer, already in possession as bailee, now holds as owner; (c) a third person in possession (a warehouseman) acknowledges to the buyer that he holds for him — "where the goods at the time of sale are in the possession of third persons, there is **no delivery** by seller to buyer **unless and until such third person acknowledges** to the buyer that he holds the goods on his behalf" (s. 36(3)).

### 5. Rules as to delivery

1. **Concurrent conditions** — delivery and payment go together (s. 32).
2. **Part delivery** — in progress of delivery of the whole, it has the effect of delivery of the whole for passing property; but not if intended to sever the part from the whole (s. 34).
3. **Buyer to apply** — apart from express contract, the seller need not deliver until the buyer **applies** (s. 35).
4. **Place** — as agreed; otherwise goods sold are delivered where they are at the time of sale; goods agreed to be sold, where they are at the time of the agreement, or where manufactured or produced (s. 36(1)).
5. **Time** — if the seller must send the goods and no time is fixed, within a **reasonable time** (s. 36(2)).
6. **Goods with a third person** — attornment required; documents of title operate normally (s. 36(3)).
7. **Reasonable hour** — demand or tender at an unreasonable hour may be treated as ineffectual (s. 36(4)).
8. **Expenses** — of putting the goods into a deliverable state are borne by the **seller** (s. 36(5)).
9. **Wrong quantity — s. 37:** short delivery — buyer may reject, or accept and pay pro rata at the contract rate; excess — accept the contract quantity and reject the rest, or (in Pakistan, where separation is **difficult or time consuming** — 1994 amendment) reject the whole; mixed goods — accept the conforming goods and reject the rest, or reject all; all subject to usage, agreement and course of dealing.
10. **Instalments — s. 38:** the buyer is not bound to accept delivery by instalments unless agreed; in instalment contracts, whether a defective instalment is a **repudiation** of the whole or a **severable** breach depends on the terms and circumstances (*Maple Flock Co. v. Universal Furniture Products*, [1934] 1 KB 148).
11. **Delivery to a carrier or wharfinger** — prima facie delivery to the buyer (s. 39(1)); the seller must make a **reasonable contract** with the carrier (s. 39(2)); for **sea transit**, notice to enable the buyer to insure (s. 39(3)).
12. **Distant place** — where the seller agrees to deliver at his risk elsewhere, the buyer still bears the risk of **deterioration necessarily incident** to transit (s. 40).
13. **Examination — s. 41:** the buyer is not deemed to accept until he has had a **reasonable opportunity** to examine the goods.
14. **Acceptance — s. 42:** by intimation, by an act inconsistent with the seller's ownership, or by retention beyond a reasonable time without rejection.
15. **Rejection — s. 43:** the buyer need not return rejected goods; intimation of refusal is enough.
16. **Buyer's failure to take delivery — s. 44:** liable for loss and a reasonable charge for **care and custody**.

### 6. Illustration

Zafar buys 200 bales of cotton lying in Pak Warehouse, Faisalabad, from Yousaf. Yousaf hands Zafar a **delivery order** addressed to the warehouse. Delivery is **not** complete until the warehouse-keeper **acknowledges** to Zafar that he holds the bales for him (s. 36(3)) — unless the delivery order is a **document of title** transferred in the usual way. If Yousaf delivers 220 bales mixed in a way that makes separation **difficult**, Zafar may **reject the whole** (s. 37(2) as amended in Pakistan).

### 7. Conclusion

Delivery is the **voluntary transfer of possession** (s. 2(2)), effected by anything agreed or that puts the goods in the buyer's possession (s. 33) — **actually**, **symbolically** or **constructively** (s. 36(3)). The rules in ss. 32–44 settle the **place, time, manner, quantity, instalments, carriage, examination and acceptance**, and they operate separately from the rules on passing of **property**.

## Why this answer scores

- **Definition** from s. 2(2) and s. 33, with its **elements**.
- Three **types**, each with examples, and s. 36(3) for attornment.
- A complete numbered list of **rules** with sections, including the **Pakistani s. 37(2)** amendment.
- It distinguishes **delivery** from **property** — the conceptual point examiners look for.

# Answer 11 · Caveat emptor — the doctrine and its exceptions (PU LL.B)

:::glance PUNJAB UNIVERSITY LL.B PART III · LAW OF CONTRACT · 2020 (ANNUAL) · Q. 9 · 20 MARKS
What do you understand by the phrase "Doctrine of Caveat Emptor"? Explain the exceptions to this doctrine in the light of relevant provisions of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](d11_map.png)

## Decoding the question

State the **doctrine** (s. 16's opening words), its **rationale**, and then every **exception** — fitness for purpose, merchantable quality, usage, description, sample, title, fraud — with cases. The Pakistani candidate must add **s. 16A** (1994), which imposes a **duty to disclose defects** and reshapes the doctrine in Pakistan.

## The model answer

### 1. Meaning

*Caveat emptor* means "**let the buyer beware**". The buyer must examine the goods and judge their quality and suitability for himself; if he buys goods that turn out defective or unsuitable, he cannot hold the seller responsible — unless the seller gave a warranty, committed fraud, or the law implies a term.

### 2. Statutory basis — s. 16 (opening words)

"Subject to the provisions of this Act and of any other law for the time being in force, there is **no implied warranty or condition as to the quality or fitness** for any particular purpose of goods supplied under a contract of sale, **except as follows** …" The rule is thus stated together with its exceptions.

### 3. Rationale

The buyer is best placed to decide what he wants and to inspect what he buys; the seller cannot be an insurer of every buyer's purpose. The rule promotes **certainty** and **self-reliance** in commerce. But modern trade — complex goods, latent defects, reliance on sellers' expertise — has led the law to **qualify** it heavily.

### 4. Exception 1 — fitness for a particular purpose — s. 16(1)

Where the buyer **makes known** (expressly or by implication) the **particular purpose**, so as to show that he **relies on the seller's skill or judgment**, and the goods are of a description the seller **deals in**, there is an implied condition that they are **reasonably fit** for that purpose. *Priest v. Last* ([1903] 2 KB 148 — a hot-water bottle that burst); *Frost v. Aylesbury Dairy* ([1905] 1 KB 608 — milk with typhoid germs; strict liability).

**Proviso — trade name:** no implied condition of fitness for a specified article sold under its **patent or trade name** — but only where the buyer does **not rely** on the seller (*Baldry v. Marshall*, [1925] 1 KB 260 — reliance on the seller's recommendation of a Bugatti).

### 5. Exception 2 — merchantable quality — s. 16(2)

Where goods are bought **by description** from a seller who **deals** in such goods, there is an implied condition that they are of **merchantable quality** — saleable as goods of that description at the normal price. If the buyer **examined** the goods, there is no condition as to defects that examination **ought to have revealed**; but **latent** defects remain covered (*Grant v. Australian Knitting Mills*, [1936] AC 85, PC — woollen underwear with hidden sulphites).

### 6. Exception 3 — usage of trade — s. 16(3)

An implied condition or warranty as to quality or fitness may be annexed by **usage of trade**.

### 7. Exception 4 — sale by description — s. 15

Goods sold by description must **correspond with the description** (*Varley v. Whipp*, [1900] 1 QB 513; *Beale v. Taylor*, [1967] 1 WLR 1193). If by sample and description, they must correspond with both.

### 8. Exception 5 — sale by sample — s. 17

Implied conditions that the bulk corresponds with the sample, that the buyer has a reasonable opportunity to compare, and that the goods are free from defects rendering them unmerchantable that would not be apparent on reasonable examination of the sample (*Drummond v. Van Ingen* (1887) 12 App Cas 284).

### 9. Exception 6 — Pakistan's s. 16A: the duty to disclose defects

> "16A. **Notwithstanding anything contained in section 16**, and save where the parties have entered into an agreement to the contrary, the seller shall be under an **obligation to inform the buyer of any defect** in the goods sold at the time of the contract, except in a case where the defect is **obviously known** to the buyer." (Act XVIII of 1994.)

1. It applies "**notwithstanding**" s. 16 — it overrides the general rule of caveat emptor as to **disclosure**.
2. The seller must **inform** the buyer of defects **at the time of the contract** — silence is no longer safe for a Pakistani seller.
3. **Exceptions:** an **agreement to the contrary** (e.g., an "as is where is" sale), and defects **obviously known** to the buyer.
4. **Effect:** non-disclosure is a breach of a statutory duty, giving the buyer a remedy in damages (and rejection where the defect goes to the root), and is strong evidence of **fraud by concealment** (Contract Act s. 17).
5. It brings the law into line with the Islamic principle of **khiyar al-'aib** (option for defect).

### 10. Exception 7 — fraud, misrepresentation and active concealment

Caveat emptor never protects a seller who **misrepresents** the goods or **actively conceals** defects (Contract Act ss. 17–19, applied by SGA s. 3). *Illustration (d) to s. 19, Contract Act:* concealment of a vein of ore makes the contract voidable.

### 11. Exception 8 — implied terms as to title — s. 14

Caveat emptor applies to **quality**, not **title**: the seller impliedly undertakes that he has a **right to sell**, and warrants **quiet possession** and freedom from **encumbrances** (*Rowland v. Divall*, [1923] 2 KB 500).

### 12. Exclusion

Implied terms may be excluded by **express agreement**, **course of dealing** or **usage** (s. 62), but exclusion clauses are construed **strictly** (*Wallis v. Pratt*, [1911] AC 394 — excluding "warranties" does not exclude conditions), and consumer protection laws may forbid unfair exclusions.

### 13. Conclusion

*Caveat emptor* remains the starting point (s. 16): the law implies no term as to quality or fitness. But the exceptions — fitness for a made-known purpose, merchantable quality, usage, description, sample, title and fraud — have reduced it to a rule for the **careless** buyer who neither relies on the seller nor inspects. In **Pakistan**, **s. 16A** goes further and requires the seller to **disclose defects**, so that the maxim is better stated as *caveat venditor* — let the **seller** beware — wherever he knows of a defect.

## Why this answer scores

- It states the doctrine **from the statute** and gives its **rationale**.
- Every exception has its **section** and **leading case**.
- It highlights **s. 16A** — the Pakistani provision most candidates do not know.
- The conclusion **evaluates** the doctrine's current strength rather than just listing.

# Answer 12 · Who is an unpaid seller? His rights against the goods (PU LL.B)

:::glance PUNJAB UNIVERSITY LL.B PART III · LAW OF CONTRACT · 2018 AND 2019 (SUPPLEMENTARY) · Q. 10 · 20 MARKS
Who is an "unpaid seller"? What are his rights against the goods provided in the Sale of Goods Act?
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](d12_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Define the **unpaid seller** (s. 45); then his rights **against the goods** (s. 46) — **lien** (ss. 47–49), **stoppage in transit** (ss. 50–52), **withholding delivery** (s. 46(2)), **resale** (s. 54) — with conditions, termination, and the effect of a **sub-sale or pledge** by the buyer (s. 53). A comparison table of lien and stoppage, and a brief note of his personal remedies, finishes the answer.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction

A seller who has parted with his goods, or agreed to, but has not been paid, would be at the mercy of the buyer's solvency if his only remedy were a suit for the price. The SGA gives him **real rights** over the goods themselves.

### 2. Who is an unpaid seller — s. 45

The seller is deemed an "unpaid seller" — (a) "when the **whole of the price has not been paid or tendered**"; (b) "when a **bill of exchange or other negotiable instrument** has been received as **conditional payment**, and the condition on which it was received has not been fulfilled by reason of the **dishonour** of the instrument or otherwise" (s. 45(1)). "Seller" includes any person **in the position of a seller** — an agent to whom the bill of lading has been endorsed, or a consignor or agent who has paid or is responsible for the price (s. 45(2)). Part payment does not end his status.

### 3. His rights — s. 46

"**Notwithstanding that the property in the goods may have passed** to the buyer, the unpaid seller of goods, as such, has by implication of law— (a) a **lien** on the goods for the price while he is in possession of them; (b) in case of the **insolvency** of the buyer a right of **stopping the goods in transit** after he has parted with the possession of them; (c) a right of **re-sale** as limited by this Act" (s. 46(1)). "Where the property in goods has **not passed** to the buyer, the unpaid seller has, in addition to his other remedies, a right of **withholding delivery** similar to and co-extensive with his rights of lien and stoppage in transit" (s. 46(2)).

### 4. Right 1 — lien (ss. 47–49)

1. **When — s. 47(1):** the unpaid seller **in possession** may retain the goods until payment or tender of the price (a) where sold **without any stipulation as to credit**; (b) where sold on credit but the **credit has expired**; (c) where the **buyer becomes insolvent**.
2. Even if he holds as the buyer's **agent or bailee** (s. 47(2)).
3. **Part delivery** — lien on the remainder unless the part delivery shows a waiver (s. 48).
4. **Loss of lien — s. 49:** (a) delivery to a **carrier** for transmission to the buyer **without reserving the right of disposal**; (b) the buyer or his agent **lawfully obtains possession**; (c) **waiver**. Not lost merely by obtaining a **decree for the price** (s. 49(2)).
5. It is for the **price only**, not for storage or other charges.

### 5. Right 2 — stoppage in transit (ss. 50–52)

1. **When — s. 50:** the **buyer becomes insolvent**, and the unpaid seller who has **parted with possession** may **resume possession** while the goods are **in transit**, and retain them until payment.
2. **Duration of transit — s. 51:** from delivery to a carrier until the **buyer or his agent takes delivery**; ends on delivery before arrival, or on the carrier's **acknowledgment** after arrival that he holds for the buyer; continues if the buyer **rejects** the goods; ends if the carrier **wrongfully refuses** delivery; after part delivery, the rest may be stopped.
3. **How — s. 52:** by taking **actual possession**, or by **notice** to the carrier (or his principal, in time to prevent delivery); the carrier must **re-deliver** to the seller at the seller's expense.

### 6. Lien and stoppage compared

{w:22,39,39}
|Point|Lien|Stoppage in transit|
|Possession|Seller keeps it|Seller has parted with it and resumes it|
|Buyer's insolvency|Not required|Essential|
|Starts / ends|Ends when goods go to the carrier or buyer|Begins where lien ends; ends when transit ends|
|Nature|Right to retain|Right to regain|

### 7. Right 3 — withholding delivery (s. 46(2))

Where property has **not passed**, the unpaid seller may simply **withhold** delivery, with rights co-extensive with lien and stoppage.

### 8. Effect of a sub-sale or pledge by the buyer — s. 53

The seller's lien or stoppage is **not affected** by the buyer's sale or other disposition unless the seller **assented** (s. 53(1)). **Except:** where a **document of title** has been lawfully transferred to the buyer and he transfers it to a person who takes **in good faith and for consideration** — a **sale** defeats the seller's right; a **pledge** leaves it exercisable only **subject to the pledgee's rights**, and the seller may require the pledgee first to resort to the buyer's other securities (s. 53(2)).

### 9. Right 4 — resale (s. 54)

1. **No rescission** by lien or stoppage alone (s. 54(1)).
2. Where the goods are **perishable**, or the seller gives **notice** of intention to resell and the buyer does not pay within a **reasonable time**, the seller may **resell** and recover **damages** for any loss; the buyer gets **no profit** (s. 54(2)).
3. If **no notice** is given (for non-perishables), the seller **cannot** recover damages and the buyer is entitled to any **profit** (s. 54(2)).
4. The **second buyer** gets a **good title** against the original buyer, notice or not (s. 54(3)).
5. Where the seller **expressly reserved** a right of resale on default and resells, the original contract is **rescinded**, without prejudice to damages (s. 54(4)).

### 10. Personal remedies (in brief)

Besides rights against the goods, the unpaid seller may sue for the **price** (s. 55), for **damages for non-acceptance** (s. 56), for damages on **anticipatory repudiation** (s. 60), and for **interest** (s. 61).

### 11. Illustration

Sadiq sells 500 bales of cloth to Rehman on 30 days' credit and sends them by goods train. Rehman becomes **insolvent** while the goods are in transit. Sadiq has **no lien** (he parted with possession to the carrier — s. 49(1)(a)) but may **stop the goods in transit** by notice to the railway (ss. 50–52). He then gives Rehman **notice of resale** and, if not paid within a reasonable time, resells and claims any shortfall as damages (s. 54(2)); the new buyer gets good title (s. 54(3)).

### 12. Conclusion

An unpaid seller is one who has not received or been tendered the **whole price**, or whose negotiable instrument has been **dishonoured** (s. 45). Against the goods he has a **lien** while in possession, a right of **stoppage in transit** on the buyer's insolvency, a right to **withhold** delivery where property has not passed, and a limited right of **resale** — rights that survive even the passing of property, and are defeated only by his own assent or by a bona fide transfer of a **document of title**.
