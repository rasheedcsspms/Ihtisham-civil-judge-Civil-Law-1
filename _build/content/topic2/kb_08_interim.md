# Chapter 8 · Interim relief: arrest and attachment before judgment, temporary injunctions and receivers (s. 94; Orders XXXVIII to XL)

## 8.1 Why interim relief?

A suit can take years. If the defendant can, in the meantime, sell the disputed land, demolish the building, run away or hide his assets, the final decree will be **worthless**. Interim (interlocutory) relief keeps the **subject matter safe** and the **position fair** until the court decides. It is "supplemental" to the suit.

> "94. In order to prevent the ends of justice from being defeated the Court may, if it is so prescribed,— (a) issue a warrant to arrest the defendant and bring him before the Court to show cause why he should not give security for his appearance …; (b) direct the defendant to furnish security to produce any property belonging to him … or order the attachment of any property; (c) grant a temporary injunction and in case of disobedience commit the person guilty thereof to the civil prison and order that his property be attached and sold; (d) appoint a receiver of any property …; (e) make such other interlocutory orders as may appear to the Court to be just and convenient."

The **detailed rules** are in Orders XXXVIII (arrest and attachment before judgment), XXXIX (temporary injunctions and interlocutory orders) and XL (receivers). Where a matter is not covered by these rules, the court may act under its **inherent power (s. 151)** — e.g., to grant an injunction in a case not falling within O. XXXIX rr. 1–2 (*Manohar Lal Chopra v. Seth Hiralal*, AIR 1962 SC 527).

### Compensation for wrongful interim orders — s. 95

If an arrest, attachment or temporary injunction was obtained on **insufficient grounds**, or the suit **fails** and there was **no reasonable or probable ground** for it, the court may award the defendant compensation **up to Rs. 10,000** (within its pecuniary limits); the order bars a separate suit for that compensation (s. 95(2)). An order under s. 95 is appealable (s. 104(1)(g)).

## 8.2 Arrest before judgment — O. XXXVIII rr. 1 to 4

Where at any stage of a suit (other than a suit relating to immovable property under s. 16(a)–(d)) the court is satisfied, by affidavit or otherwise, that the defendant, **with intent to delay the plaintiff, avoid process or obstruct execution**, (i) has absconded or left the local limits, (ii) is about to do so, or (iii) has disposed of or removed his property from the local limits — or (b) is **about to leave Pakistan** in circumstances suggesting the decree will be obstructed — the court may issue a **warrant** to arrest him and bring him to show cause why he should not furnish **security for his appearance** (r. 1). He is not arrested if he pays the amount specified in the warrant. If he fails to show cause, he must deposit money / property or give security (r. 2); in default he may be **committed to civil prison** until the decision — but **not more than six months** (six weeks if the claim is not over fifty rupees) (r. 4).

## 8.3 Attachment before judgment — O. XXXVIII rr. 5 to 13

### When

> "5. (1) Where, at any stage of a suit, the Court is satisfied, by affidavit or otherwise, that the defendant, **with intent to obstruct or delay the execution of any decree** that may be passed against him— (a) is about to dispose of the whole or any part of his property, or (b) is about to remove the whole or any part of his property from the local limits of the jurisdiction of the Court, the Court may direct the defendant, within a time to be fixed by it, either to furnish security … or to appear and show cause why he should not furnish security."

### Procedure and effect

1. The plaintiff **specifies the property** and its estimated value (r. 5(2)); the court may order **conditional attachment** at once (r. 5(3)).
2. If the defendant fails to show cause or furnish security, the property is **attached** (r. 6(1)); if he does, the attachment is withdrawn (r. 6(2)).
3. Attachment is made in the manner of attachment in execution (r. 7); claims to the property are investigated like execution claims (r. 8).
4. Attachment is **withdrawn** when security is furnished or the suit is **dismissed** (r. 9).
5. It does **not affect the prior rights of strangers**, nor bar other decree-holders from seeking sale (r. 10).
6. After decree, **no re-attachment** is needed (r. 11).
7. **Agricultural produce** in an agriculturist's possession cannot be attached before judgment (r. 12).

:::case Raman Tech. & Process Engineering Co. v. Solanki Traders ((2008) 2 SCC 302)
__Held.__ The power to order attachment before judgment is a **drastic and extraordinary** power. It should not be exercised mechanically or merely for the asking, nor used as a lever to coerce the defendant into settling. The court must be satisfied, on specific facts (not vague allegations), that the defendant is **about to remove or dispose of his property with intent to obstruct or delay execution**. A mere possibility that the defendant may dispose of property, or that he is in financial difficulty, is not enough.

__Ratio.__ Attachment before judgment is a **protective** measure against a dishonest defendant, not a security for every plaintiff with a money claim.
:::

## 8.4 Temporary injunctions — O. XXXIX rr. 1 and 2

### Meaning

An **injunction** is a judicial order directing a party to **do** (mandatory) or **refrain from doing** (prohibitory) a particular act. A **temporary** (interim) injunction lasts until a specified time or until further order; a **perpetual** injunction is granted by the final decree. Temporary injunctions are "regulated by the Code of Civil Procedure" (s. 53, Specific Relief Act 1877); perpetual and mandatory injunctions by ss. 54–57 of the Specific Relief Act (Civil Law-II).

### When an injunction may be granted — r. 1

> "1. Where in any suit it is proved by affidavit or otherwise— (a) that any property in dispute in a suit is in danger of being wasted, damaged or alienated by any party to the suit, or wrongfully sold in execution of a decree, or (b) that the defendant threatens, or intends, to remove or dispose of his property with a view to defraud his creditors, the Court may by order grant a temporary injunction to restrain such act, or make such other order for the purpose of staying and preventing the wasting, damaging, alienation, sale, removal or disposition of the property as the Court thinks fit, until the disposal of the suit or until further orders."

### Injunction against breach — r. 2

> "2. (1) In any suit for restraining the defendant from committing a breach of contract or other injury of any kind, whether compensation is claimed in the suit or not, the plaintiff may, at any time after the commencement of the suit, and either before or after judgment, apply to the Court for a temporary injunction to restrain the defendant from committing the breach of contract or injury complained of, or any breach of contract or injury of a like kind arising out of the same contract or relating to the same property or right."

## 8.5 The three ingredients — the heart of every injunction answer

The rules speak of property "in danger" and "injury"; the courts, through long practice, have distilled **three ingredients**, all of which must be shown (SHC 2022 asked exactly this):

![The three ingredients of a temporary injunction — all must be shown](injunction_triad.png)

1. **Prima facie case** — the plaintiff must show a **serious question to be tried**, i.e., a case which, on the material on record, is likely to succeed, or at least is not frivolous or vexatious. The court does **not** decide the merits finally; it makes a tentative assessment on the pleadings, documents and affidavits.
2. **Balance of convenience** — the court compares the **inconvenience** (hardship, loss) that each party would suffer if the injunction were granted or refused, and leans towards the side where refusing would cause **greater** harm.
3. **Irreparable loss** — the plaintiff would suffer an injury that **cannot be adequately compensated in money** (e.g., demolition of a house, loss of a unique property, sale to a third party, loss of goodwill, disruption of children's schooling), if the injunction is refused.

:::case American Cyanamid Co. v. Ethicon Ltd. ([1975] AC 396, House of Lords)
__Facts.__ A patent holder sought an interim injunction to stop a competitor from selling surgical sutures alleged to infringe its patent.

__Held.__ Lord Diplock rejected the old requirement that the plaintiff show a "strong prima facie case" (probability of success). The court need only be satisfied that there is a **serious question to be tried**. Then it considers whether **damages** would be an adequate remedy for either side; if there is doubt, it weighs the **balance of convenience**, and where other factors are evenly balanced, it is prudent to **preserve the status quo**.

__Ratio.__ An interlocutory injunction is about **managing the risk of injustice** before trial, not about deciding the merits; the court asks (1) serious question, (2) adequacy of damages, (3) balance of convenience, (4) status quo.
:::

:::case Dalpat Kumar v. Prahlad Singh ((1992) 1 SCC 719)
__Held.__ The satisfaction that there is a **prima facie case** does not mean that the plaintiff must prove his title — only that there is a bona fide dispute raised which needs investigation and decision on merits. **Irreparable injury** means injury that is material and cannot be adequately compensated by damages. **Balance of convenience** requires the court to weigh the comparative mischief or inconvenience likely to arise from granting or withholding the injunction. The three are **sine qua non**; the grant is discretionary and the court must also consider the **conduct** of the party.
:::

:::case Wander Ltd. v. Antox India (P) Ltd. (1990 Supp SCC 727)
__Held.__ An appellate court will **not interfere** with the trial court's discretion in granting or refusing an interlocutory injunction merely because it would have taken a different view; it interferes only if the discretion was exercised **arbitrarily, capriciously, perversely**, or ignoring settled principles.
:::

### Further principles

- **Equitable and discretionary:** *he who seeks equity must do equity* and *come with clean hands* — a plaintiff who suppresses facts, delays (laches) or acted wrongfully may be refused even if the triad is shown.
- **Status quo:** where the triad is evenly balanced, the court preserves the **existing position**.
- **No injunction to give the final relief at the interim stage**, especially a **mandatory** interim injunction, except in exceptional cases — e.g., to restore a status quo wrongfully disturbed just before or after the suit (*Dorab Cawasji Warden v. Coomi Sorab Warden*, (1990) 2 SCC 117: a mandatory interim injunction requires a **strong** prima facie case, irreparable injury and balance of convenience, and is granted only in rare cases).
- **Terms:** the court may impose terms — undertaking for damages, security, keeping accounts (r. 2(2)).
- **Injunctions against Government / statutory bodies:** notice is compulsory (r. 3 proviso), and injunctions in public-revenue matters lapse after six months (r. 4A).

## 8.6 Notice, time limits and disobedience — the Pakistani rules

:::ict O. XXXIX rr. 2(3)–(4), 2A, 2B, 3 and 4A
**Notice — r. 3:** "The Court shall **in all cases**, before granting an injunction, direct notice of the application for the same to be given to the opposite party." The court may dispense with notice where the **object would be defeated by delay** — **except** (i) where goods pledged with a **bank** are being sold for default, and (ii) where the injunction is sought against **Government, a Government servant as such, or any statutory authority, board or corporation** (unless the case involves **ejectment** from, or **demolition** of, premises). Notice to Government or such bodies must be **not less than two days nor more than seven days**.

**Ex parte injunction — r. 2A (1994):** "An interim injunction passed under rules 1 or 2 in the absence of defendant shall **not ordinarily exceed fifteen days**", extendable if service fails for reasons not attributable to the plaintiff, or the defendant seeks time.

**Life of an injunction after hearing — r. 2B (1994):** an injunction made after hearing (or after notice) "shall **cease to have effect on the expiration of six months** unless extended by the Court after hearing the parties again and for reasons to be recorded"; a **report of such extension** must be submitted to the **High Court**.

**Public revenue — r. 4A:** an injunction in a suit questioning action under laws in Part I of the First Schedule to the Constitution, or relating to **assessment or collection of public revenues** (including dues of Government-owned banks and corporations), **ceases after six months** unless the case is decided or the injunction set aside earlier.

**Disobedience — r. 2(3)–(4):** the court may **attach the property** of the person guilty of disobedience or breach and **detain him in civil prison for up to six months**; an attachment cannot remain beyond **one year**, after which the property may be sold and compensation paid.
:::

:::why Why notice and time limits?
An injunction restrains a person before he is found to have done anything wrong. **Notice** respects *audi alteram partem*; **short ex parte injunctions** prevent abuse of one-sided orders; the **six-month review** stops interim orders from becoming permanent through delay; special protection for Government and public revenue prevents the paralysis of public functions by private suits.
:::

:::case Morgan Stanley Mutual Fund v. Kartick Das ((1994) 4 SCC 225)
__Held.__ An **ex parte** injunction should be granted only in exceptional circumstances. The court should consider: (a) whether irreparable or serious mischief will ensue if not granted; (b) whether refusal would involve greater injustice than grant; (c) the time when the plaintiff first noticed the act (delay is fatal); (d) whether the plaintiff acquiesced; (e) whether the applicant made a **full and fair disclosure** of all material facts (utmost good faith); (f) even if granted, it should be for a **limited period**; and (g) general principles of prima facie case, balance of convenience and irreparable loss.
:::

## 8.7 Other provisions of Order XXXIX

- **r. 4:** any order for an injunction may be **discharged, varied or set aside** on application by a dissatisfied party (e.g., on changed circumstances or suppression of facts).
- **r. 5:** an injunction to a **corporation** binds its members and officers whose personal action it restrains.
- **r. 6:** **interim sale** of movable property subject to speedy decay, or for other just cause.
- **r. 7:** detention, preservation or **inspection** of the subject matter; authority to enter land, take samples or make experiments.
- **r. 10:** where a party **admits** holding money or a thing as trustee for, or due to, another party, the court may order it **deposited in court** or delivered.
- **Appeal:** orders under **O. XXXIX rr. 1, 2, 4 and 10** are appealable — **O. XLIII r. 1(r)**.

## 8.8 Cases in which an injunction cannot be granted (SHC 2022 short note)

Section **56 of the Specific Relief Act 1877** lists cases in which an injunction **cannot** be granted. Courts apply the same principles at the interim stage. An injunction cannot be granted:

1. **(a)** to stay a judicial proceeding **pending at the institution of the suit**, unless necessary to prevent a multiplicity of proceedings;
2. **(b)** to stay proceedings in a court **not subordinate** to the court granting the injunction;
3. **(c)** to restrain a person from applying to a **legislative body**;
4. **(d)** to interfere with the **public duties** of any department of Government, or with the sovereign acts of a foreign government;
5. **(e)** to stay proceedings in a **criminal** matter;
6. **(f)** to prevent the breach of a contract whose performance **would not be specifically enforced**;
7. **(g)** to prevent, on the ground of nuisance, an act of which it is **not reasonably clear** that it will be a nuisance;
8. **(h)** to prevent a continuing breach in which the applicant has **acquiesced**;
9. **(i)** when **equally efficacious relief** can certainly be obtained by any other usual mode of proceeding (except in breach of trust);
10. **(j)** when the **conduct** of the applicant or his agents has been such as to disentitle him to the assistance of the court;
11. **(k)** where the applicant has **no personal interest** in the matter.

(Section 57: where a contract contains a **negative agreement**, an injunction may be granted to enforce it even though the positive part cannot be specifically enforced — the *Lumley v. Wagner* principle.)

:::tip How to write an injunction order (LHC 2017 question)
Use this skeleton: (1) title and nature of the application (O. XXXIX rr. 1–2 read with s. 151); (2) brief facts of plaint and application; (3) reply; (4) **law** — three ingredients with one case; (5) **prima facie case** — discuss documents; (6) **balance of convenience** — compare hardships; (7) **irreparable loss** — why money cannot compensate; (8) any **bar** (s. 56 SRA; O. XXXIX r. 3 / r. 4A for Government); (9) **conclusion**: application accepted / dismissed; terms; "observations are tentative and shall not affect the trial"; (10) date, signature. See Chapter 18 and the Q&A volume for a full specimen.
:::

## 8.9 Receivers — Order XL

> "1. (1) Where it appears to the Court to be **just and convenient**, the Court may by order— (a) appoint a receiver of any property, whether before or after decree; (b) remove any person from the possession or custody of the property; (c) commit the same to the possession, custody or management of the receiver; and (d) confer upon the receiver all such powers, as to bringing and defending suits and for the realization, management, protection, preservation and improvement of the property, the collection of the rents and profits thereof, the application and disposal of such rents and profits, and the execution of documents as the owner himself has, or such of those powers as the Court thinks fit. (2) Nothing in this rule shall authorise the Court to remove from the possession or custody of property any person whom any party to the suit has not a present right so to remove."

### Grounds and principles for appointing a receiver (SHC 2022 short note)

1. The appointment is **discretionary** and a **drastic** remedy, because it takes property out of a party's hands.
2. The plaintiff must show a **strong prima facie case** of title or interest in the property.
3. The property must be in **danger** — of waste, mismanagement, dissipation or loss — so that it is "just and convenient" to protect it.
4. A person in **bona fide possession** under a claim of right (e.g., a long-standing co-owner or a tenant) should not ordinarily be dispossessed, especially where possession is old and the plaintiff's title is doubtful (r. 1(2) also prevents removing anyone whom no party has a present right to remove).
5. The plaintiff's own **conduct** (delay, acquiescence) matters.

(These are the classic five principles of *T. Krishnaswamy Chetty v. C. Thangavelu Chetty*, AIR 1955 Mad 430, often followed in Pakistan.)

### Duties, remuneration and liability — rr. 2 to 5

- **r. 2:** the court fixes the receiver's **remuneration**.
- **r. 3 — duties:** furnish **security**; submit **accounts** as directed; **pay** amounts due as directed; and be **responsible for loss** caused by his **wilful default or gross negligence**.
- **r. 4:** if he fails, the court may **attach and sell his property** to make good the loss.
- **r. 5:** the **Collector** may be appointed receiver of revenue-paying land, with his consent.
- A receiver is an **officer of the court**; his possession is the court's possession. He cannot be sued for acts done as receiver **without leave** of the court, and interference with his possession is contempt.
- **Appeal:** orders under **O. XL rr. 1 and 4** are appealable — **O. XLIII r. 1(s)**.

:::maxim He who seeks equity must do equity; he who comes into equity must come with clean hands
Injunctions and receivers are **equitable** reliefs. A plaintiff who hides material facts, is guilty of delay, or acted wrongfully himself will be refused, even if he has a prima facie case.
:::

:::maxim Status quo ante — the existing position before
Where the court is in doubt, it preserves the position existing **before** the dispute (or the last peaceful, uncontested position), so that neither side gains an advantage while the case is pending.
:::
