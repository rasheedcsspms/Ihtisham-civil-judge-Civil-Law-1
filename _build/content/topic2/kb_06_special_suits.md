# Chapter 6 · Suits in particular cases (ss. 79 to 93; Orders XXVII to XXXVI)

## 6.1 Suits by or against Government — ss. 79 to 82, Order XXVII

### Naming the Government — s. 79

> "In a suit by or against the Government the authority to be named as plaintiff or defendant, as the case may be, shall be— (a) in the case of a suit by or against the Federal Government, **Pakistan**; (b) in the case of a suit by or against a Provincial Government, **the Province**."

So a suit against the Federal Government is titled "X v. **Federation of Pakistan** (through Secretary, Ministry of …)", and against a province "X v. **Province of Punjab** (through …)". Suing "the Secretary" or "the Ministry" alone is a defect of form, curable by amendment.

### The notice — s. 80 (the Pakistani text)

:::ict Section 80 CPC — as substituted by Ordinance XLIV of 1962
> "80. (1) A suit may be instituted against the Government or against a public officer, in respect of any act purporting to be done by such public officer in his official capacity, after the expiration of two months next after notice in writing has been delivered to or left at the office of,— (a) in the case of a suit against the Federal Government, a Secretary to that Government; (b)(i) in the case of a suit against the Provincial Government …, a Secretary to that Government or the Collector of the District; …and in the case of a public officer, delivered to him or left at his office stating the cause of action, the name, description of place of residence of the plaintiff and the relief which he claims; and the plaint shall contain a statement that such notice has been so delivered or left.
> (2) Where any such suit is instituted without delivering or leaving such notice as aforesaid or before the expiration of the said period of two months or where the plaint does not contain a statement that such notice has been so delivered or left, **the plaintiff shall not be entitled to any costs if settlement as regards the subject-matter of the suit is reached or the Government or the public officer concedes the plaintiff's claim, within the period of two months from the date of the institution of the suit**:
> Provided that in a suit instituted without such notice, the Court shall allow **not less than three months** to the Government to submit its written statement."

**What this means:** In **India**, s. 80 is **mandatory** — a suit filed without the two-month notice is **not maintainable** and the plaint is rejected (*Bihari Chowdhary v. State of Bihar*, (1984) 2 SCC 627). In **Pakistan**, since 1962, sub-section (2) itself provides the consequences of not giving notice: the suit is **maintainable**, but (i) the plaintiff **loses costs** if the Government settles or concedes within two months of the suit, and (ii) the court must give the Government **at least three months** to file its written statement. Therefore, **want of s. 80 notice is not a ground to reject a plaint** in Pakistan.
:::

:::why Why notice to Government at all?
The State acts through many officers; a two-month notice gives it the chance to **examine the claim and settle it** without litigation, saving public money and court time. Pakistan keeps this purpose but refuses to let a technical omission defeat a just claim — the plaintiff simply loses costs if Government would have settled.
:::

:::trap Do not confuse with special statutes
Some special laws impose their **own** notice requirements, which may be mandatory (and conditions precedent to suit) under those laws. Section 80's Pakistani rule applies to the general notice under the CPC. Also distinguish the **forum** rule in s. 24 of the Civil Courts Ordinance (suits against Government only before a Civil Judge with unlimited pecuniary jurisdiction — Topic 1).
:::

### Other privileges of Government — ss. 81–82, Order XXVII

- **s. 81:** a public officer sued for an official act is **not liable to arrest**, nor his property to **attachment**, except in execution of a decree; and the court shall **exempt him from personal appearance** if he cannot leave his duty without detriment to the public service.
- **s. 82:** a decree against Government or a public officer (for an official act) must specify a **time** for satisfaction; if unsatisfied, the court **reports** to Government, and **execution cannot issue until three months** after the report.
- **O. XXVII r. 1:** pleadings signed and verified by persons appointed by Government; **r. 2:** authorised persons are recognised agents; **r. 3:** use the name in s. 79; **r. 4:** the **Government pleader** receives processes; **r. 5:** a **reasonable time** is fixed for Government to answer; **r. 7:** extension of time for a public officer to make a reference to Government; **r. 8:** where Government undertakes the defence of a public officer, the Government pleader appears; **r. 8A:** **no security** is required from Government under O. XLI rr. 5–6 (stay pending appeal).
- **Order XXVII-A:** where a **substantial question as to the interpretation of constitutional law** is involved, the court shall not decide it until **notice** is given to the **Attorney General for Pakistan** (federal questions) or the **Advocate General** (provincial questions); Government may be added as a defendant (r. 2).
- **s. 35C:** Government is not liable for actual costs, adjournment costs or special costs under ss. 35, 35A and 35B (Topic 1).

## 6.2 Aliens and foreign States — ss. 83 to 87

- **s. 83:** **alien friends**, and alien enemies residing in Pakistan **with the permission** of the Federal Government, may sue as if they were citizens; other alien enemies may not sue.
- **s. 84:** a **foreign State** may sue in Pakistan (to enforce private rights vested in its Head or officers in their public capacity), if recognised by the Federal Government.
- **ss. 86–87** (suits against foreign rulers) were **repealed** by the **State Immunity Ordinance 1981**, which now governs when a foreign State is immune from the jurisdiction of Pakistani courts (immunity is the rule; commercial transactions and certain other matters are exceptions).

## 6.3 Corporations, firms and trustees — Orders XXIX to XXXI

- **O. XXIX (corporations):** pleadings may be **signed and verified** by the secretary, any director or other principal officer able to depose to the facts (r. 1); summons may be served on the secretary, director or principal officer, or left at the registered office (r. 2); the court may require the personal appearance of an officer (r. 3).
- **O. XXX (firms):** two or more persons carrying on business in Pakistan as partners may **sue or be sued in the firm name** (r. 1); the plaintiff may demand **disclosure of the partners' names** (r. 2); service on a partner or the person managing the business (r. 3); a partner's death does not require joining his legal representative (r. 4); a person served as **manager** may be told the capacity in which he is served (r. 5); partners appear individually but proceedings continue in the firm name (r. 6); a person served as partner may appear **under protest** denying he is a partner (r. 8); **suits between a firm and a partner** or between firms with common partners are allowed (r. 9); a person carrying on business in a name other than his own may be sued in that name (r. 10). A decree against a firm is executed against firm property and against partners as provided in O. XXI r. 50.
- **O. XXXI (trustees, executors, administrators):** in suits about property vested in a trustee, executor or administrator, the **beneficiaries need not be parties** — the trustee represents them (r. 1); all trustees, executors and administrators must be joined (r. 2).

## 6.4 Minors and persons of unsound mind — Order XXXII

### The scheme

- **r. 1:** every suit **by** a minor is instituted in his name by a **next friend**; **r. 2:** a plaint filed without a next friend may be taken off the file, with costs against the pleader who presented it.
- **r. 3:** where the **defendant is a minor**, the court, "**on being satisfied of the fact of his minority**", shall appoint a proper person as **guardian for the suit** (*guardian ad litem*). The application is supported by an **affidavit** that the proposed guardian has **no interest adverse** to the minor and is fit (r. 3(3)); **notice** must go to the minor and to his legal guardian, or father / natural guardian, or the person in whose care he is (r. 3(4)). The guardian continues through appeal, revision and execution (r. 3(5)).
- **r. 4:** any person of sound mind who has attained majority may act, if his interest is **not adverse** to the minor and he is not a defendant (for a next friend) or plaintiff (for a guardian); a **court-appointed** guardian has preference; **no one is appointed without consent**; if no one is fit and willing, the court may appoint **one of its officers**.
- **r. 5:** every application for the minor is made by the next friend / guardian; **any order made without the minor being so represented may be discharged**, with costs against the pleader who knew of the minority.
- **r. 6:** the next friend / guardian cannot **receive money** for the minor without leave; security may be required.
- **r. 7:** **no compromise** on behalf of a minor **without the leave of the court expressly recorded**; a compromise without such leave is **voidable** against all parties other than the minor.
- **rr. 8–11:** retirement, removal (where interest is adverse or he neglects his duty) and replacement of next friend or guardian.
- **rr. 12–14:** a minor plaintiff on **attaining majority** may elect to continue or abandon the suit; a minor co-plaintiff may repudiate; the minor may apply to dismiss a suit that was unreasonable or improper.
- **r. 15:** the Order applies to **persons of unsound mind** adjudged so, or found by the court on inquiry to be incapable of protecting their interests.

### Questions from the LHC 2023 paper

1. **Who is a guardian ad litem?** A person appointed by the court to represent and defend a **minor defendant** for the purposes of that suit only (O. XXXII r. 3). He is different from a guardian of the person or property appointed under the Guardians and Wards Act 1890, though such a guardian is preferred (r. 4(2)).
2. **Can the court decide whether a party is really a minor?** **Yes.** Rule 3(1) says the court appoints a guardian "on being satisfied of the fact of his minority", so the court itself determines the question of minority — on documents (birth certificate, CNIC / B-form, school record) and, if needed, evidence.
3. **Effect of a decree against a minor without a guardian?** A decree passed against a minor who was **not represented** by a guardian for the suit is **not binding on the minor** — he was never effectively before the court. Rule 5(2) allows any such order to be **discharged**. The minor can challenge it (for example, under s. 12(2) as a decree without jurisdiction over his person, or by suit / appeal as the case allows) on the ground of non-representation. Courts have, however, upheld decrees where a guardian was in fact appointed but with a procedural irregularity, if the minor's interests were fully protected and no prejudice was caused.

:::why Why such care for minors?
A minor cannot understand or protect his legal interests (s. 11 Contract Act — he is not even competent to contract). The court acts as *parens patriae* (parent of the nation) for him: it ensures that someone **without conflicting interest** speaks for him, that he is **not bound by an unfair compromise**, and that money decreed for him is not wasted.
:::

:::maxim Parens patriae — the State as parent of the country
Courts have a protective jurisdiction over those who cannot protect themselves — minors and persons of unsound mind. Every rule of Order XXXII is an application of this idea.
:::

## 6.5 Suits by paupers (indigent persons) — Order XXXIII

- **r. 1 — Who is a pauper:** a person "not possessed of sufficient means to enable him to pay the fee prescribed by law for the plaint", or, where no fee is prescribed, not entitled to property worth one thousand rupees, excluding necessary wearing apparel and the subject matter of the suit.
- **r. 2:** application contains plaint particulars plus a **schedule of his property**; signed and verified like a pleading.
- **r. 3:** presented **in person** (unless exempted).
- **r. 4:** court may examine the applicant on the merits and his property.
- **r. 5 — Rejection:** (a) not in proper form; (b) applicant **not a pauper**; (c) he **fraudulently disposed of property** within two months before applying; (d) allegations **show no cause of action**; (e) he has made an agreement giving another person an interest in the subject matter.
- **rr. 6–8:** notice to the opposite party and the Government pleader; inquiry into pauperism; if granted, the application is **numbered and registered as the plaint**, and the suit proceeds without court fee.
- **r. 9 — Dispaupering:** the court may dispauper him if he is guilty of vexatious conduct, his means are found sufficient, or he has agreed to share the proceeds.
- **rr. 10–12:** if the pauper **succeeds**, the court fee is calculated and is a **first charge** on the subject matter of the decree; if he **fails** or is dispaupered, he is ordered to pay the court fee; Government may recover it.
- **r. 15:** refusal of permission bars a subsequent application of like nature for the same right to sue, but the applicant may institute an **ordinary suit** (paying court fee), provided he **first pays the costs** incurred by Government and the opposite party in opposing his application. The court fee is recoverable by the Collector as arrears of land revenue (r. 14).

## 6.6 Interpleader — s. 88 and Order XXXV

> "88. Where two or more persons claim adversely to one another the same debt, sum of money or other property, moveable or immoveable, from another person, who claims no interest therein other than for charges or costs and who is ready to pay or deliver it to the rightful claimant, such other person may institute a suit of interpleader against all the claimants for the purpose of obtaining a decision as to the person to whom the payment or delivery shall be made and of obtaining indemnity for himself: Provided that where any suit is pending in which the rights of all parties can properly be decided, no such suit of interpleader shall be instituted."

### Conditions

1. **Two or more persons** claim **adversely to one another**;
2. the **same** debt, sum of money or property;
3. from **another person** (the stakeholder) who claims **no interest** in it other than charges or costs;
4. who is **ready to pay or deliver** it to the rightful claimant; and
5. **no other suit is pending** in which the rights of all parties can properly be decided (proviso).

### Procedure — Order XXXV

- **r. 1 — plaint:** besides usual particulars, states (a) the plaintiff claims **no interest** other than charges or costs; (b) the **claims of the defendants** severally; (c) that there is **no collusion** with any defendant.
- **r. 2:** the court may require the plaintiff to **pay or place the thing in court** before he gets any order.
- **r. 3:** where a defendant is **already suing** the plaintiff about the same subject matter, that suit is **stayed** on intimation.
- **r. 4 — first hearing:** the court may (a) **discharge the plaintiff** from all liability, award him costs and dismiss him from the suit; or (b) retain all parties till the end if justice requires; it may decide **title** on admissions, or frame **issues** between the claimants and make a claimant the plaintiff.
- **r. 5:** **agents cannot sue their principals**, and **tenants cannot sue their landlords**, to compel them to interplead with persons other than those claiming **through** the principal or landlord. (Illustrations: B, an agent holding A's jewels, cannot interplead A and C where C claims the jewels were wrongfully obtained from him; but B can interplead A and C where A himself pledged the jewels to C and they dispute the debt.)
- **r. 6:** the plaintiff's costs may be made a **charge** on the thing claimed.
- **Appeals:** orders under O. XXXV rr. 3, 4 and 6 are appealable — O. XLIII r. 1(p).

:::eg Interpleader in Islamabad
A bank in Blue Area holds Rs. 4 million in the account of the late Javed. His widow claims it as nominee; his brother claims it under an alleged gift; and a creditor has a decree against Javed. The bank claims nothing but its charges and is willing to pay the rightful person. If no suit is pending in which all three claims can be decided, the bank may file an **interpleader suit** against all three, deposit the money in court, and ask to be **discharged** with costs. The claimants then fight among themselves.
:::

:::why Why interpleader?
A neutral holder of money or property should not be exposed to **multiple suits** and the risk of paying twice. Interpleader lets him step out after depositing the property, while the real disputants settle their rights in one proceeding.
:::

## 6.7 Special case, public nuisance and public charities, ADR — ss. 89A to 93, Order XXXVI

- **s. 90, O. XXXVI — Special case:** parties may agree in writing to state a **question of fact or law** for the court's opinion; the court decides it as a suit and the agreement is registered as a suit.
- **s. 91 — Public nuisance:** the **Advocate General**, or **two or more persons with the leave of the court** (Islamabad text after Act XLVIII of 2016), may sue for a declaration and injunction even though no special damage is caused. An order refusing leave is appealable (s. 104(1)(fff)).
- **s. 92 — Public charitable or religious trusts:** the Advocate General, or two or more persons having an interest in the trust **with the leave of the court**, may sue in the principal civil court of original jurisdiction for removal of trustees, settling a scheme, accounts, etc.; no such suit lies otherwise (s. 92(2)).
- **s. 89A — Alternate dispute resolution:** the court may, where necessary, "with the consent of the parties", adopt mediation, conciliation or other ADR for expeditious disposal. (In Islamabad, the primary objective in s. 1(4)(b) also asks courts to encourage ADR.)

:::recap Chapter 6 in eight lines
- s. 79: name "Pakistan" / "the Province". **s. 80 (Pakistan): notice not a bar** — loss of costs if settled within two months; Government gets ≥ 3 months for written statement.
- s. 81: no arrest / attachment; exemption from appearance; s. 82: time for satisfaction; execution 3 months after report. O. XXVII r. 8A: no security from Government; O. XXVII-A: notice to Attorney / Advocate General on constitutional questions.
- Aliens may sue (s. 83); foreign States sue (s. 84); immunity under State Immunity Ordinance 1981.
- Corporations (O. XXIX), firms in firm name (O. XXX), trustees represent beneficiaries (O. XXXI).
- **Minors:** next friend; guardian ad litem appointed after court is satisfied of minority; no adverse interest; notice; compromise only with recorded leave; unrepresented minor not bound (O. XXXII).
- **Paupers:** cannot pay court fee; inquiry; rejection grounds; court fee first charge on decree (O. XXXIII).
- **Interpleader:** adverse claims to the same property against a disinterested stakeholder; no pending suit; agents and tenants cannot use it against principals and landlords (s. 88, O. XXXV).
- Special case (s. 90); public nuisance and charities with leave (ss. 91–92); ADR with consent (s. 89A).
:::

# Chapter 7 · Summary suits on negotiable instruments (Order XXXVII)

## 7.1 The idea

A **promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque (hundi)** is a written, unconditional promise or order to pay. Business depends on such instruments being honoured quickly. Order XXXVII gives the holder a **fast-track suit**: the defendant **cannot even defend** unless he first obtains the court's **leave**, by showing a real defence.

## 7.2 Which courts and which suits — rr. 1 and 2

- **r. 1:** the Order applies to the **High Court**, the **District Court**, and any other civil court **notified** by the High Court.
- **r. 2(1):** **all suits upon bills of exchange, hundis or promissory notes** may, if the plaintiff so desires, be instituted under this Order, by a plaint in the prescribed form, with summons in **Form No. 4, Appendix B**.
- **r. 2(2):** the defendant **shall not appear or defend** unless he obtains **leave**; if he does not, the allegations of the plaint are **deemed admitted** and the plaintiff gets a decree for the principal, interest (at the rate under ss. 79–80 Negotiable Instruments Act up to suit, and thereafter as the court thinks fit), and fixed costs.
- **r. 2(3):** such a decree "**may be executed forthwith**".

## 7.3 Leave to defend — r. 3

> "3. (1) The Court shall, upon application by the defendant, give leave to appear and to defend the suit, upon affidavits which disclose such facts as would make it incumbent on the holder to prove consideration, or such other facts as the Court may deem sufficient to support the application. (2) Leave to defend may be given unconditionally or subject to such terms as to payment into Court, giving security, framing and recording issues or otherwise as the Court thinks fit. (3) The provisions of section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1908, shall apply to applications under sub-rule (1)."

- **Time:** the application for leave must be filed within **ten days** from service of summons (**Art. 159, Limitation Act**); delay may be condoned under s. 5 (r. 3(3), added 1980).
- **Test:** the defendant's **affidavit** must disclose facts showing a **triable issue** or a **substantial / plausible defence** — e.g., that the note was given **without consideration**, was a security for a transaction that failed, was forged, was materially altered, or has been discharged.

### The four outcomes (classic principles)

:::case Mechelec Engineers & Manufacturers v. Basic Equipment Corporation ((1976) 4 SCC 687)
__Held.__ The Supreme Court of India (approving *Santosh Kumar v. Bhai Mool Singh*) summarised the principles for leave to defend in summary suits:
(a) If the defendant shows a **good defence** on the merits — **unconditional** leave.
(b) If he raises a **triable issue** showing a **fair or bona fide or reasonable defence**, though not a positively good one — **unconditional** leave ordinarily.
(c) If the defence discloses triable issues but there is **doubt about its genuineness** — leave on **conditions** (payment into court or security).
(d) If the defence is **sham, frivolous or vexatious**, or practically moonshine — **leave refused** and the plaintiff gets judgment.
(e) If any part of the claim is **admitted**, leave is refused unless that part is deposited.

__Ratio.__ The purpose of O. XXXVII is speed, not to shut out genuine defences; leave is the rule where a real triable issue exists, and conditions are the tool where the defence looks doubtful.
:::

## 7.4 Denial of consideration — the LHC 2023 question

Under **s. 118(a) of the Negotiable Instruments Act 1881**, it is **presumed** that every negotiable instrument was made or drawn **for consideration**, until the contrary is proved. Therefore:

1. A **bare denial** of consideration does not raise a triable issue — the presumption stands.
2. A **specific, plausible case** of want of consideration (with particulars — e.g., "the cheque was given as security for a supply contract which the plaintiff never performed; the plaintiff's own letter dated … admits non-supply") **does** raise a triable issue, because, if proved, it would make it "incumbent on the holder to **prove consideration**" — the very words of r. 3(1).
3. **Onus:** initially on the **defendant** to rebut the statutory presumption by showing facts that make non-consideration probable; once he does, the burden **shifts** to the plaintiff to prove consideration. (A holder in due course is protected against defects of title of prior parties — s. 9 NI Act.)

## 7.5 Setting aside a summary decree — r. 4

"After decree the Court may, **under special circumstances**, set aside the decree, and if necessary stay or set aside execution, and may give leave to the defendant to appear … and to defend the suit." Special circumstances include non-service or defective service of the Form No. 4 summons, or other reasons that prevented the defendant from applying for leave in time.

## 7.6 Other rules

- **r. 5:** the court may order the instrument to be **deposited** with a court officer, and stay proceedings until the plaintiff gives security for costs.
- **r. 6:** **noting charges** of a dishonoured bill are recoverable in the same way.
- **r. 7:** otherwise the **ordinary procedure** applies; once leave is granted, the suit is tried like a regular suit (issues, evidence).

:::why Why a summary procedure?
Negotiable instruments are the life-blood of commerce; if every dishonoured cheque required a full trial, honest creditors would be defeated by delay and dishonest debtors would profit. The leave requirement **reverses the usual position**: instead of the plaintiff proving everything, the defendant must first show he has something real to try.
:::

:::tip Summary suit vs ordinary suit (LLB 2007 question)
(1) **Leave required** to defend; (2) no defence → plaint **deemed admitted**; (3) decree **executable forthwith**; (4) **10 days** for leave (Art. 159); (5) court may grant **conditional leave**; (6) only on **bills, hundis, promissory notes** (cheques are bills of exchange); (7) only in notified courts; (8) special power to set aside decree in **special circumstances** (r. 4).
:::

:::recap Chapter 7 in five lines
- O. XXXVII: suits on bills, hundis, promissory notes in notified courts; Form No. 4 summons.
- No leave → allegations deemed admitted → decree, executable forthwith (r. 2).
- Leave on affidavit showing a **triable issue** (r. 3); within **10 days** (Art. 159); s. 5 LA applies; unconditional, conditional or refused (*Mechelec*).
- **s. 118 NI Act** presumption of consideration; bare denial insufficient; plausible particulars shift the burden to the holder.
- Decree may be set aside for **special circumstances** (r. 4); once leave given, ordinary trial (r. 7).
:::
