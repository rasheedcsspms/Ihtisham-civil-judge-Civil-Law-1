# Answer 8 · Auction-purchaser's possession obstructed — is the r. 97 inquiry warranted? (LHC ASJ 2020)

:::glance LHC ASJ EXAM 2020 · CIVIL LAW-II · Q. 1 · 20 MARKS
A, who is the purchaser of immovable property sold in execution of a decree and in whose favour a certificate in respect of that property has been granted under Order 21 Rule 94 CPC, applies under Order 21 Rule 95 CPC for delivery of possession of that property. Before A goes to the spot to take possession of that property, B comes into the court and files an application saying that he is in possession of that property on his own account and that possession should not be given to A unless his (B's) application is decided. A is not at all willing for the inquiry. He maintains that the warrant of possession should be issued and that on the prospective objection of B, the Court should not start upon any inquiry at that stage. Not minding the contentions of A, the Court orders that B's application being tantamount to obstruction or resistance in obtaining possession of the property, an inquiry should be started under Order 21 Rule 97 CPC. **Whether such a procedure adopted by the executing Court is warranted by the Civil Procedure Code?**
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](b8_map.png)

## Decoding the question

A pure **execution-procedure problem**. The examiner tests whether you know: (1) the purchaser's right to possession (rr. 94–96); (2) the **trigger** for r. 97 (actual resistance or obstruction, complained of by the decree-holder / purchaser); (3) the remedy of a **stranger** — after **dispossession** (r. 100), not before; (4) the Pakistani **r. 103** (all questions of title and possession decided in execution, no separate suit); and (5) whether the court can nevertheless entertain B's objection under **s. 47 / s. 151**. A balanced, reasoned conclusion earns the marks.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — the issue

The question is whether an executing court may treat a **pre-emptive application** by a third party (B), filed **before any attempt** to deliver possession, as "resistance or obstruction" and start an **inquiry under O. XXI r. 97**, over the auction-purchaser's (A's) objection.

### 2. The purchaser's right to possession — rr. 94 to 96

When the sale becomes absolute, the court grants a **sale certificate** (r. 94); the property vests in the purchaser from the date of sale (s. 65). On the purchaser's application, the court orders delivery: **actual** possession where the property is in the occupancy of the judgment-debtor "or of some person on his behalf or of some person claiming under a title created by the judgment-debtor subsequently to the attachment" (r. 95), and **symbolic** possession where a tenant is in occupation (r. 96). A has followed the correct course under r. 95.

### 3. When does r. 97 come in?

"Where the holder of a decree for the possession of immoveable property or the **purchaser** of any such property sold in execution of a decree **is resisted or obstructed** by any person in obtaining possession of the property, **he may make an application** to the Court complaining of such resistance or obstruction" (r. 97(1)). Two points follow:

1. The **trigger** is actual **resistance or obstruction** in obtaining possession — a past fact, not a fear.
2. The **applicant** under r. 97 is the **decree-holder or purchaser**, not the obstructer. Rule 97 is the purchaser's remedy, and he may choose whether to use it.

### 4. The stranger's remedy — rr. 99 to 101

A person **other than the judgment-debtor** claiming in good faith to be in possession **on his own account** is protected in two ways: (i) if he **resists** and the purchaser complains, the court, on inquiry, **dismisses** the purchaser's application (r. 99); (ii) if he is **dispossessed**, he may **himself** apply (r. 100) within **30 days** (Art. 165), and if he was in possession on his own account he is **restored** (r. 101). A transferee *pendente lite* from the judgment-debtor gets no protection (r. 102).

### 5. Title decided in execution — r. 103 (Pakistan)

"**All questions arising as to title, right or interest in, or possession of immoveable property** between an applicant under rule 97 and the opposite party, or between an applicant under rule 100 and the opposite party, **shall be adjudicated upon and determined by the Court**, and **no separate suit shall lie** for the determination of any such matter" (r. 103, substituted by the Law Reforms Ordinance 1972). The orders are **deemed decrees** (s. 2(2)) and appealable as such.

### 6. Applying the rules — was the procedure warranted?

#### i) Strictly under r. 97 — no

(1) There has been **no resistance or obstruction** yet; A has not even gone to the spot. B's application is an **anticipatory objection**. (2) Rule 97 contemplates an application **by A**, which A declines to make. (3) B is **not yet dispossessed**, so r. 100 is not available to him either. On the plain words of rr. 97 and 100, the court could not **deem** B's application to be "resistance" and start a r. 97 inquiry at A's expense.

#### ii) The correct course on the rules

The court should issue the **warrant of possession** under r. 95. If B then **resists**, A may apply under r. 97 and the court will inquire (rr. 98–99, 103). If B is **dispossessed**, he may apply under r. 100 and the court will inquire (rr. 101, 103). Either way, B's title and possession will be **fully adjudicated** under r. 103.

### 7. Can the court act under s. 47 or s. 151?

#### i) Section 47

B is a **stranger** to the decree, not a party or a representative of a party; s. 47 does not govern a dispute between the purchaser and an independent stranger.

#### ii) Section 151

The court has inherent power "to make such orders as may be necessary for the ends of justice or to prevent abuse of the process" (s. 151, exercised **with recorded reasons** in Islamabad). Some courts have entertained such objections to **avoid a breach of the peace** and needless dispossession of a bona fide occupant, since r. 103 anyway makes the executing court the **only** forum. But inherent power **cannot be used contrary to the scheme** of the Code (*Manohar Lal Chopra v. Seth Hiralal*, AIR 1962 SC 527). Where the Code gives B a specific remedy after dispossession (r. 100) and gives A the choice under r. 97, the court should not override that scheme merely because B **anticipates** resistance.

### 8. Policy considerations

The purpose of O. XXI is to give the decree-holder / purchaser the **fruits of the decree quickly**. Allowing third parties to stall delivery by filing **anticipatory objections** would encourage collusive obstruction (often by the judgment-debtor's relatives). At the same time, a genuine occupant is fully protected by rr. 99–101 and the inquiry under r. 103 — with no separate suit, so nothing is lost by following the sequence the Code prescribes.

### 9. Conclusion

The procedure adopted by the executing court is **not warranted by O. XXI r. 97**: there was no actual resistance or obstruction, and the r. 97 application belongs to the purchaser, who declined to make it. The court should have issued the **warrant of possession** under r. 95, leaving B to resist (whereupon A may invoke r. 97) or, if dispossessed, to apply under **r. 100** — in either event, the question of B's title and possession would be **decided by the executing court under r. 103**, with no separate suit. At most, for recorded reasons of preventing a breach of the peace, the court might **note** B's objection and direct the bailiff to report the facts — but it should not stall delivery by starting a r. 97 inquiry on a "prospective" objection.

## Why this answer scores

- It quotes the **exact trigger** in r. 97 ("is resisted or obstructed") and identifies **who** may apply — the key to the problem.
- It sets out the **complete Pakistani scheme** — rr. 95–103, including r. 103 and Arts. 165/167 — and the **s. 47 / s. 151** angles.
- It gives a **clear verdict** and the **correct procedure**, plus a balanced note on inherent powers.

# Answer 9 · Procedure for executing a money decree; the competent court (PU LLB 2008)

:::glance PUNJAB UNIVERSITY LLB (CPC) 2008 · Q. 7 · STANDARD JUDICIAL-EXAM QUESTION · 20 MARKS
Describe the procedure of execution of a money decree. Which Court has competent jurisdiction?
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](b9_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Two parts: **which court** (ss. 37–42, 46) and **the procedure** — from the application to satisfaction: limitation, the application, notice, objections (with r. 23-A), modes (arrest and detention; attachment and sale), exemptions, claims, sale, rateable distribution, and the Pakistani safeguards (s. 51 proviso, s. 56). A flowchart-style answer with sections and rules earns marks.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — enforcing a money decree

A **money decree** orders the judgment-debtor to pay a sum of money. If he does not pay voluntarily, the decree-holder **executes** it through the court. "Every decree for the payment of money … may be executed by the **detention in prison** of the judgment-debtor, or by the **attachment and sale of his property**, or by **both**" (O. XXI r. 30).

### 2. Which court is competent?

1. **The court which passed the decree** (s. 38) — including, where the decree was passed in appeal, the **court of first instance**, and where that court has ceased to exist or lost jurisdiction, the court that would now have jurisdiction (s. 37).
2. **The transferee court** — the decree court may send the decree for execution where the judgment-debtor resides / works, or has property, elsewhere, or for other recorded reasons (s. 39); it then has the **same powers** as if it had passed the decree (s. 42).
3. **Precept** — the decree court may direct another court to **attach** property pending transfer (two months) (s. 46).
4. **Foreign decrees** of reciprocating territories may be executed under **s. 44A** by the District Court.

### 3. Limitation

The first application within **three years** (Art. 181 — Art. 182 was omitted in 1972); fresh applications within the **six-year** ceiling of **s. 48**, unless fraud or force by the judgment-debtor prevented execution.

### 4. The application — O. XXI rr. 10, 11

A **written application** in tabular form: suit number, parties, date of decree, appeals, payments or adjustments, amount due with interest and costs, the **mode** of execution (arrest; attachment of specified property), signed and verified (r. 11(2)). An **oral application** may be allowed for immediate arrest of a judgment-debtor present in court (r. 11(1)). For immovable property, a description and the judgment-debtor's share (r. 13).

### 5. Notice and objections — rr. 22, 23, 23-A; s. 47

- **Notice to show cause** if the application is made **more than one year** after the decree or against a **legal representative** (r. 22).
- **Objections** relating to execution, discharge or satisfaction are decided by the executing court under **s. 47** — no separate suit.
- **Pakistani rule — r. 23-A:** "An objection by the judgment-debtor to the execution of a decree shall not be considered by the Court unless … he either **deposits the decretal amount** in Court or **furnishes security** for its payment."
- **Uncertified payments** out of court are not recognised (r. 2).

### 6. Mode 1 — arrest and detention

#### i) The safeguard

Detention "shall not be ordered unless, after giving the judgment-debtor an **opportunity of showing cause**", the court, **for reasons recorded**, is satisfied that he (a) is likely to **abscond** or has **dishonestly transferred / concealed** property; or (b) **has the means to pay and refuses or neglects** to pay; or (c) owes the sum in a **fiduciary** capacity (s. 51 proviso). Civil imprisonment is for **wilful** default, not for poverty (*Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin*, (1980) 2 SCC 360).

#### ii) Procedure

The court **first issues a notice** to show cause instead of a warrant, unless he is likely to abscond (O. XXI r. 37); inquiry; up to **15 days** in custody / on security to pay (r. 40); then detention — **simple imprisonment up to one year** (s. 55(1)); release on payment or satisfaction (s. 58); detention does **not discharge** the debt but bars re-arrest under the same decree (s. 55(3)).

#### iii) Limits

**No woman** may be arrested or detained for a money decree (s. 56); no entry into a dwelling-house between **sunset and sunrise**; outer doors broken only if the judgment-debtor occupies and refuses access; privacy of women respected (s. 55(1) provisos).

### 7. Mode 2 — attachment of property

- **What is attachable:** all saleable property of the judgment-debtor (s. 60(1)) **except** necessaries, tools of trade, agriculturist's house and implements, books of account, mere right to sue, pensions, wages of labourers, **salary beyond the first Rs. 100 and half the remainder**, provident fund, future maintenance, expectancy, etc. (s. 60 proviso).
- **How:** movables by **seizure** (r. 43); debts and shares by **prohibitory order** — the garnishee may pay into court (r. 46); salary by order to the disbursing officer (r. 48); immovables by **prohibitory order proclaimed** at the spot and court-house (r. 54).
- **Examination of the judgment-debtor** on oath about his property (r. 41).
- **Effect:** any private transfer after attachment is **void as against** the attachment (s. 64).

### 8. Claims by third parties — rr. 58 to 62

A third party claiming the attached property must object within a **reasonable time** or **one year** of the first attachment (r. 58 proviso); he must prove title **at the date of attachment** (r. 59); the court releases or continues attachment (rr. 60–61) and **finally decides title** — **no separate suit** (r. 62; r. 63 omitted).

### 9. Sale and realisation — rr. 64 to 94

**Proclamation** after notice (r. 66); no sale within **30 days** (immovable) / **15 days** (movable) (r. 68); public auction; decree-holder bids only **with leave** (r. 72); purchaser deposits **25%** at once and the balance within **15 days** (rr. 84–85); sale may be set aside on deposit (r. 89), for material irregularity or fraud with substantial injury (r. 90 — deposit up to 20%), or no saleable interest (r. 91) — within **30 days** (Art. 166); confirmation and **certificate** (rr. 92, 94).

### 10. Distribution of proceeds — s. 73

Where several money decree-holders have applied to the same court **before receipt** of the assets, the proceeds are **rateably distributed** among them after costs (s. 73).

### 11. Execution against Government

A decree against Government or a public officer (for an official act) specifies a time for satisfaction; execution cannot issue until **three months** after the court's report to Government (s. 82).

### 12. Conclusion

A money decree is executed by the court that passed it or a transferee court (ss. 37–42), on a timely application (Art. 181; s. 48), through **detention** of the judgment-debtor — only for wilful default after show-cause — or **attachment and sale** of non-exempt property, with third-party claims and objections decided finally within execution and the proceeds distributed rateably. The Pakistani additions — **deposit before objection (r. 23-A)**, **no separate suit** (rr. 62, 103) and **no arrest of women** (s. 56) — balance speed for the creditor with fairness to the debtor.

## Why this answer scores

- It answers **both** parts — forum and procedure — in the order a court actually proceeds.
- Every step carries its **section / rule** and the key **numbers** (3 / 6 years; 1 year; Rs. 100 + half; 25% / 15 days; 30 days).
- It shows **Pakistani features** (r. 23-A, s. 56, r. 62 / r. 63 omitted, Art. 182 omitted).
- *Jolly George Varghese* anchors the human-rights limit on civil imprisonment.

# Answer 10 · Suit against Government without notice — s. 80 and Order XXVII (PU LLB 2007)

:::glance PUNJAB UNIVERSITY LLB (CPC) 2007 · Q. 4 · STANDARD JUDICIAL-EXAM QUESTION · 20 MARKS
Can a suit be filed against Government without serving a notice? What are its details?
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](b10_map.png)

## Decoding the question

The short answer in **Pakistan is "Yes"** — s. 80 (as substituted in 1962) does **not** bar a suit filed without notice; it only affects **costs** and the **time** for the written statement. Most textbooks (Indian) say the opposite. The "details" are: the contents of the notice, to whom it is given, the two-month period, the consequences of non-compliance under s. 80(2), the purpose, and related rules — s. 79 (naming), s. 81–82, O. XXVII, O. XXVII-A, s. 24 of the Civil Courts Ordinance (forum), and special statutes with their own mandatory notices.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — the State as litigant

The Government is the largest litigant in Pakistan. The CPC gives it some procedural **privileges** — chiefly a **prior notice** before it is sued — so that it can examine claims and settle genuine ones without litigation. The question is whether the notice is a **condition precedent** to a suit.

### 2. The text of s. 80(1)

"A suit may be instituted against the Government or against a public officer, in respect of any act purporting to be done by such public officer in his official capacity, **after the expiration of two months** next after **notice in writing** has been delivered to or left at the office of — (a) in the case of a suit against the Federal Government, a **Secretary** to that Government; (b) in the case of a suit against a Provincial Government, a Secretary to that Government or the **Collector** of the District …; and in the case of a **public officer**, delivered to him or left at his office, stating the **cause of action**, the **name, description and place of residence** of the plaintiff and the **relief** which he claims; and the plaint shall contain a statement that such notice has been so delivered or left."

### 3. Contents and service of the notice

The notice must (1) be **in writing**; (2) state the **cause of action**; (3) give the plaintiff's **name, description and residence**; (4) state the **relief** claimed; (5) be delivered to or left at the office of the **Secretary** (Federal / Provincial), the **Collector** (provincial), or the **public officer** concerned; and (6) the suit is filed **after two months**, with a statement in the plaint that notice was given.

### 4. Consequences of not giving notice — s. 80(2) (Pakistan)

"Where any such suit is instituted **without** delivering or leaving such notice … or **before the expiration** of the said period of two months or where the plaint does not contain a statement that such notice has been so delivered or left, **the plaintiff shall not be entitled to any costs if settlement** as regards the subject-matter of the suit is reached or the Government or the public officer **concedes the plaintiff's claim, within the period of two months from the date of the institution of the suit**: Provided that in a suit instituted without such notice, the Court shall allow **not less than three months** to the Government to submit its written statement."

### 5. So — can the suit be filed without notice? Yes

1. Section 80 itself provides **what happens** if notice is not given: loss of costs in case of early settlement, and extended time for the written statement.
2. It does **not** say that the suit shall be dismissed or the plaint rejected; therefore want of notice is **not a ground** for rejection under O. VII r. 11(d) ("barred by any law").
3. This is the **Pakistani** position since the substitution of s. 80 by **Ordinance XLIV of 1962**. In **India**, s. 80 remains **mandatory** and a suit without notice is not maintainable (*Bihari Chowdhary v. State of Bihar*, (1984) 2 SCC 627) — a contrast worth stating.

### 6. Rationale

The notice gives Government a chance to **reconsider** its position and **settle** just claims, avoiding public expense. Pakistan keeps this purpose through a **cost incentive** rather than a **jurisdictional bar**, so that a citizen with a genuine grievance — especially one needing urgent relief such as an injunction against demolition — is not shut out of court by a technicality.

### 7. Which suits — scope of s. 80

Suits against **Government** (any act), and against a **public officer** for an act "purporting to be done … in his official capacity" (whether within or in excess of authority). Not covered: suits against a public officer for **private** acts, or suits against **statutory corporations** that are not "Government" (their own statutes may require notice).

### 8. Special statutes with their own notices

Some special laws require a **notice before suit** as a condition, or provide a special limitation for suits against their authorities. Such statutory requirements must be examined under **those** statutes; s. 80's Pakistani rule does not automatically dilute them. In an exam answer, flag the distinction.

### 9. Related privileges and rules

- **s. 79:** the defendant is named "**Pakistan**" or "**the Province**".
- **s. 81:** no **arrest** or attachment (except in execution); exemption from **personal appearance** where public service would suffer.
- **s. 82:** decree to specify time for satisfaction; execution only **three months** after report to Government.
- **O. XXVII:** pleadings signed / verified by appointed officers (r. 1); Government pleader receives process (r. 4); **reasonable time** to answer (r. 5); time for a public officer to make a reference (r. 7); **no security** from Government for stay (r. 8A).
- **O. XXVII-A:** notice to the **Attorney General / Advocate General** in cases involving a substantial question of **constitutional interpretation**.
- **s. 24, Civil Courts Ordinance 1962:** such suits only before a **Civil Judge with unlimited pecuniary jurisdiction**, at district headquarters.
- **O. XXXIX r. 3:** injunction against Government only after **notice** (2–7 days), unless the case involves **ejectment or demolition**.
- **s. 35C:** Government is **not liable** for actual / adjournment / special costs under ss. 35–35B.

### 10. Illustration

On 1 March the Capital Administration issues a notice to demolish a boundary wall of Naveed's house in E-11 within 48 hours. Naveed files a suit on 2 March for declaration and injunction **without** a s. 80 notice. The suit is **maintainable**; the court may issue an injunction (notice may be dispensed with under O. XXXIX r. 3 because the case involves **demolition**) and must allow the Government at least **three months** for its written statement. If the Government concedes the claim within two months, Naveed will get **no costs**.

### 11. Conclusion

Yes — in Pakistan, a suit may be filed against Government or a public officer **without** a s. 80 notice. The notice remains desirable: its absence costs the plaintiff his costs if the claim is settled within two months, and gives Government at least three months for its written statement — but it is **not a condition precedent** and does not make the suit incompetent. The "details" of suing Government — naming (s. 79), privileges (ss. 81–82, O. XXVII), forum (s. 24 Ordinance) and special statutory notices — complete the picture.

## Why this answer scores

- It gives the **correct Pakistani answer** at once and proves it from the **text of s. 80(2)** — while noting the contrary Indian rule.
- It covers all the **details**: contents, addressees, two months, consequences, scope.
- It connects s. 80 with **s. 79, ss. 81–82, O. XXVII, O. XXVII-A, O. XXXIX r. 3 and s. 24 of the Ordinance** — integrated knowledge.
- A short **illustration** shows the rule working in an urgent case.
