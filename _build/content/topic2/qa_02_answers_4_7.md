# Answer 4 · Review (s. 114) and amendment of judgments (s. 152) (KPHC 2026)

:::glance KP HIGH COURT 2026 · CIVIL LAW-I · Q. 4 · 20 MARKS
Differentiate between a civil court's power of 'review' under section 114 CPC, 1908, from the powers of a civil court to 'amend judgments, decrees and orders' under section 152 CPC, 1908.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](b4_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Two powers by which a court revisits its **own** decision. The examiner wants: the text of each, its **purpose**, its **scope and limits**, **procedure and time**, and a clear **comparison** — with illustrations showing where one ends and the other begins. Mention the principle that a signed judgment "shall not afterwards be altered or added to, save as provided by section 152 or on review" (O. XX r. 3).

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — finality and its two exceptions

Once a judgment is pronounced and signed, the court becomes *functus officio* — its job is done. "The judgment … when once signed, shall not afterwards be altered or added to, **save as provided by section 152 or on review**" (O. XX r. 3). The CPC thus allows only two ways for a court to revisit its own judgment: **correction** (s. 152) and **review** (s. 114).

### 2. Section 152 — the text

"Clerical or arithmetical mistakes in judgments, decrees or orders or errors arising therein from any accidental slip or omission may **at any time** be corrected by the Court either **of its own motion** or on the application of any of the parties."

### 3. Scope of s. 152

#### i) What it covers

(a) **Clerical mistakes** — typing errors in names, numbers, dates, khasra numbers; (b) **arithmetical mistakes** — wrong totals or interest calculations; (c) **accidental slips or omissions** — e.g., the court intended to award costs as stated in the judgment but the decree omits them.

#### ii) What it does not cover

It cannot be used to **change the decision**, add a relief not granted, correct an error of **law or judgment**, or rewrite reasoning. The test: does the correction make the record say **what the court actually intended** at the time? (*Actus curiae neminem gravabit* — a slip of the court must not prejudice a party.)

#### iii) Time and initiative

"At any time" — even after appeal or execution has begun; by the court **suo motu** or on application.

### 4. Section 114 and Order XLVII — the text

"Any person considering himself aggrieved by a decree or order from which (a) an appeal is allowed by this Code, but from which no appeal has been preferred; or (b) no appeal is allowed by this Code, may apply for a review of judgment to the Court which passed the decree or made the order" (s. 114, Islamabad text).

### 5. Grounds of review — O. XLVII r. 1

(1) **Discovery of new and important matter or evidence** which, after due diligence, was not within the applicant's knowledge or could not be produced; (2) **mistake or error apparent on the face of the record**; (3) **any other sufficient reason** — analogous to the first two (*Moran Mar Basselios Catholicos v. Mar Poulose Athanasius*, AIR 1954 SC 526).

### 6. Limits of review

Review is **not an appeal in disguise**; an error that needs a long process of reasoning or a debatable point is not "apparent" (*Thungabhadra Industries v. Govt. of A.P.*, AIR 1964 SC 1372; *Parsion Devi v. Sumitri Devi*, (1997) 8 SCC 715).

### 7. Procedure of review

Application to the **same court** (and, for grounds other than new evidence or clerical errors, the **same judge** — r. 2); **notice** to the opposite party before grant, strict proof of new evidence (r. 4); rejection **not appealable**, grant objectionable by appeal (r. 7; O. XLIII r. 1(w)); re-hearing after grant (r. 8); **no review of a review** (r. 9); limitation **90 days** (Art. 173).

### 8. Comparison

{w:22,39,39}
|Basis|Amendment — s. 152|Review — s. 114 / O. XLVII|
|Purpose|Make the record reflect what the court **intended**|Re-open the decision itself for a **legal** reason|
|Errors covered|Clerical, arithmetical, accidental slip or omission|New evidence; error apparent; analogous sufficient reason|
|Effect on decision|**None** — decision unchanged|May **alter or reverse** the decision|
|Who may move|Court **suo motu** or any party|Only a **person aggrieved**, by application|
|Time|**At any time**|**90 days** (Art. 173)|
|Judge|Any successor may correct|Same judge (unless unavailable)|
|Notice / hearing|Desirable, not always required for patent slips|Notice **mandatory** before granting|
|Remedy against order|Appeal / revision as the case may be|Rejection not appealable; grant appealable (O. XLIII r. 1(w))|
|Repetition|Any time a slip is found|No review of review (r. 9)|

### 9. Illustrations

1. The judgment decrees Rs. 750,000, but the decree sheet says Rs. 75,000 → **s. 152** (clerical).
2. The court computed interest at 10% for three years as Rs. 150,000 on Rs. 1 million; the correct figure is Rs. 300,000 → **s. 152** (arithmetical).
3. The court dismissed the suit as time-barred, overlooking s. 14 Limitation Act exclusion clearly shown by an exhibited order of return of plaint → **review** (error apparent).
4. After decree, the defendant discovers a registered receipt of payment which, despite due diligence, he could not trace earlier → **review** (new evidence, strict proof).
5. The losing party argues that the court wrongly believed a witness → neither; remedy is **appeal**.

### 10. Related powers

- **s. 153:** general power to amend any defect or error in **proceedings** to decide the real question.
- **Appellate correction:** an appellate court may correct slips in its own decree under s. 152.
- **Inherent power (s. 151):** to recall an order passed by the court's own mistake (e.g., without notice), with recorded reasons in Islamabad.

### 11. Conclusion

Section 152 is a **housekeeping** power to correct slips so that the record speaks the truth of what the court decided; review is a **narrow corrective jurisdiction** to reconsider the decision itself for patent error or genuinely new evidence. One leaves the decision untouched; the other may overturn it — and both stop short of an appeal.

## Why this answer scores

- It starts from **O. XX r. 3** — the rule that makes s. 152 and review the only exceptions to finality.
- The **comparison table** covers ten points of difference.
- Five short **illustrations** sharply separate slips, patent errors and appealable errors.
- Leading cases mark the limits of review.

# Answer 5 · Interpleader suits — how and where instituted, and their rules (KPHC 2026)

:::glance KP HIGH COURT 2026 · CIVIL LAW-I · Q. 1 · 20 MARKS
What do you mean by interpleader suit? How and where should it be instituted? Explain in detail its rules in the light of different provisions of CPC, 1908.
*(Also LHC 2017 short note: "Interpleader suit".)*
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](b5_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Three parts: **meaning** (s. 88 and its conditions), **how and where** (plaint contents under O. XXXV r. 1, payment into court, ordinary rules of place of suing and pecuniary jurisdiction), and **rules** (O. XXXV rr. 1–6, including the agents / tenants bar, appeals and costs). Add illustrations and the rationale.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — the stakeholder's dilemma

Sometimes a person holds money or property in which he claims **no interest**, but **two or more persons** claim it **against each other**. If he pays one, the other may sue him; he risks paying twice. The **interpleader suit** lets him bring all the claimants before the court, deposit the thing, be **discharged**, and leave the claimants to "interplead" — to fight among themselves.

### 2. Meaning — s. 88

"Where two or more persons claim adversely to one another the same debt, sum of money or other property, moveable or immoveable, from another person, who claims no interest therein other than for charges or costs and who is ready to pay or deliver it to the rightful claimant, such other person may institute a suit of interpleader against all the claimants for the purpose of obtaining a decision as to the person to whom the payment or delivery shall be made and of obtaining indemnity for himself."

### 3. Essential conditions

1. **Two or more claimants**;
2. claiming **adversely to one another** (not jointly);
3. the **same** debt, money or property, movable or immovable;
4. from a person who claims **no interest** except **charges or costs**;
5. who is **ready** to pay or deliver it to the rightful claimant;
6. **no pending suit** in which the rights of all parties can properly be decided (proviso).

### 4. The bar of a pending suit — proviso to s. 88

"Where any suit is pending in which the rights of all parties can properly be decided, no such suit of interpleader shall be instituted." The interpleader suit is a device to **avoid** multiplicity; it cannot itself create it.

### 5. Where to institute

The CPC prescribes no special forum: the ordinary rules apply — the court of the **lowest grade competent** (s. 15) within whose **local limits** the defendants (claimants) reside or the cause of action arose (s. 20), or where the **immovable property** is situate (s. 16), within the court's **pecuniary limits** (value of the property or money). In Islamabad, a Civil Judge with the necessary pecuniary powers.

### 6. How to institute — the plaint (O. XXXV r. 1)

Besides the usual particulars (O. VII r. 1), the plaint must state: "(a) that the plaintiff claims **no interest** in the subject-matter in dispute other than for charges or costs; (b) the **claims made by the defendants severally**; and (c) that there is **no collusion** between the plaintiff and any of the defendants." All the claimants are made **defendants**.

### 7. Payment into court — r. 2

"Where the thing claimed is capable of being paid into Court or placed in the custody of the Court, the plaintiff may be required to so pay or place it before he can be entitled to any order in the suit." This guarantees that the stakeholder is genuinely neutral.

### 8. Stay of other suits — r. 3

Where any defendant is **already suing the plaintiff** about the same subject matter, the court in which that suit is pending shall, on being informed by the interpleader court, **stay** it as against the plaintiff; his costs may be provided for.

### 9. Procedure at the first hearing — r. 4

The court may: (a) **declare the plaintiff discharged** from all liability to the defendants, award him costs and **dismiss him** from the suit; or (b) retain all parties till final disposal if justice or convenience requires. Where admissions or evidence allow, it **adjudicates title** at once (r. 4(2)); otherwise it **frames issues** between the claimants and may make a claimant the **plaintiff** in place of or in addition to the original plaintiff, and tries the suit in the ordinary manner (r. 4(3)).

### 10. Agents and tenants cannot interplead — r. 5

"Nothing in this Order shall be deemed to enable agents to sue their principals, or tenants to sue their landlords, for the purpose of compelling them to interplead with any persons other than persons making claim through such principals or landlords." An agent or tenant who holds through a principal or landlord cannot deny that person's title by setting up a stranger's adverse claim (the estoppel of Art. 115 Qanun-e-Shahadat). *Illustrations:* (a) A deposits jewels with his agent B; C claims they were wrongfully taken from him — **B cannot interplead** A and C. (b) A pledges the jewels to C through B, and A and C dispute whether C's debt is paid — **B can interplead** A and C (C claims through A).

### 11. Costs and appeals — r. 6, O. XLIII

Where the suit is properly instituted, the plaintiff's costs may be given as a **charge on the thing claimed** (r. 6). Orders under O. XXXV **rr. 3, 4 and 6** are appealable (O. XLIII r. 1(p)); the final decree between the claimants is appealable under s. 96.

### 12. Illustration and conclusion

A bank in Blue Area holds Rs. 4 million in a deceased customer's account; his widow (as nominee), his brother (under an alleged gift) and a decree-holder creditor all claim it. No suit is pending in which all claims can be decided. The bank files an interpleader suit, deposits the money, and is **discharged** at the first hearing; the court frames issues among the three claimants.

The interpleader suit protects an **honest, disinterested holder** from **double vexation**, avoids **multiplicity** of suits, and lets the real disputants have their rights decided in one proceeding — subject to its strict conditions and the bar on agents and tenants.

## Why this answer scores

- **All three parts** of the question — meaning, how / where, rules — are answered under separate headings.
- **Every rule** of O. XXXV (rr. 1–6) and both parts of s. 88 (including the proviso) are covered.
- The **agents / tenants** bar is explained with the Code's own illustrations and its estoppel rationale.
- The answer correctly notes that **forum** is governed by ordinary rules (ss. 15–20), a point many candidates miss.

# Answer 6 · Summary suits — leave to defend and denial of consideration (LHC 2023)

:::glance LHC CIVIL JUDGE EXAM 2023 · CIVIL LAW-II · Q. 8 · 20 MARKS
Suits under Order XXXVII of the Civil Procedure Code 1908 envisage regular trial after leave to appear and defend is granted, upon finding need to adjudicate triable question(s). Whether assertion of denial of consideration by the defendant constitutes a triable question of law, and on whom the onus lies to prove absence of consideration.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](b6_map.png)

## Decoding the question

The examiner wants: (1) the nature of O. XXXVII and the **leave** requirement; (2) the **test for leave** ("triable issue"); (3) whether **denial of consideration** is a triable issue — the answer depends on **how** it is pleaded; (4) the **onus** — read with **s. 118 of the Negotiable Instruments Act** (presumption of consideration) and Arts. 117–118 Qanun-e-Shahadat; and (5) the consequences (conditional or unconditional leave; regular trial).

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — the summary procedure

Order XXXVII provides a **summary procedure** for suits "upon bills of exchange, hundis or promissory notes" (r. 2(1)) in the High Court, the District Court and other notified courts (r. 1). Its object is **speedy recovery** on commercial instruments: the defendant "shall not appear or defend the suit unless he obtains leave from a Judge" (r. 2(2)).

### 2. Consequence of not obtaining leave

If the defendant does not obtain leave, "the allegations in the plaint shall be **deemed to be admitted**, and the plaintiff shall be entitled to a decree" for the principal, interest and costs (r. 2(2)); the decree "may be **executed forthwith**" (r. 2(3)).

### 3. Leave to defend — r. 3

"The Court shall, upon application by the defendant, give leave to appear and to defend the suit, upon **affidavits** which disclose such facts as would make it **incumbent on the holder to prove consideration**, or such other facts as the Court may deem sufficient to support the application" (r. 3(1)). Leave may be **unconditional or conditional** — payment into court, security, framing of issues (r. 3(2)). The application must be made within **ten days** of service of summons (Art. 159, Limitation Act); **s. 5** of the Limitation Act applies (r. 3(3)).

### 4. The test — triable issue

Following *Mechelec Engineers v. Basic Equipment Corporation* ((1976) 4 SCC 687): (a) a **good defence** → unconditional leave; (b) a **triable issue** showing a fair, bona fide defence → unconditional leave ordinarily; (c) triable issue but **doubtful genuineness** → conditional leave; (d) **sham or moonshine** defence → leave refused; (e) admitted part → deposit required.

### 5. Is denial of consideration a triable question?

#### i) The text of r. 3 itself

Rule 3(1) speaks of facts "as would make it **incumbent on the holder to prove consideration**". So a plea of want of consideration is precisely the kind of defence the rule contemplates — **if** it is supported by facts.

#### ii) Bare denial — not enough

A **bald, general denial** ("no consideration was received") raises **no triable issue**, because the law presumes consideration (s. 118(a), NI Act). The affidavit must disclose **specific facts** showing why there was no consideration.

#### iii) Specific, plausible facts — triable

A plausible case — e.g., the note was given as **security** for a transaction that never materialised, or the consideration **failed**, supported by documents or circumstances — raises a **triable issue** of **fact** (or a mixed question), on which leave should be granted, conditionally if the defence looks doubtful.

#### iv) A question of law?

Whether consideration passed is primarily a **question of fact**; whether the facts alleged, if proved, amount to want or failure of consideration in law is a **mixed** question. Either way, if it is real, it is "triable".

### 6. The statutory presumption — s. 118(a) NI Act

"Until the contrary is proved, the following presumptions shall be made: (a) **of consideration** — that every negotiable instrument was made or drawn for consideration, and that every such instrument, when it has been accepted, endorsed, negotiated or transferred, was accepted, endorsed, negotiated or transferred for consideration."

### 7. On whom the onus lies

1. **Initially on the defendant** — because of the presumption in s. 118(a), the defendant who asserts want of consideration must **rebut** it ("until the contrary is proved"). Under Arts. 117–118 QSO, the burden lies on the person who asserts.
2. The defendant may discharge this burden by **direct evidence** or by **circumstances** that make the non-existence of consideration probable; a mere denial in the witness box is not enough.
3. Once he does so, the **onus shifts to the plaintiff** to prove that consideration was in fact given.
4. A **holder in due course** (who took the instrument for value, in good faith, before maturity — s. 9 NI Act) is protected against the defence of want of consideration between prior parties.

### 8. What happens after leave — regular trial

Once leave is granted, "the procedure in suits hereunder shall be the same as the procedure in suits instituted in the ordinary manner" (r. 7): written statement, **issues** (including "Whether the promissory note was executed without consideration? **OPD**"), evidence and judgment.

### 9. Illustration

Rafiq sues Hamid under O. XXXVII on a promissory note for Rs. 2 million. Hamid's affidavit says he signed the note as **security** for a supply of steel which Rafiq **never delivered**, and annexes Rafiq's letter admitting non-supply. This discloses facts making it "incumbent on the holder to prove consideration" — **unconditional leave** should be granted. Had Hamid merely said "no consideration", leave would be refused (or granted only on deposit).

### 10. Setting aside a decree — r. 4

Even after a decree, the court may, under **special circumstances** (e.g., no proper service of the Form No. 4 summons), set it aside and grant leave to defend.

### 11. Conclusion

Denial of consideration is a **triable issue only when it is specific and plausible**; a bare denial is defeated by the presumption of s. 118(a) NI Act. The **onus** initially lies on the **defendant** to rebut the presumption; once he shows facts making want of consideration probable, the **plaintiff** must prove consideration. Leave is then granted — unconditionally or on terms — and the suit proceeds as a regular trial.

## Why this answer scores

- It answers **both** questions directly — triable issue? onus on whom? — with reasons.
- It uses the **text of O. XXXVII r. 3(1)** ("make it incumbent on the holder to prove consideration") as the key.
- It links to **s. 118 NI Act** and **Arts. 117–118 QSO** — showing integrated knowledge.
- The *Mechelec* categories and a crisp **illustration** show practical judgment.

# Answer 7 · Guardian ad litem; deciding minority; decree against an unrepresented minor (LHC 2023)

:::glance LHC CIVIL JUDGE EXAM 2023 · CIVIL LAW-II · Q. 7 · 20 MARKS
Explain the term 'Guardian Ad Litem'. Whether the court, before whom lis is brought involving minor, is competent to decide the dispute regarding factum of minority of the minor. Discuss the effect of any decree passed, affecting the rights of the minor, without the appointment of guardian, in the context of Order XXXII of Civil Procedure Code 1908.
:::

## The answer map

![The answer map: every branch is one numbered heading of the model answer below](b7_map.png)

## Decoding the question

Three questions: (1) **meaning** of guardian ad litem and the rules on his appointment; (2) whether the **court itself decides minority** — answer from O. XXXII r. 3(1) ("on being satisfied of the fact of his minority"); (3) **effect of a decree** passed without a guardian — O. XXXII r. 5(2) and general principles (decree not binding on the minor; remedies). Frame it within the *parens patriae* duty of the court.

## The model answer

### 1. Introduction — the court as protector

A **minor** (a person under 18 — Majority Act 1875) cannot understand or protect his legal rights; he cannot even contract (s. 11, Contract Act). The court therefore acts as ***parens patriae*** — the protector of those who cannot protect themselves. Order XXXII ensures that a minor is always **represented** in litigation by a person **without conflicting interest**.

### 2. Meaning — guardian ad litem

A **guardian ad litem** ("guardian for the suit") is a person appointed **by the court** to represent and defend a **minor defendant** in a particular suit and all proceedings arising from it (O. XXXII r. 3). A minor **plaintiff** sues through a **next friend** (r. 1). A guardian ad litem is different from a guardian of the person or property appointed under the **Guardians and Wards Act 1890**, though such a guardian is preferred for appointment (r. 4(2)).

### 3. Appointment — O. XXXII r. 3

"Where the defendant is a minor, the Court, **on being satisfied of the fact of his minority**, shall appoint a proper person to be guardian for the suit for such minor" (r. 3(1)). The application (by the plaintiff or on the minor's behalf) is supported by an **affidavit** that the proposed guardian has **no interest adverse** to the minor and is **fit** (r. 3(3)); **notice** must be given to the minor and to his court-appointed guardian, or father / natural guardian, or the person in whose care he is (r. 3(4)). The appointment continues through **appeal, revision and execution** (r. 3(5)).

### 4. Who may be appointed — r. 4

Any person of **sound mind** who has **attained majority**, whose interest is **not adverse** to the minor, and who is not a plaintiff in the suit; a **court-appointed guardian** has preference; **no one without his consent**; if no fit person is willing, the court may appoint **one of its officers** at the parties' cost (r. 4(1)–(4)).

### 5. Duties and safeguards

The guardian must defend the minor's interests with due care; he cannot **receive money** for the minor without leave (r. 6) or enter a **compromise** without the **express leave of the court recorded** in the proceedings — otherwise the compromise is **voidable** at the minor's instance (r. 7). He may be **removed** for adverse interest, neglect or misconduct (rr. 9, 11).

### 6. Can the court decide the factum of minority? — Yes

#### i) The text

Rule 3(1) requires the court to be "**satisfied of the fact of his minority**" before appointing a guardian — so the court **itself determines** whether the party is a minor.

#### ii) How

On the material produced — **B-form / CNIC**, birth certificate, school and matriculation records, medical opinion on age — and, if disputed, by **framing a preliminary issue** and recording evidence. The court is competent because the question is **incidental** to the conduct of the suit before it; no separate proceeding is needed. The finding governs representation in that suit.

### 7. Effect of a decree without a guardian

#### i) The rule in r. 5(2)

"Every order made in a suit or on any application, before the Court in or by which a minor is in any way concerned or affected, **without such minor being represented** by a next friend or guardian for the suit, as the case may be, **may be discharged**, and, where the pleader of the party at whose instance such order was obtained knew, or might reasonably have known, the fact of such minority, with costs to be paid by such pleader."

#### ii) The principle

A minor who was **never represented** was, in law, **never before the court**. A decree passed against him **does not bind him**; as against the minor it is treated as **void / a nullity**, and he may avoid it.

#### iii) Remedies of the minor

(a) Application to **discharge / set aside** the decree under O. XXXII r. 5(2) read with s. 151; (b) application under **s. 12(2)** CPC (want of jurisdiction over his person, or fraud where his minority was concealed); (c) **appeal** treating the decree as a nullity; (d) on attaining majority, **resist execution** on the ground that the decree is not binding on him.

#### iv) Irregularity vs absence of representation

Distinguish **total absence** of a guardian (decree not binding) from a mere **irregularity** in appointment (e.g., notice under r. 3(4) not given to the father, but a fit guardian was appointed and defended properly). Courts have generally upheld decrees in the second situation if the minor's interests were **fully protected** and **no prejudice** resulted, because the purpose of Order XXXII was achieved.

### 8. Illustration

Sajid sues Bilal, aged 15, for possession of a shop inherited by Bilal, describing him as an adult. No guardian is appointed; an ex parte decree is passed. Bilal's mother later shows his B-form. The decree is **not binding** on Bilal: it may be **discharged** under O. XXXII r. 5(2), with **costs against the plaintiff's pleader** if he knew of the minority; Bilal may also apply under s. 12(2). If, instead, the court had appointed Bilal's uncle as guardian (with no adverse interest) who defended fully, but notice to the mother under r. 3(4) was not issued, the decree would ordinarily **stand**, absent prejudice.

### 9. Persons of unsound mind — r. 15

Rules 1–14 apply equally to persons **adjudged of unsound mind**, and to persons found by the court on inquiry to be **incapable of protecting their interests** by reason of mental infirmity.

### 10. Conclusion

A **guardian ad litem** is the court-appointed voice of a minor defendant. The court is **competent** — indeed **bound** — to decide the factum of minority before appointing him (r. 3(1)). A decree passed against a minor **without any guardian** is **not binding** on him and may be discharged (r. 5(2)) or challenged under s. 12(2), while a mere procedural irregularity in an otherwise proper representation does not vitiate the decree if no prejudice is shown. The whole scheme gives effect to the court's *parens patriae* duty.

## Why this answer scores

- **All three questions** are answered separately, each from the **text of Order XXXII** (rr. 3(1), 5(2)).
- It distinguishes **guardian ad litem**, **next friend** and a **guardian under the Guardians and Wards Act**.
- It separates **absence of representation** (decree not binding) from **irregularity** (decree stands if no prejudice) — the nuance that earns top marks.
- **Remedies** of the minor are listed, including s. 12(2) — Pakistani law.
