# Chapter 1 · The hearing: evidence and affidavits (Orders XVIII and XIX)

## 1.1 Who begins — O. XVIII rr. 1 to 3

> "1. The plaintiff has the right to begin unless the defendant admits the facts alleged by the plaintiff and contends that either in point of law or on some additional facts alleged by the defendant the plaintiff is not entitled to any part of the relief which he seeks, in which case the defendant has the right to begin."

- **General rule:** the plaintiff begins, because he must prove his case first (the burden of proof — Arts. 117–118 Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984).
- **Exception:** where the defendant **admits** the plaintiff's facts but sets up **law** or **new facts** to defeat the claim (e.g., "Yes, I borrowed the money, but I repaid it" or "the claim is time-barred"), the **defendant begins**, because the only questions left are his.
- **r. 2:** the party beginning states his case and produces evidence on the issues he must prove; the other party then states his case, produces evidence and addresses the court; the party beginning replies generally.
- **r. 3:** where there are several issues and the burden of some lies on the other party, the party beginning may produce his evidence on those issues at once, or **reserve** it by way of answer to the other side's evidence.

## 1.2 How evidence is recorded — rr. 4 to 16

- **r. 4:** witnesses are examined **orally in open court**, "in the presence and under the personal direction and superintendence of the Judge".
- **r. 5:** in **appealable** cases, the evidence of each witness is taken down in writing, in the language of the court, ordinarily as a **narrative** (not question and answer), read over to the witness, corrected and **signed by the Judge**.
- **r. 10:** any particular question and answer may be taken down if the court thinks it important; **r. 11:** questions **objected to** and allowed are recorded with the objection and the decision.
- **r. 12:** the court may record **remarks on the demeanour** of a witness — very valuable to the appellate court.
- **r. 13:** in **non-appealable** cases, a memorandum of the substance of evidence is enough.
- **r. 15:** a judge may deal with evidence taken by his **predecessor** as if he had taken it himself, or re-examine witnesses.
- **r. 16:** where a witness is about to leave the jurisdiction (or other sufficient cause), the court may examine him **immediately**, out of turn.

:::why Why open court and judge's supervision?
Oral evidence in open court lets the judge see the witness — his hesitation, confidence and demeanour — which is often the best test of truth. Publicity also guards against abuse and builds public confidence (*justice must be seen to be done*). The judge's personal supervision makes the record trustworthy for the appellate court.
:::

## 1.3 Affidavits — Order XIX

- **r. 1:** any court may, **for sufficient reason**, order that any particular fact be proved by **affidavit**, or that the affidavit of any witness be read at the hearing — but where a party bona fide desires the production of a witness for **cross-examination** and the witness can be produced, the order shall not authorise his evidence to be given by affidavit.
- **r. 2:** on application of either party, the court may order the **attendance of the deponent for cross-examination**.
- **r. 3:** affidavits are confined to facts the deponent can prove **of his own knowledge**, except on **interlocutory applications**, where statements of **belief** may be admitted if the grounds are stated.

:::trap Affidavit is not "evidence" at trial by default
In a regular trial, affidavits replace oral evidence only when the court so orders under O. XIX r. 1 and the other side does not need cross-examination. On interim applications (injunction, receiver, attachment), affidavits are the normal mode of proof.
:::

:::recap Chapter 1 in four lines
- Plaintiff begins; defendant begins if he admits the facts and relies on law or new facts (O. XVIII r. 1).
- Evidence orally in open court under the judge's supervision; narrative form in appealable cases; objections and demeanour recorded (rr. 4–13).
- Successor judge may use predecessor's evidence (r. 15); immediate examination of departing witness (r. 16).
- Affidavits by order for sufficient reason; cross-examination may be ordered; own knowledge only, except interlocutory matters (O. XIX).
:::

# Chapter 2 · Judgment and decree (s. 33, Order XX, ss. 152–153)

## 2.1 Section 33 and the timing of judgment — O. XX r. 1

> "33. The Court, after the case has been heard, shall pronounce judgment, and on such judgment a decree shall follow."

> "1. (1) On completion of evidence, the Court shall fix a date, **not exceeding fifteen days**, for hearing of arguments of parties. (2) The Court shall, after the case has been heard, pronounce judgment in open Court, either at once or on some future day **not exceeding thirty days**, for which due notice shall be given to the parties or their advocates." (O. XX r. 1, as substituted by Act XIV of 1994)

:::why Why time limits for judgment?
"Justice delayed is justice denied." A judgment written long after arguments loses the freshness of the evidence and the arguments, and the litigant is left uncertain. The 15-day and 30-day limits are directory (no penalty is prescribed), but a judge who ignores them without reason may face administrative action.
:::

## 2.2 Contents of a judgment — rr. 2 to 5

- **r. 2:** a judge may pronounce a judgment **written but not pronounced** by his predecessor.
- **r. 3:** the judgment is **dated and signed in open court** when pronounced and "when once signed, shall not afterwards be altered or added to, save as provided by section 152 or on review".
- **r. 4(2):** judgments (other than small causes) shall contain "**a concise statement of the case, the points for determination, the decision thereon, and the reasons for such decision**".
- **r. 5:** in suits where issues have been framed, the court shall state its **finding, with reasons, on each separate issue**, unless the finding on one or more issues is sufficient for the decision.

:::tip Why the court should decide all issues
Even if one issue (say, limitation) disposes of the suit, a trial court should ordinarily decide **all** issues of fact on the evidence. If the appellate court disagrees on limitation, it can then decide the case on the other findings instead of remanding it — saving years. Rule 5 permits deciding only the decisive issue, but good practice is to decide all.
:::

## 2.3 The decree — rr. 6 to 8 and special decrees

- **r. 6:** the decree must **agree with the judgment**; contain the suit number, parties' names and descriptions, particulars of the claim; specify **clearly the relief granted**; and state the **costs** — amount, by whom and in what proportion. Costs may be set off against sums due.
- **r. 7:** the decree **bears the date** on which the judgment was pronounced; the judge signs it after checking it agrees with the judgment.
- **r. 9:** decree for **immovable property** — a description sufficient to identify it (boundaries, survey / khasra numbers).
- **r. 10:** decree for **movable property** — states the money to be paid as an alternative if delivery cannot be had.
- **r. 11 — instalments:** the court may, **for sufficient reason**, at the time of passing a money decree, order payment to be **postponed or made by instalments** (with or without interest), even against the contract; after decree, only with the **decree-holder's consent** (r. 11(2)). (Application for instalments after decree — **six months** from the decree, Art. 175 Limitation Act.)
- **r. 12 — possession and mesne profits:** the court may decree possession, rents and mesne profits **prior to the suit**, and direct an inquiry into mesne profits from the suit until delivery of possession, relinquishment by the judgment-debtor, or **three years** from the decree, whichever is earliest.
- **rr. 13–18 — preliminary decrees** in administration suits, **pre-emption** (r. 14: deposit of purchase money by a fixed date, failing which the suit is dismissed), partnership dissolution (r. 15), accounts between principal and agent (r. 16), and **partition** (r. 18).
- **r. 19:** decree where set-off is allowed; **r. 20:** certified copies of judgment and decree to be furnished.

## 2.4 Correcting judgments and decrees — ss. 152 and 153

> "152. Clerical or arithmetical mistakes in judgments, decrees or orders or errors arising therein from any accidental slip or omission may at any time be corrected by the Court either of its own motion or on the application of any of the parties."

> "153. The Court may at any time, and on such terms as to costs or otherwise as it may think fit, amend any defect or error in any proceeding in a suit; and all necessary amendments shall be made for the purpose of determining the real question or issue raised by or depending on such proceeding."

{w:24,38,38}
|Point|Amendment under s. 152|Review under s. 114 / O. XLVII|
|What is corrected|**Clerical or arithmetical** mistakes; **accidental slips or omissions**|**Error apparent on the face of the record**; new evidence; other sufficient reason|
|Changes the decision?|**No** — makes the decree say what the court **intended** to say|**May** change the decision on the merits|
|Time limit|"**At any time**"|**90 days** (Art. 173 Limitation Act; 15 days in small causes — Art. 161)|
|Initiative|Court **of its own motion** or on application|Only on **application** of an aggrieved person|
|Example|Decree says Rs. 50,000 though judgment says Rs. 500,000; plot No. 21 typed as 12|Court overlooked a binding Supreme Court judgment or a statutory provision|

:::maxim Actus curiae neminem gravabit
A mistake made by the court (a slip of the pen, a wrong figure) must not prejudice a party. Section 152 lets the court correct its own slip at any time, even after an appeal has been decided, so long as it does not alter the substance of the decision.
:::

:::recap Chapter 2 in six lines
- s. 33: judgment then decree. O. XX r. 1 (1994): arguments within **15 days** of evidence; judgment within **30 days** of hearing.
- Judgment signed in open court; not altered except under s. 152 or review (r. 3).
- Contents: concise statement, points, decision, **reasons** (r. 4(2)); **finding on each issue** (r. 5).
- Decree agrees with judgment; clear relief; costs (r. 6); dated on judgment day (r. 7).
- Instalments at decree for sufficient reason; after decree only with decree-holder's consent (r. 11). Mesne profits inquiry up to 3 years (r. 12). Preliminary decrees (rr. 13–18).
- **s. 152** corrects slips at any time without changing the decision; **s. 153** general power to amend proceedings; contrast **review**.
:::

# Chapter 3 · Death, marriage and insolvency of parties (Order XXII)

## 3.1 The basic rule — survival of the right to sue

> "1. The death of a plaintiff or defendant shall not cause the suit to abate if the right to sue survives."

The right to sue **survives** in most property, contract and money claims (the heirs inherit the right or liability). It does **not** survive in purely **personal** actions — e.g., a suit for damages for **defamation** or for personal injury that dies with the person (*actio personalis moritur cum persona* — a personal action dies with the person), or a suit for restitution of conjugal rights.

- **r. 2:** where one of several plaintiffs or defendants dies and the right to sue survives to or against the survivors alone, the court records the fact and the suit proceeds.
- **r. 3 / r. 4:** where the right does **not** survive to the survivors alone, or a sole plaintiff / defendant dies and the right survives, the court, **on application**, brings the **legal representatives** on record and proceeds. (Limitation for the application: **90 days from the death** — Arts. 176–177 Limitation Act.)
- **r. 5:** any question whether a person is a legal representative is decided by the court.
- **r. 6:** no abatement if death occurs **after the conclusion of hearing and before judgment**; judgment may be pronounced and has effect as if pronounced before death.

## 3.2 The Pakistani rule — no abatement for failure to substitute

:::ict O. XXII rr. 3(2), 4(3) and 4(4) — substituted / added by the Law Reforms Ordinance 1972
> "(2) Where within the time allowed by law no application is made or intimation is given under sub-rule (1), the Court may proceed with the suit, and any order made or judgment pronounced in such suit shall, notwithstanding the death of such plaintiff, have the same force and effect as if it had been made or pronounced before the death took place." (r. 3(2))

> "(3) When within the time limited by law no application is made or intimation is given under sub-rule (1), the Court may proceed with the suit, and any order made or judgment pronounced in such suit shall, notwithstanding the death of such defendant, have the same force and effect as if it had been pronounced before the death took place." (r. 4(3))

> "(4) It shall not be necessary to substitute the legal representatives of any such defendant who has failed to file a written statement or has failed to appear and contest the suit at the hearing; and judgment may in such case be pronounced against the said defendant notwithstanding his death …" (r. 4(4))

**What this means:** In India, if legal representatives are not brought on record within time, the suit **abates** automatically. In **Pakistan**, there is **no abatement** — the court may **proceed** and its judgment binds as if pronounced before the death. The legal representative who was not brought on record is protected by **r. 9(2)**: he may apply to **set aside** any order or judgment made in his absence on showing **sufficient cause** (limitation: **60 days** from the order or judgment — Art. 171; s. 5 Limitation Act applies — r. 9(3)).

Also, under the Pakistani rules, a plaintiff (O. VII r. 26) and a defendant (O. VIII r. 13) in certain suits file a **list of legal representatives** and nominate a person to **intimate** a death — so that the court learns of it and can substitute quickly.
:::

:::why Why abolish abatement?
Abatement punished the living for the fact of a death and for the slowness of finding heirs; years of trial could be lost on a technicality. The Pakistani rules keep the suit alive, while protecting the heir who was not heard by giving him a right to have the judgment set aside for sufficient cause. It reflects the principle that procedure is the handmaid of justice.
:::

## 3.3 Marriage, insolvency and devolution — rr. 7 to 12

- **r. 7:** the **marriage** of a female party does not cause abatement; a decree against her may be executed against her alone (against the husband too only where he is liable for her debts by law, with permission).
- **r. 8:** the **insolvency** of a plaintiff does not cause abatement unless the official assignee / receiver declines to continue or to give security for costs; then the defendant may seek **dismissal**, and **r. 9(1)** bars a fresh suit.
- **r. 10:** in other cases of **assignment, creation or devolution** of any interest **during** the suit, the suit may, **by leave of the court**, be continued by or against the person to whom the interest has come (e.g., a purchaser *pendente lite*, who is in any case bound by the decree under s. 52 of the Transfer of Property Act — *lis pendens*).
- **r. 11:** the Order applies to **appeals** (plaintiff = appellant, etc.).
- **r. 12:** rules 3, 4 and 8 do **not** apply to **execution** proceedings.

:::maxim Actio personalis moritur cum persona — a personal action dies with the person
Claims for purely personal wrongs (defamation, assault, malicious prosecution, personal status) do not survive the death of the person wronged or the wrongdoer, so the suit cannot be continued by or against heirs. Claims affecting **property** or the **estate** survive.
:::

:::recap Chapter 3 in five lines
- Death does not abate a suit if the right to sue survives (r. 1); personal actions die with the person.
- Survivors continue (r. 2); otherwise legal representatives are brought on record on application — **90 days** (Arts. 176–177) (rr. 3–4).
- **Pakistan (1972): no abatement** — court may proceed; judgment binds (rr. 3(2), 4(3)); no substitution needed for a non-contesting defendant (r. 4(4)).
- Legal representative may get an order / judgment set aside for sufficient cause — **60 days** (Art. 171; r. 9(2)–(3)).
- Marriage (r. 7), insolvency (r. 8), devolution pendente lite by leave (r. 10); Order applies to appeals (r. 11) but rr. 3, 4, 8 not to execution (r. 12).
:::

# Chapter 4 · Withdrawal, compromise, payment into court and security for costs (Orders XXIII to XXV)

## 4.1 Withdrawal of suits — O. XXIII rr. 1 and 2

> "1. (1) At any time after the institution of a suit the plaintiff may, as against all or any of the defendants, withdraw his suit or abandon part of his claim. (2) Where the Court is satisfied— (a) that a suit must fail by reason of some formal defect, or (b) that there are other sufficient grounds for allowing the plaintiff to institute a fresh suit for the subject-matter of a suit or part of a claim, it may, on such terms as it thinks fit, grant the plaintiff permission to withdraw from such suit or abandon such part of a claim with liberty to institute a fresh suit … (3) Where the plaintiff withdraws from a suit, or abandons part of a claim, without the permission referred to in sub-rule (2), he shall be liable for such costs as the Court may award and shall be precluded from instituting any fresh suit in respect of such subject-matter or such part of the claim. (4) Nothing in this rule shall be deemed to authorise the Court to permit one of several plaintiffs to withdraw without the consent of the others."

1. **Absolute withdrawal** (r. 1(1)) — a right of the plaintiff; no permission needed; but a **fresh suit is barred** (r. 1(3)) and costs may be imposed.
2. **Withdrawal with permission to file a fresh suit** (r. 1(2)) — only if (a) the suit must fail for a **formal defect** (e.g., misjoinder, wrong valuation, defective notice, non-joinder of a necessary party, wrong court), or (b) other **sufficient grounds** (read *ejusdem generis* — grounds analogous to a formal defect).
3. **r. 2:** in the fresh suit, the plaintiff is bound by **limitation** as if the first suit had not been filed — no exclusion of time spent (except as the Limitation Act itself allows).

:::eg Formal defect
Nasreen sues for possession of a shop but forgets to implead her co-owner sister, a necessary party, and the limitation is still open. She may apply to withdraw with permission to file a fresh, properly constituted suit (r. 1(2)(a)). If instead she simply withdraws without permission, a fresh suit on the same subject matter is barred (r. 1(3); s. 12(1)).
:::

## 4.2 Compromise — O. XXIII r. 3

> "3. Where it is proved to the satisfaction of the Court that a suit has been adjusted wholly or in part by any **lawful** agreement or compromise, or where the defendant satisfies the plaintiff in respect of the whole or any part of the subject-matter of the suit, the Court shall order such agreement, compromise or satisfaction to be recorded, and shall pass a decree in accordance therewith so far as it relates to the suit."

- The compromise must be **lawful** (not void under the Contract Act — e.g., not against public policy, not without free consent).
- The court **records** it and passes a **compromise (consent) decree** "so far as it relates to the suit".
- **Minors:** a compromise on behalf of a minor needs the **express leave of the court recorded** in the proceedings (O. XXXII r. 7); without it, it is **voidable** at the minor's instance.
- **Order XXIII does not apply to execution** proceedings (r. 4); compromises in execution are dealt with under O. XXI r. 2 (adjustment certified to the court).

### Remedies against a compromise decree

1. **No appeal** lies from a decree passed with the consent of the parties — in Islamabad expressly: "No appeal shall lie from a decree passed by the Court with consent of the parties" (**s. 96(3)**, as substituted by Act XIX of 2023).
2. A party alleging that **no compromise was made**, or that it was obtained by **fraud or misrepresentation**, must apply under **s. 12(2)** to the court that passed the decree — a separate suit is barred.
3. An order **recording or refusing to record** a compromise is appealable under **O. XLIII r. 1(m)**.

:::why Why are consent decrees protected?
A compromise ends litigation by agreement — the ideal outcome. Allowing a party to appeal from what he himself agreed to would defeat this. But consent obtained by fraud is no consent, so s. 12(2) remains available. The consent decree also operates as an **estoppel** between the parties.
:::

## 4.3 Payment into court — Order XXIV

In a suit for a debt or damages, the defendant may at any stage **deposit** what he considers full satisfaction (r. 1); notice is given to the plaintiff (r. 2); **no interest** runs on the deposited sum after notice (r. 3). If the plaintiff accepts it as part satisfaction and continues, and the court finds the deposit was full satisfaction, the plaintiff bears the later costs (r. 4(1)); if he accepts it in full, judgment follows and costs go against the party most to blame (r. 4(2)). The Code's illustrations show the rule: a plaintiff who sued without demand gets no costs; a defendant who disputed and later paid pays the costs.

## 4.4 Security for costs — Order XXV

Where a sole plaintiff (or all plaintiffs) **reside out of Pakistan** and possess no sufficient immovable property in Pakistan other than the property in suit, the court may, of its own motion or on a defendant's application, order the plaintiff to **give security for all costs** incurred and likely to be incurred by the defendant (r. 1(1)). A person who leaves Pakistan in circumstances suggesting he will not be available to pay costs is deemed to reside out of Pakistan (r. 1(2)). In a **money suit by a woman** plaintiff who has no sufficient immovable property in Pakistan, a like order may be made (r. 1(3)). If security is not furnished in time, the court **shall dismiss** the suit (r. 2(1)); the plaintiff may have the dismissal set aside on **sufficient cause**, after notice to the defendant (r. 2(2)–(3)).

:::recap Chapter 4 in five lines
- Withdrawal without permission → costs and **no fresh suit** (O. XXIII r. 1(3)); with permission only for **formal defect** or sufficient grounds (r. 1(2)); limitation not saved (r. 2); one plaintiff cannot withdraw for all (r. 1(4)).
- Compromise must be **lawful**; court records it and passes decree (r. 3); minor's compromise needs recorded leave (O. XXXII r. 7).
- **No appeal from a consent decree** (s. 96(3), Islamabad); challenge for fraud via **s. 12(2)**; order recording / refusing compromise appealable (O. XLIII r. 1(m)).
- Payment into court stops interest and shifts costs (O. XXIV).
- Security for costs from plaintiffs residing out of Pakistan (and women plaintiffs in money suits without immovable property); failure → dismissal, restorable on sufficient cause (O. XXV).
:::

# Chapter 5 · Commissions (ss. 75 to 78, Order XXVI)

## 5.1 Purposes of a commission — s. 75

> "75. Subject to such conditions and limitations as may be prescribed, the Court may issue a commission— (a) to examine any person; (b) to make a local investigation; (c) to examine or adjust accounts; or (d) to make a partition."

{w:26,74}
|Commission|When and how (Order XXVI)|
|**To examine witnesses** (rr. 1–8)|Persons **exempt** from attendance (e.g., women not appearing in public, high officials — ss. 132–133), persons **sick or infirm**, persons residing **beyond the local limits** or about to leave, Government servants who cannot attend without detriment to public service (r. 4). Commissions abroad (r. 5). Depositions are read in evidence only if the witness is still unable to attend, or with the consent of the party against whom they are used (r. 8).|
|**Local investigation** (rr. 9–10)|To **elucidate** a matter in dispute, ascertain market value, mesne profits, damages, boundaries (e.g., whether a wall encroaches on the plaintiff's plot). The Commissioner inspects, records evidence, and reports in writing; the **report and evidence are evidence in the suit** and the Commissioner may be examined in court (r. 10(2)). The court may order a further inquiry if dissatisfied (r. 10(3)).|
|**Accounts** (rr. 11–12)|Examination or adjustment of accounts; the Commissioner's proceedings and report are evidence, but the court may direct further inquiry.|
|**Partition** (rr. 13–14)|After a **preliminary decree** for partition, the Commissioner divides the property by metes and bounds and allots shares; the court then passes the **final decree**.|

- **r. 15:** the party applying deposits the **expenses** before the commission issues.
- **r. 16:** Commissioners may examine parties and witnesses, call for documents, and enter land or buildings.
- **r. 18:** parties must appear before the Commissioner; failing which, he may proceed ex parte.
- **ss. 76–78:** commissions to courts in other provinces and to foreign courts; execution of commissions received from foreign courts.

:::trap A local commission is not to collect evidence for a party
A local commission is issued to **elucidate** a matter that the court cannot properly decide without a local inspection — not to collect evidence that a party should have produced himself, or to decide the case. The Commissioner's report is **evidence**, open to objection and cross-examination; it is not a judgment.
:::

:::recap Chapter 5 in three lines
- s. 75: commissions to examine persons, make local investigations, examine accounts, make partition.
- Witness commissions for exempt, sick, distant or official witnesses; depositions read only if the witness still cannot attend (O. XXVI r. 8).
- Local investigation / accounts / partition reports are evidence; Commissioner may be examined; expenses deposited first.
:::
