
# Chapter 7 · Maintenance (nafaqa)

## 7.1 Meaning and basis

**Maintenance (nafaqa)** includes **food, clothing and lodging** (and, in modern understanding, medical care and other necessities suited to the parties' status). Quran: "The father of the child shall bear the cost of the mother's food and clothing on a reasonable basis" (2:233); "Lodge them where you dwell, according to your means" (65:6); "Let the man of means spend according to his means" (65:7).

**Who is entitled:** (1) the **wife**; (2) **children**; (3) **parents and grandparents** in need; (4) other **relations within prohibited degrees** who are poor (to a limited extent).

## 7.2 Maintenance of the wife

**Conditions:**

1. A **valid** marriage (no maintenance in an irregular marriage);
2. the wife has attained an age at which she can render conjugal society (or the husband has taken her to his house);
3. she is **not disobedient** (*nashiza*) — she has not, without lawful excuse, refused to live with him or to obey his reasonable directions.

**When refusal is justified** (and maintenance continues): non-payment of **prompt dower**; **cruelty**; the husband's taking a **second wife** without permission or failing to treat her equitably; unsafe or unsuitable residence; illness; a lawful condition in the nikahnama (e.g., the right to live in her parents' city).

**Amount:** the classical Hanafi view (Khassaf) considers the **status of both** spouses; Pakistani courts fix maintenance with reference to the **husband's means** and the **wife's reasonable needs**, increasing it periodically. A wife is entitled to maintenance **even if she has means of her own**.

**Past maintenance:** classical Hanafi law allowed arrears only if fixed by **agreement or decree**; Shafi'i and Shia law allow arrears. Pakistani **Family Courts** routinely award maintenance from the date the husband **ceased** to maintain the wife without lawful excuse, within the period of limitation.

**During iddat:** a divorced wife is entitled to maintenance during **iddat** (Hanafi); a **widow** is not.

## 7.3 Maintenance of children

1. The **father** is bound to maintain his **sons until they attain puberty** (or become able to earn) and his **daughters until they are married**, as well as **disabled** children of any age.
2. The obligation continues **even if the children live with the mother** (in her hizanat) — the mother's custody does not relieve the father.
3. If the father is poor and unable to earn, the obligation passes to the **mother** (if she has means), and then to the **paternal grandfather**.
4. A **khula** or settlement between the spouses **cannot waive** the children's right to maintenance; the right belongs to the child.
5. Children's maintenance suits are brought in the **Family Court** by the child through the mother or other next friend.

## 7.4 Remedies for maintenance

1. **Suit in the Family Court** (Family Courts Act, Schedule).
2. **Interim maintenance — s. 17A, Family Courts Act:** at any stage of a suit for maintenance, the Family Court shall pass an order for **interim monthly maintenance**, payable by the **14th of each month**; if the defendant fails to pay, the Court may **strike off his defence** and decree the suit.
3. **Arbitration Council — MFLO s. 9:** "If any husband fails to maintain his wife adequately, or where there are more wives than one, fails to maintain them equitably, the wife, or all or any of the wives, may in addition to seeking any other legal remedy available apply to the **Chairman** who shall constitute an **Arbitration Council** to determine the matter, and the Arbitration Council may issue a **certificate** specifying the amount which shall be paid as maintenance by the husband" (s. 9(1)). Revision lies to the **Collector**, whose decision is final (s. 9(2)); arrears are recoverable as **arrears of land revenue** (s. 9(3)).
4. **DMMA s. 2(ii)** — dissolution for **two years'** failure to maintain.
5. **Execution** of the Family Court's decree as a decree for money (s. 13, Family Courts Act), including arrest and, where the decree provides, attachment of salary.

:::recap Chapter 7 in five lines
- Maintenance = food, clothing, lodging (and necessities) — Quran 2:233, 65:6–7.
- Wife: valid marriage + not disobedient; justified refusal does not end the right; even if she has means; arrears from cessation; iddat maintenance (not widow).
- Children: sons till puberty / earning, daughters till marriage, disabled always; father liable even when mother has custody; cannot be waived by khula.
- Remedies: Family Court suit; interim maintenance by the 14th, defence struck off (s. 17A FCA); MFLO s. 9 Arbitration Council certificate, land revenue; DMMA s. 2(ii).
- Execution under s. 13 FCA.

# Chapter 8 · Parentage, legitimacy and acknowledgment

## 8.1 Maternity and paternity

- **Maternity** is established by **birth** — a child is the child of the woman who gave birth to it, whether the birth was legitimate or not (Hanafi: an illegitimate child inherits from the mother).
- **Paternity** is established only by **marriage** — a child is the legitimate child of a man only if it is born of a valid (or irregular) marriage, or of a relationship recognised by acknowledgment.

:::maxim Al-walad lil-firash — the child belongs to the marriage bed
A child born to a married woman is presumed to be the child of her husband (Hadith, Bukhari). The presumption is rebutted only by **lian** — the husband's sworn denial and the procedure of mutual imprecation.
:::

## 8.2 Presumptions of legitimacy

**Muslim law:**
1. A child born **at least six months** after the marriage is legitimate (the minimum period of gestation — Quran 46:15 and 2:233 read together: 30 months minus 24 months of suckling).
2. A child born **within two years** (Hanafi) — **ten months** (Shia), **four years** (Maliki, Shafi'i) — after the dissolution of marriage is legitimate, unless disclaimed by the husband.

**Qanun-e-Shahadat 1984, Art. 128:** the birth of a person during the continuance of a valid marriage between his mother and any man, **not earlier than six lunar months from the date of the marriage**, or **within two years after its dissolution**, the mother remaining unmarried, is **conclusive proof** that he is the legitimate child of that man, **unless the man disowns the child** (lian). Art. 128 thus adopts the Hanafi periods as rules of evidence.

## 8.3 Acknowledgment of paternity (iqrar)

Where the **paternity** of a child is **not known** (neither proved nor disproved), a man may establish it by **acknowledging** the child as his **legitimate** child.

**Conditions of a valid acknowledgment:**

1. The acknowledger must be **adult and sane**.
2. The **ages** must admit the relationship (the acknowledger at least about 12½ years older).
3. The child's **paternity must not be known** to be another's.
4. The child must **not be the offspring of zina** (adultery or fornication), and the mother must be a woman whom the acknowledger **could lawfully have married** at the time of conception.
5. The acknowledgment must be of the child as a **legitimate son or daughter**, not merely as a son.
6. An **adult** acknowledged child must **not repudiate** it.

:::case Habibur Rahman Chowdhury v. Altaf Ali Chowdhury ((1922) 48 IA 114, Privy Council)
__Held.__ The doctrine of acknowledgment applies only where the fact of a **lawful marriage** is **uncertain**; it raises a **presumption** of a lawful marriage between the parents. It **cannot legitimate** a child **proved** to be illegitimate — e.g., born of a woman whom the father could not have married, or conceived before any marriage. "A son to be legitimate must be the offspring of a man and his wife … ; any other offspring is the offspring of *zina*."
:::

*Sadiq Hussain v. Hashim Ali* ((1916) 43 IA 212, PC): an acknowledgment once made is **irrevocable**, and the acknowledged child inherits.

## 8.4 Adoption

Muslim law **does not recognise adoption** as creating the legal relationship of parent and child (Quran 33:4–5: "Call them by the names of their fathers"). An adopted child does not inherit from the adoptive parents, and the prohibited degrees of marriage do not arise from adoption. The adoptive parent may provide for the child by **gift** (*hiba*) or a **will** (up to one-third). The Islamic institution of **kafala** (fostering and care) is encouraged.

:::recap Chapter 8 in four lines
- Maternity by birth; paternity by marriage; *al-walad lil-firash*.
- Legitimate if born ≥ 6 months after marriage or within 2 years of dissolution (Hanafi) — Art. 128 QSO conclusive unless disowned (lian).
- Acknowledgment (iqrar): paternity unknown, ages possible, not zina, lawful marriage possible, as legitimate child, not repudiated; irrevocable (*Habibur Rahman*; *Sadiq Hussain*).
- No adoption in Muslim law (Quran 33:4–5); provide by gift or will; kafala.
:::

# Chapter 9 · Guardianship (wilayat) and custody (hizanat)

## 9.1 Three kinds of guardianship

![Guardianship and custody — the structure](guardianship_map.png)

1. **Guardianship in marriage (wilayat-e-nikah)** — to contract the marriage of a minor (Chapter 2).
2. **Guardianship of the person (wilayat-e-nafs)** — the right and duty to control and educate the minor; under Muslim law it belongs primarily to the **father**.
3. **Guardianship of property (wilayat-e-mal)** — the right to manage the minor's property.

Distinct from these is **custody (hizanat)** — the physical **care and upbringing** of the young child, which Muslim law gives primarily to the **mother** and female relations.

{w:22,39,39}
|Point|Guardianship (wilayat)|Custody (hizanat)|
|Nature|Legal authority over the minor's person and property|Physical care and upbringing of a young child|
|Primarily belongs to|The **father**, then his executor, the paternal grandfather and his executor|The **mother**, then other female relations (in a fixed order)|
|Duration|Until **majority** (18; 21 if a court guardian is appointed — Majority Act s. 3)|Hanafi: boy till **7**, girl till **puberty** (subject to welfare)|
|Can be lost|By removal under GWA s. 39|By the hazina's disqualification (9.3)|
|Effect|Father remains guardian even when mother has custody — decides education, religion, residence (subject to court)|Does not give control of property or the right to change the child's religion|

:::maxim The welfare of the minor is the paramount consideration
In every custody and guardianship dispute, the court is guided by **what appears to be for the welfare of the minor**, consistently with the law to which the minor is subject (GWA s. 17(1)). The rules of hizanat are **guides** to the minor's welfare, not rigid rights of parents.
:::

## 9.2 Custody (hizanat) — the right of the mother

**Hanafi periods:** the mother is entitled to custody of her **son until he completes seven years** and of her **daughter until she attains puberty**. (Shia: son till 2 years, daughter till 7; Maliki: son till puberty, daughter till marriage.)

**Order of female hazinas (Hanafi)** if the mother is absent or disqualified: (1) mother's mother, how high soever; (2) father's mother, how high soever; (3) full sister; (4) uterine sister; (5) consanguine sister; (6) full sister's daughter; (7) uterine sister's daughter; (8) consanguine sister's daughter; (9) maternal aunts; (10) paternal aunts. After the females, custody goes to the **father** and other male agnates.

**After the period of hizanat** custody goes to the **father** (or other guardian), **subject to the welfare** of the minor.

## 9.3 Disqualifications of a hazina

The mother (or other female) loses her right of hizanat if she:

1. **Marries a person not related to the child within the prohibited degrees** (a "stranger" to the child) — her right **revives** on the dissolution of that marriage (Hanafi);
2. leads an **immoral** life (e.g., is a prostitute);
3. **neglects** the child or is cruel to it;
4. **changes her residence** to a distant place so that the father cannot exercise supervision;
5. is **of unsound mind** or suffers from a contagious disease rendering her unfit;
6. **apostatises** (where this affects the child's welfare), or is **non-Muslim** — in Pakistani practice, only if it endangers the child's upbringing in his faith;
7. **demands remuneration** where another female relation will look after the child for free (classical);
8. lives in a house where the child may be ill-treated.

**Welfare overrides.** Pakistani courts consistently hold that these disqualifications are **not absolute**: even a mother who has remarried may keep custody if the child's welfare so requires (e.g., the father is unfit, or a change of custody would harm the child); and the father may get custody before the end of hizanat if the mother is unfit.

:::case Mst. Zohra Begum v. Sh. Latif Ahmad Munawwar (PLD 1965 Lahore 695, Full Bench)
__Held.__ The rules of Muslim law on hizanat are founded on the **welfare of the minor**; the court's paramount duty under the Guardians and Wards Act is to see what is for the minor's welfare. The mother's right to custody is **not an absolute right** that she can claim regardless of the child's welfare, and her remarriage does not automatically defeat her custody where the welfare of the child requires that the child remain with her.

__Ratio.__ Personal-law rules of custody are presumptions of what is good for the child; they **yield** to the minor's welfare in the facts of each case.
:::

## 9.4 Guardianship of the property under Muslim law

**Legal guardians of property (in order):** (1) the **father**; (2) the **executor** appointed by the father's will; (3) the **paternal grandfather**; (4) the executor appointed by the grandfather's will; (5) the **court**. The **mother**, brother and uncle are **not** legal guardians of property, though they may be appointed by the court or act as **de facto guardians**.

**Powers of a legal guardian (classical):** to sell or mortgage **movables** for the minor's necessities; to sell **immovables** only in specified cases — e.g., double the value is offered, the minor has no other means of support, there are debts of the deceased to be paid, the property is falling into ruin, or the expenses exceed the income.

**De facto guardian** (a person who, without legal authority, takes charge of the minor's property): has **no power to alienate** the minor's immovable property; such a transfer is **void**.

:::case Imambandi v. Mutsaddi ((1918) 45 IA 73, Privy Council)
__Held.__ A Muslim **mother**, who is not a legal guardian of property, has no power, as a de facto guardian, to transfer her minor children's interest in immovable property; such a transfer is **void** (a nullity), not merely voidable, and cannot be ratified by the minor on attaining majority.
:::

## 9.5 The Guardians and Wards Act 1890

The Act supplements personal law: the court applies the minor's personal law but always for the minor's **welfare**. In the ICT, the **Family Court** is deemed the **District Court** for the purposes of this Act (s. 25, Family Courts Act).

### (1) Appointment or declaration — ss. 7 to 19

1. **Power — s. 7:** where the Court is satisfied that it is **for the welfare of a minor**, it may make an order **appointing** a guardian of his person or property or both, or **declaring** a person to be such a guardian. An order impliedly removes any guardian not appointed by will or court.
2. **Who may apply — s. 8:** (a) the person desiring to be or claiming to be the guardian; (b) any **relative or friend** of the minor; (c) the **Collector** of the district where the minor ordinarily resides or has property; (d) the Collector having authority over the minor's class.
3. **Which court — s. 9:** for the **person** — the District Court where the minor **ordinarily resides**; for **property** — that court, or a District Court where he has **property** (which may return the application to a more convenient court).
4. **Form — s. 10:** a petition, signed and verified like a plaint, stating the minor's name, sex, religion, date of birth, residence, the proposed guardian, the minor's property, etc.
5. **Interim orders — s. 12:** the Court may direct the person having custody to **produce** the minor and make orders for **temporary custody and protection** of the person or property of the minor (a female minor to be produced in accordance with custom; no temporary custody to a "husband" of a female minor unless she is already with him with her parents' consent).
6. **Welfare and factors — s. 17:** "In appointing or declaring the guardian of a minor, the Court shall … be guided by what, consistently with the law to which the minor is subject, appears in the circumstances to be for the **welfare** of the minor" (s. 17(1)). The Court shall have regard to the **age, sex and religion** of the minor, the **character and capacity** of the proposed guardian and his **nearness of kin**, the **wishes of a deceased parent**, and any existing or previous **relations** of the proposed guardian with the minor or his property (s. 17(2)). If the minor is **old enough to form an intelligent preference**, the Court **may consider** it (s. 17(3)). No one shall be appointed against his will (s. 17(5)).
7. **Where no guardian of the person may be appointed — s. 19:** (a) a **married female** minor whose husband is not unfit; (b) a minor whose **father is living** and is not, in the Court's opinion, **unfit** to be guardian of the person; (c) a minor whose property is under a **Court of Wards**. (The father may still lose **custody** where welfare so requires — custody and guardianship being different.)

### (2) Duties, rights and liabilities — ss. 20 to 37

1. **Fiduciary relation — s. 20:** the guardian stands in a **fiduciary relation** to the ward and must make **no profit** from his office; the relation affects purchases between them immediately or soon after the ward attains majority.
2. **Minor as guardian — s. 21:** a minor is incompetent to act as guardian except of his own wife or child.
3. **Guardian of the person — s. 24:** "charged with the **custody** of the ward and must look to his **support, health and education** and such other matters as the law to which the ward is subject requires".
4. **Return of the ward — s. 25:** if a ward **leaves or is removed** from the custody of a guardian of his person, the Court, if it thinks it is **for the welfare of the ward** to return, may order his return and enforce it by **arrest** (using a Magistrate's powers under s. 100 CrPC). The father's or mother's custody suits are commonly brought under s. 25. Residence of the ward elsewhere against the guardian's will does not itself end the guardianship (s. 25(3)).
5. **Removal from jurisdiction — s. 26:** a court-appointed guardian of the person may not remove the ward from the Court's jurisdiction **without leave**.
6. **Guardian of property — s. 27:** must deal with the property "as carefully as a **man of ordinary prudence** would deal with it if it were his own", and may do all acts reasonable and proper for its **realisation, protection or benefit**.
7. **Testamentary guardian — s. 28:** his power to deal with immovable property is subject to restrictions in the will, unless the Court permits.
8. **Limits on a court-appointed guardian of property — s. 29:** he shall **not, without the previous permission of the Court**, (a) **mortgage, charge, sell, gift, exchange or otherwise transfer** any part of the ward's **immovable property**, or (b) **lease** it for more than **five years** or for any term extending more than **one year beyond** the ward's majority.
9. **Voidable — s. 30:** a disposal in contravention of s. 28 or 29 is **voidable** at the instance of any person affected.
10. **Permission — s. 31:** shall not be granted "except in case of **necessity** or for an **evident advantage** to the ward"; the order must recite the necessity or advantage and describe the property, and the Court may require the sale by auction, etc.
11. **Accounts and liability — ss. 34–37:** a court-appointed guardian of property may be required to give a **bond**, exhibit an **inventory** and render **accounts**; he is liable as a **trustee** (s. 37).

### (3) Termination — ss. 38 to 42

**Removal — s. 39.** The Court may, on the application of any person interested or of its own motion, remove a guardian appointed or declared by the Court, or appointed by will, for:

(a) **abuse of trust**; (b) **continued failure** to perform the duties of the trust; (c) **incapacity** to perform them; (d) **ill-treatment or neglect** of the ward; (e) **contumacious disregard** of the Act or the Court's orders; (f) **conviction** of an offence implying a defect of character unfitting him; (g) an **interest adverse** to the faithful performance of his duties; (h) **ceasing to reside** within the Court's jurisdiction; (i) for a guardian of property, **bankruptcy or insolvency**; (j) his guardianship **ceasing, or being liable to cease, under the minor's personal law**. A testamentary guardian is not removed under (g) or (h) except in the limited cases in the proviso.

**Discharge — s. 40** (on resignation); **cessation — s. 41:** a guardian of the **person** ceases by his death, removal or discharge; the Court of Wards assuming superintendence; the ward **attaining majority**; a **female ward's marriage** to a husband not unfit; or the father ceasing to be unfit. A guardian of **property** ceases by his death, removal or discharge; the Court of Wards; or the ward's **majority**. On cessation the Court may require delivery of property and **accounts** (s. 41(3)–(4)).

**Successor — s. 42; joint guardians — s. 38** (survivorship).

### (4) Appeals — s. 47

An appeal lies (to the High Court, or to the District Court where the order is passed by a subordinate officer) against orders **appointing or refusing** to appoint a guardian (s. 7), **returning** an application (s. 9(3)), making or refusing an order for **return** of the ward (s. 25), refusing **leave** to remove the ward (s. 26), refusing **permission** under ss. 28–29, **removing** a guardian (s. 39), refusing **discharge** (s. 40), and others. Where the Family Court decides a guardianship matter, the appeal provisions of the **Family Courts Act (s. 14)** govern.

## 9.6 Visitation (meeting schedule) and modern practice

When custody is given to one parent, the Court fixes a **schedule of meetings** for the other (weekly visits, school holidays, Eid days, video calls), as the child's welfare requires a relationship with **both** parents. Courts consider the child's **preference** (s. 17(3)) where mature, keep **siblings together**, and avoid frequent changes of custody. **Mediation** and **reconciliation** are encouraged at every stage (Family Courts Act ss. 10, 12) — the theme of the ASJ 2025 question.

:::eg A custody dispute in Islamabad
Amna and Bilal divorced; their son Ali is 9 and daughter Hira is 5. Amna has remarried a man who is not related to the children. Bilal applies under s. 25 GWA for custody of both.
1. **Ali (9)** — the mother's hizanat for a boy ended at 7; prima facie the father is entitled, but the Court asks what is best for Ali's **welfare** (his schooling, attachment, the father's circumstances) and may consider his **preference** (s. 17(3)).
2. **Hira (5)** — within the mother's period of hizanat, but the mother's marriage to a stranger is a **disqualification**; the right passes to the **maternal grandmother** if she is fit and willing. Yet if Hira's welfare requires her to stay with her mother (e.g., the stepfather is kind, the father is unable to care for her), the Court may leave her with the mother (*Zohra Begum*).
3. Whoever gets custody, the Court will fix a **visitation schedule** for the other parent; the father remains liable for **maintenance** of both children (Chapter 7) and remains their **natural guardian** (s. 19(b)).
:::
